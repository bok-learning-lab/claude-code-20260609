### Core claims
- **Two arms of immunity:**
  - **Innate (nonspecific) immunity** — inherited; epithelial barriers (skin 2 m²; intestine 200 m²; gastric pH 1–2), antimicrobial peptides, phagocytic cells (neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells; Kupffer cells in liver, microglia in CNS, alveolar macrophages, Langerhans cells in skin), interferons, complement, fever, NK cells, mast cells.
  - **Adaptive (specific) immunity** — generated postnatally by lymphocyte gene recombination; targets specific antigens; immunological memory.
- **PAMPs and DAMPs.** Innate cells recognize **pathogen-associated molecular patterns** (lipopolysaccharide LPS on Gram-negatives; peptidoglycan on Gram-positives) via **toll-like receptors (TLRs)** — 10 in humans (2011 Nobel) — and **NOD-like receptors** that form **inflammasomes** activating caspase. **Danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)** are released by necrotic (not apoptotic) cells and provoke sterile inflammation.
- **Phagocytosis** (Fig 15.2): pseudopods engulf the target → vacuole → fuses with lysosome → digestion. If pseudopods fail to seal first, lysosomal enzymes spill out and damage surrounding tissue, contributing to inflammation. Apoptotic body cells display **phosphatidylserine** ("eat me") and are engulfed without provoking inflammation.
- **Extravasation / diapedesis (Fig 15.1):** circulating leukocytes roll → are captured → adhere/activate → spread → squeeze through endothelial junctions at postcapillary venules into infected tissue. Drawn by **chemokines** (a cytokine subclass). Neutrophils arrive first (~2–5 min to cross endothelium, +5–15 min through basement membrane); monocytes/macrophages follow; lymphocytes last.
- **Fever.** The hypothalamic preoptic area is reset upward by **PGE₂**. **Exogenous pyrogens** include LPS; **endogenous pyrogens** include IL-1, IL-6, TNF (released by leukocytes/macrophages). Mild fever increases neutrophil activity, interferon production, and lowers plasma iron (limiting bacterial growth).
- **Interferons.** α and β interferon are produced by virally infected cells (and via the **STING** pathway in response to cytoplasmic viral/bacterial nucleic acids) — they protect surrounding cells from viral replication. **γ interferon** activates phagocytes and is secreted by NK cells, then by helper and cytotoxic T cells. FDA-approved interferons treat hepatitis B/C, hairy-cell leukemia, multiple sclerosis (β), chronic granulomatous disease (γ).
- **Antigens** are usually large (MW > 10,000) foreign molecules with multiple **antigenic determinant sites (epitopes)**. **Haptens** are small molecules nonantigenic alone but antigenic when bound to a carrier protein (Karl Landsteiner; basis of penicillin allergy).
- **B vs. T lymphocytes** (Table 15.4):
  - **B cells** — bone-marrow processed; secrete antibodies; provide **humoral immunity**; 10–15% of blood lymphocytes; transform into plasma cells and memory cells.
  - **T cells** — thymus processed; provide **cell-mediated immunity**; 75–80% of blood lymphocytes; long-lived (6–9 years).
- **Primary lymphoid organs:** bone marrow, thymus. **Secondary lymphoid organs:** ~450 lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, Peyer's patches. Lymphocytes circulate continuously between lymphoid organs to maximize antigen-encounter probability.
- **Thymus** grows through childhood, regresses after puberty; partially repopulates T cells after chemotherapy or HIV-induced depletion but adult T-cell repopulation occurs mostly in secondary lymphoid organs.
- **Local inflammation (Table 15.5; Fig 15.5):** bacteria penetrate skin → resident macrophages/mast cells release cytokines + chemokines → complement activation → mast cells release **histamine, heparin, serotonin, proteases, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, TNF** → vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, neutrophil/monocyte extravasation → bacteria phagocytosed, NETs (neutrophil extracellular traps) form, pus accumulates. Macroscopic signs (Celsus, ~40 AD): *rubor, calor, dolor, tumor* (redness, heat, pain, swelling). Aspirin/NSAIDs reduce pain by inhibiting COX-1 and COX-2 (less PGE₂).

#### Figure 15.10 — The fixation of complement proteins *(viewed)*

A three-panel sequence depicting the classic pathway of complement activation. **Panel 1:** A bacterial cell membrane (red lipid bilayer with embedded antigens) is shown along the bottom; an antibody (Y-shaped, green) has bound its two Fab arms to surface antigens — the antibody-antigen complex is highlighted in a circled "1". **Panel 2:** Complement protein **C4** (a pink three-lobed structure) docks onto the bound antibody's Fc stem ("2"). **Panel 3:** C4 is cleaved into two subunits; **C4a** is released into the surrounding fluid as a soluble fragment (small pink lobe, labeled "involved in chemotaxis"), while **C4b** remains attached ("fixed") to the bacterial membrane just adjacent to the antibody. The C4b fragment then becomes an active enzyme that catalyzes the further activation cascade (C2 → C3 → C5 through C9), culminating in the membrane attack complex (Fig 15.11). The figure shows the conceptual core of complement: opsonization, chemotaxis (C3a, C5a), and direct membrane attack all originate from this stepwise cleavage of C4 and C3 by antibody-triggered activation.

> Vision note: This image is the anchor for any complement-cascade question — recognition (C1), activation (C4, C2, C3), attack (C5–C9). Critical for SLE/glomerulonephritis (immune-complex disease), atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, and any biologic targeting complement (eculizumab).

#### Figure 15.13 — Migration of antigen-presenting dendritic cells to secondary lymphoid organs activates T cells *(viewed)*

A schematic loop tracing the antigen-presenting cell (APC) journey. **Step 1 (top):** A dendritic cell with extended cellular processes is shown at an infected epithelial site (skin/mucosa) processing **antigens** (red molecules) released by bacteria. **Step 2 (lower left):** The dendritic cell, now carrying processed antigen, has migrated through a **lymph vessel** into a **lymph node** (depicted centrally as a kidney-shaped organ with cortical follicles). ## Section 15.2 — Functions of B Lymphocytes (pp. 502–506)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Antibodies** (pp. 503–505)
  - **Antibody Structure** (p. 503)
  - **Diversity of Antibodies** (pp. 504–505)
- **The Complement System** (pp. 505–506)

### Core claims
- B-lymphocyte clonal expansion: antigen binds surface antibody → B cell divides in a germinal center → progeny differentiate into **plasma cells** (~2,000 antibody molecules/sec) and **memory B cells** (visually indistinguishable from progenitor; persist for years).
- **Antibody structure (Fig 15.8b):** Y-shaped, four polypeptide chains — two heavy (H, ~50 kDa) and two light (L, ~25 kDa). Each chain has a **constant (C)** stem region and a **variable (V)** tip region; variable regions of one H + one L pair form the antigen-binding **Fab (fragment, antigen-binding)** at each arm tip; the stem is **Fc (fragment, crystallizable)**.
- **Five immunoglobulin classes:** IgG (most abundant in plasma; secondary response), IgA (mucosal secretions, saliva, milk), IgM (initial primary response; pentameric surface receptor), IgD (B-cell surface receptor; unclear function), IgE (allergy and helminth defense; binds mast-cell Fc receptors).
- **Antibody diversity** is generated by two mechanisms (~10²⁰ antibody molecules; millions of distinct specificities):
  - **Antigen-independent VDJ recombination** in bone marrow during B-cell development — random rearrangement of V, D, J gene segments of heavy and light chains.
  - **Antigen-dependent somatic hypermutation and class switching** in secondary lymphoid germinal centers — point mutations in V regions during clonal expansion, with selection for higher-affinity variants; constant region switch from IgM → IgG, IgA, or IgE.
- **Complement system** = ~30 plasma proteins, of which C1–C9 form the activation cascade. Three function blocks: **recognition** (C1) → **activation** (C4 → C2 → C3) → **attack** (C5b–C9 form the **membrane attack complex (MAC)**, an osmotic pore).
- **Two activation pathways:**
  - **Classic pathway** — antibody-triggered (IgG or IgM binding antigen activates C1; faster, more efficient).
  - **Alternative pathway** — direct activation by bacterial polysaccharide surfaces (no antibody required).
- **Complement effector functions** (besides MAC formation):
  - **Opsonization** — C3b coats pathogens; phagocytes recognize bound C3b via complement receptors.
  - **Chemotaxis** — soluble C3a and C5a fragments attract neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils.
  - **Mast-cell degranulation** — C3a and C5a stimulate histamine release.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Plasma cells are protein factories that produce about 2,000 antibody proteins per second." (p. 502)
- > "Antibody proteins are also known as immunoglobulins (Ig). There are five Ig subclasses: IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, and IgE." (p. 503)
- > "It is estimated that there are about 100 million trillion (10²⁰) antibody molecules in each individual, representing a few million different specificities for different antigens." (p. 504)
- > "The complement proteins are designated C1 through C9. These proteins are present in an inactive state within plasma and other body fluids and become activated by the attachment of antibodies to antigens." (p. 505)
- > "The ability of antibodies to stimulate phagocytosis is termed opsonization." (p. 505)
- > "The attack complex is a large pore that can kill the bacterial cell through the osmotic influx of water." (p. 506)

## Section 15.3 — Functions of T Lymphocytes (pp. 506–513)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Cytotoxic, Helper, and Regulatory T Lymphocytes** (pp. 506–510)
  - **Lymphokines** (pp. 507–508)
  - **Histocompatibility Antigens** (p. 510)
- **Interactions Between Antigen-Presenting Cells and T Lymphocytes** (pp. 510–513)
  - **T Lymphocyte Response to a Virus** (pp. 511–513)

### Core claims
- **Three T-cell subpopulations**:
  - **Cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺)** — kill virus-infected cells, fungal-infected cells, transplanted cells, and tumor cells by direct contact: release **perforins** (form a transmembrane pore, like MAC) + **granzymes** (activate caspases → apoptosis of target). Recognize antigen on **class I MHC** of target.
  - **Helper T cells (CD4⁺)** — amplify both humoral and cell-mediated responses by secreting cytokines (lymphokines); recognize antigen on **class II MHC** of antigen-presenting cells. Subdivided into TH1 (IFN-γ → macrophage activation), TH2 (IL-4/5/13 → B-cell IgE class switch, eosinophil recruitment, allergic asthma), and TH17 (IL-17 → neutrophil-rich responses, mucosal defense).
  - **Regulatory T cells (Treg, formerly suppressor)** — express FOXP3; secrete TGF-β; brake immune responses; prevent autoimmunity. FOXP3 mutations cause severe autoimmune disease.
- **HIV / AIDS (Clinical Application):** HIV is a retrovirus that preferentially infects helper T cells (especially GI-mucosal CD4⁺ cells where up to 30% reside). **Antiretroviral therapy (ART)** uses reverse-transcriptase inhibitors + protease inhibitor "cocktails" to indefinitely suppress viral replication; integration of viral DNA into memory T-cell host genome prevents cure. Early continual treatment reduces transmission risk by 96%.
- **Lymphokines / cytokines (Table 15.7):** ~30+ identified. IL-1 (macrophage; T-cell activation, fever); IL-2 (helper T cell; T-cell proliferation); IL-3 (stem cell + mast cell); IL-4 (B-cell IgE production; cytotoxic T cell); IL-5 (eosinophil); IL-6 (T and B cell); GM-CSF (granulocyte-monocyte differentiation).
- **MHC (HLA) molecules:** **Class I MHC** on essentially all nucleated cells; present endogenous (cytosolic) peptides — surveyed by CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells. **Class II MHC** restricted to APCs (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells); present exogenous (phagocytosed) peptides — surveyed by CD4⁺ helper T cells. Each MHC molecule binds many different peptides nonspecifically; T-cell receptor (TCR) recognizes the MHC-peptide complex.
- **Antigen presentation (Fig 15.13):** dendritic cell at site of infection → engulfs antigen → migrates via lymphatic vessels to the lymph node → presents to T cells in association with class II MHC → activated helper T cells secrete IL-2 → proliferate, then exit lymph node via efferent lymph → home to the infection site via chemokines.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Cytotoxic T lymphocytes kill their victim cells by cell-mediated destruction. This means that they must be in actual physical contact with the victim cells. When this occurs, the cytotoxic T cells secrete molecules called perforins and enzymes called granzymes." (p. 506)
- > "Helper T lymphocytes aid the specific immune response of B lymphocytes and cytotoxic T lymphocytes through secretion of chemical regulators called lymphokines." (p. 507)
- > "Regulatory T lymphocytes have been distinguished by the activation of a gene known as FOXP3, which codes for a transcription factor that appears to be required for the development and maintenance of regulatory T lymphocytes." (p. 507)
- > "HIV is classified as a retrovirus because its genetic code is carried in its RNA. The enzyme reverse transcriptase is needed to transcribe this viral RNA into complementary DNA for viral replication." (p. 508)
- > "When the foreign antigens are bound to class-2 MHC molecules, they are recognized by helper T lymphocytes." (p. 510)

## Section 15.4 — Active and Passive Immunity (pp. 513–518)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Active Immunity and the Clonal Selection Theory** (pp. 514–516)
  - **Active Immunity** (pp. 515–516)
- **Immunological Tolerance** (pp. 516–517)
- **Passive Immunity** (pp. 517–518)

### Core claims
- **Primary vs. secondary response:**
  - **Primary** — first antigen exposure; lag of ~5–14 days; IgM peaks first, then IgG; antibody titer moderate; can leave the person symptomatic.
  - **Secondary** — re-exposure to same antigen; fast onset (1–2 days), high-titer IgG dominates; little to no symptoms; basis of vaccine-induced protection.
- **Clonal selection theory (Burnet, Talmage, Lederberg):** B lymphocytes inherit the capacity to bind only one antigen specificity (via random VDJ recombination during development). Antigen "selects" the matching B cell to proliferate, generating an antibody-secreting **clone** plus **memory cells**. The lymphocyte's specificity is determined *before* antigen exposure — the antigen merely activates the pre-existing clone.
- **Active immunity** = primary exposure-driven antibody and memory cell production. Achieved by infection, vaccination with attenuated or killed pathogens, or pure recombinant antigens. Vaccines induce immunity without disease.
- **Immunological tolerance** = failure of immune response to self-antigens:
  - **Central tolerance** — thymic deletion (clonal deletion) of autoreactive T cells during T-cell maturation; bone-marrow deletion or anergy induction of autoreactive B cells.
  - **Peripheral tolerance** — Treg-mediated suppression of autoreactive lymphocytes outside primary lymphoid organs.
- **Passive immunity** = transfer of preformed antibodies:
  - **Natural** — transplacental IgG from mother to fetus; IgA in colostrum and breast milk.
  - **Artificial** — antitoxin / antiserum injection (tetanus, rabies, snake venom); intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, pooled donor IgG); monoclonal antibody therapy (rituximab, trastuzumab, bevacizumab).
- **Monoclonal antibodies** are produced by **hybridomas** — a single B cell fused with a myeloma cell (immortalized line) yielding a clonal antibody factory. >25 therapeutic monoclonal antibodies approved (targeting TNF, IgE, HER2, VEGF, CD20, checkpoint receptors).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "According to this theory, B lymphocytes inherit the ability to produce particular antibodies (and T lymphocytes inherit the ability to respond to particular antigens)." (p. 515)
- > "Some of these cells become plasma cells that secrete antibodies for the primary response; others become memory B cells that can be stimulated to secrete antibodies during the secondary response." (p. 515)
- > "The development of a secondary response provides active immunity against the specific pathogens. The development of active immunity requires prior exposure to the specific antigens, at which time the sluggishness of the primary response may cause the person to develop the disease." (p. 515)
- > "Passive immunity refers to the immune protection that can be produced by the transfer of antibodies to a recipient from a human or animal donor." (p. 517)
- > "Through this transfer of antibodies, the fetus and the newborn baby are immune to the same antigens as the mother. However, because the baby did not itself produce the lymphocyte clones needed to form these antibodies, such passive immunity gradually disappears." (p. 517)

Left side shows successive horizontal rows (First, Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth day), each row depicting the cell population after the next round of division — starting from a single B cell at top and branching into a pyramid of ~32 cells at the bottom. Each cell is drawn as a small pink sphere; the lines connecting daughter cells show binary fission at each step. Right side shows the parallel maturation pathway in detail: **Day 1** — a small naive **B lymphocyte** with prominent nucleus and minimal cytoplasm; **Day 2** — the same cell with ribosomes appearing; **Day 3** — cytoplasm now contains visible endoplasmic reticulum; **Day 4** — cell is enlarged with abundant ER; **Day 5** — divided into **memory cells** (small, gray-cytoplasm cells with simpler interior) and **plasma cells** (larger, with elaborate rough ER for antibody secretion). The bottom of the diagram is labeled "Development of clone." The teaching point: antigen exposure of a single naive B cell triggers serial divisions over several days, producing both long-lived memory cells (for the secondary response) and short-lived plasma cells (the antibody factories for the primary response).

> Vision note: This figure is the canonical reference for the entire mechanism of adaptive immunity and vaccine response. Anchor for primary vs. secondary response, vaccine timing, and any "how do antibodies get made fast" query.

## Section 15.5 — Tumor Immunology (pp. 518–520)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Innate Lymphoid Cells** (pp. 519–520)
- **Effects of Aging and Stress** (p. 520)

### Core claims
- **Immunological surveillance** = continuous patrol of body by T cells and NK cells, which detect and destroy nascent tumor cells before they can grow.
- **Tumor antigens** arise from: (1) **dedifferentiation** producing fetal antigens (carcinoembryonic antigen for colon cancer; α-fetoprotein for liver cancer); (2) **viral oncoproteins** (HPV E6/E7 in cervical cancer; EBV in Burkitt's lymphoma); (3) **mutated proteins (neoantigens)** from somatic carcinogen-induced mutations; (4) **overexpressed self-antigens** from gene amplification.
- **Immune checkpoint blockade (2018 Nobel — Allison and Honjo):** tumors evade attack by engaging inhibitory T-cell checkpoint receptors (CTLA-4, PD-1). Antibody drugs (ipilimumab targeting CTLA-4; nivolumab, pembrolizumab targeting PD-1) block these brakes and unleash anti-tumor T-cell activity. Major successes in advanced melanoma and non-small-cell lung cancer.
- **Adoptive cell transfer:**
  - **TIL therapy** — tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes harvested, expanded in vitro with IL-2, reinfused.
  - **CAR-T therapy** — patient's T cells engineered to express a chimeric antigen receptor (e.g., anti-CD19); reinfused; remarkable success in pediatric ALL and other B-cell malignancies; limited success in solid tumors.
- **Innate lymphoid cells (ILCs):** lymphocyte-like cells lacking antigen-specific receptors. Two subclasses: **noncytotoxic helper ILCs** (innate equivalent of TH cells; produce IL-5, IL-6; prominent at mucosal barriers); **natural killer (NK) cells** (innate equivalent of CTLs; kill tumor and virus-infected cells; express receptors that recognize class I MHC for self-tolerance; recently shown to develop memory).
- **Aging and stress** reduce immune competence. Stress-induced corticosteroid secretion suppresses immunity (basis for medical use of cortisone in inflammation and transplant rejection); thymus involutes; lymphocyte mutation burden accumulates with age, contributing to age-associated cancer risk.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "When cancers develop, this immunological surveillance system—primarily the function of T cells and natural killer cells—has failed to prevent the growth and metastasis of the tumor." (p. 518)
- > "Immune checkpoint blockade involves the administration of antibodies that block the inhibitory checkpoint receptors on cytotoxic T cells. Blocking inhibitory checkpoint receptors unleashes the T cells' ability to attack the tumor … A Japanese and an American scientist won the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their discoveries leading to checkpoint blockade therapy." (p. 519)
- > "Natural killer (NK) cells are ILCs that are analogous to the cytotoxic T cells of the adaptive immune system, but they lack the receptor diversity generated by gene rearrangements to provide receptors for specific antigens." (p. 519)

## Section 15.6 — Diseases Caused by the Immune System (pp. 520–525)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Autoimmunity** (pp. 520–522)
- **Immune Complex Diseases** (p. 522)
- **Allergy** (pp. 522–525)
  - **Immediate Hypersensitivity** (pp. 522–525)
  - **Delayed Hypersensitivity** (p. 525)

### Core claims
- **Autoimmune diseases** affect 5–7% of the population; two-thirds of patients are women. Mechanisms of self-tolerance failure:
  1. **Sequestered antigens released** (thyroglobulin in Hashimoto's; lens protein in sympathetic ophthalmia).
  2. **Hapten modification** of self-antigens (drug-induced thrombocytopenia).
  3. **Anti-antibody production** (rheumatoid factor — IgM anti-IgG in rheumatoid arthritis).
  4. **Molecular mimicry** — anti-pathogen antibodies cross-react with self-antigens (rheumatic fever, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis).
  5. **Aberrant class II MHC expression** on cells that normally don't present antigen (Graves' disease — thyroid cells present TSH-receptor antigen; type 1 diabetes — β cells present insulin antigens).
  6. **Treg insufficiency** (FOXP3 deficiency → IPEX syndrome).
- **Immune complex diseases:** circulating antigen-antibody complexes deposit in vessels, glomeruli, joints; activate complement; chronic inflammation. **SLE** — IgG autoantibodies against nuclear constituents (chromatin, dsDNA, snRNP); ANA test for diagnosis; 90% female; treated with antimalarials, NSAIDs, immunosuppressives. **Rheumatoid arthritis** — autoantibodies against citrullinated proteins (CCP test) and IgG (rheumatoid factor); chronic synovial inflammation.
- **Allergy / hypersensitivity** = inappropriate immune response to a foreign antigen (allergen). Two main types:
  - **Immediate hypersensitivity (Type I)** — IgE-mediated; symptoms in seconds-to-minutes. Allergen → TH2 secretion of IL-4/5/13 → B-cell class switch to **IgE** → IgE binds Fc receptors on mast cells and basophils → re-exposure → cross-linking of IgE on mast cells → degranulation → **histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins** release → vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, mucus, edema. Conditions: hay fever, allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, food allergies, anaphylaxis. Treatment: antihistamines (H1 blockers), β2-agonists (asthma), corticosteroids, leukotriene receptor antagonists, epinephrine for anaphylaxis.
  - **Delayed hypersensitivity (Type IV)** — T cell–mediated; symptoms in 24–72 h. Memory T cells re-exposed to antigen → secrete lymphokines → recruit macrophages and other lymphocytes. Examples: poison ivy / contact dermatitis (haptenization of skin proteins), tuberculin skin test (Mantoux), graft rejection. Treatment: corticosteroids; antihistamines are ineffective.
- **Anaphylaxis** = systemic mast-cell degranulation → widespread vasodilation, drop in blood pressure (anaphylactic shock), bronchoconstriction, laryngeal edema. Emergency treatment: intramuscular epinephrine.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "There are over 40 known or suspected autoimmune diseases that affect 5% to 7% of the population. Two-thirds of those affected are women." (p. 521)
- > "Anaphylaxis occurs when there is widespread release of histamine and other mast cell molecules in response to an allergen, resulting in systemwide and life-threatening effects (including anaphylactic shock)." (p. 523)
- > "In delayed hypersensitivity, as the name implies, symptoms take a longer time (hours to days) to develop than in immediate hypersensitivity. This may be because immediate hypersensitivity is mediated by antibodies, whereas delayed hypersensitivity is a cell-mediated T lymphocyte response." (p. 525)

### Figures

#### Figure 15.23 — The mechanism of immediate hypersensitivity *(viewed)*

A schematic four-stage progression of the IgE allergy pathway. **Stage 1 (left):** A free **allergen** (small red molecule) encounters a B cell, which differentiates into a **plasma cell** secreting IgE antibodies (purple Y-shaped molecules). **Stage 2:** The IgE antibodies enter the local mucosa and attach via their **Fc** stems to **IgE receptors** on the surface of a **mast cell** (large beige cell with multiple intracellular **granules** — small dark dots representing histamine vesicles). **Stage 3:** On second exposure, the same allergen now cross-links two adjacent IgE molecules already bound to the mast cell. **Stage 4 (right):** Cross-linking triggers mast-cell degranulation — granules fuse with the plasma membrane and release **histamine and other chemicals** (depicted as small dots dispersing into the tissue), causing the symptoms of **allergy** labeled at the far right. The arrow chain from allergen → IgE production → mast-cell sensitization → re-exposure → degranulation makes the entire immediate hypersensitivity cascade visible in one figure.

> Vision note: This is the anchor for any IgE/allergy/anaphylaxis/asthma query, including the rationale for omalizumab (anti-IgE) and for epinephrine in anaphylaxis. Critical for Ch 16 asthma discussion.

## Clinical apparatus

- **Clinical Investigation: Timmy** — chapter-opening case. 11-year-old receives Tdap vaccine (active immunity to tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis); later cuts his hand on a rusty can (local inflammation with histamine-mediated swelling and PGE₂-mediated pain; pus from neutrophil proteases and apoptosis); has seasonal cough treated with antihistamine (immediate-hypersensitivity allergic rhinitis with mast-cell histamine release).
- **Clinical Applications:**
  - **AIDS / HIV / ART** (p. 508) — HIV preferentially infects CD4⁺ helper T cells (especially GI-mucosal); ART suppresses but does not cure (memory T-cell integration); checkpoint and vaccine research ongoing.
  - **Sepsis** (p. 509) — life-threatening organ dysfunction from dysregulated immune response to infection; up to one-third of hospital deaths; symptoms include high fever, hypotension, hypoxemia, oliguria, acidosis. **Septic shock** when BP drop is profound. **Endotoxin (LPS)** of Gram-negative bacteria is the prototypical trigger.
  - **Humanized monoclonal antibodies and CAR-T** (p. 519) — chimeric mouse/human antibodies (rituximab, trastuzumab, bevacizumab); CAR-T-cell therapy for B-cell leukemias.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Innate (nonspecific) immunity** — inherited, broad-target defenses: barriers, phagocytes, interferons, complement, fever, NK cells, mast cells.
- **Adaptive (specific) immunity** — postnatally generated lymphocyte responses with antigen specificity and memory.
- **Pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)** — conserved microbial molecule (LPS, peptidoglycan) recognized by innate receptors.
- **Toll-like receptor (TLR)** — pathogen-recognition receptor of innate immunity; 10 in humans; 2011 Nobel.
- **NOD-like receptor / inflammasome** — cytoplasmic pathogen sensor; activates caspase and cytokine release.
- **Damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP)** — molecule released by necrotic (not apoptotic) cells; provokes sterile inflammation.
- **Phagocyte** — cell that engulfs material: neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells, organ-specific (Kupffer, microglia, alveolar, Langerhans).
- **Extravasation (diapedesis)** — leukocyte exit from postcapillary venule into tissue via rolling/capture/adhesion/transmigration.
- **Chemokine** — chemoattractant cytokine.
- **Interferons (α, β, γ)** — antiviral and immunomodulatory cytokines; α/β from infected cells; γ from NK and T cells.
- **Complement system** — ~30 plasma proteins; C1–C9 cascade with recognition, activation, and attack (MAC) phases.
- **Opsonization** — coating of pathogens (with antibodies or C3b) to enhance phagocytosis.
- **Membrane attack complex (MAC)** — C5b–C9 transmembrane pore that lyses target cells.
- **Antigen** — large foreign molecule that elicits specific immunity; has multiple **epitopes** (determinant sites).
- **Hapten** — small molecule that becomes antigenic only when bound to carrier protein.
- **B lymphocyte** — bone-marrow-derived; secretes antibodies; humoral immunity.
- **T lymphocyte** — thymus-processed; cytotoxic (CD8⁺), helper (CD4⁺), or regulatory; cell-mediated immunity.
- **Primary lymphoid organ** — bone marrow, thymus (lymphocyte production/processing).
- **Secondary lymphoid organ** — lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, Peyer's patches (lymphocyte-antigen encounter).
- **Plasma cell** — antibody-secreting effector B cell (~2,000 antibodies/sec).
- **Memory cell** — long-lived progeny that mediates secondary response.
- **Immunoglobulin (Ig)** — antibody protein; classes IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE.
- **Variable / constant region (Fab / Fc)** — antigen-binding tip vs. effector-binding stem of antibody.
- **VDJ recombination** — antigen-independent gene rearrangement generating antibody diversity.
- **Somatic hypermutation** — antigen-dependent point mutation in V regions during germinal-center expansion; increases affinity.
- **Class switch recombination** — IgM → IgG/IgA/IgE during germinal-center maturation.
- **MHC class I / II** — present endogenous peptides to CD8⁺ / exogenous peptides to CD4⁺ T cells.
- **Perforin / granzyme** — pore-forming protein + apoptotic protease released by CTLs and NK cells.
- **Helper T cell (CD4⁺)** — secretes cytokines; subtypes TH1, TH2, TH17.
- **Regulatory T cell (Treg)** — FOXP3⁺ suppressor; enforces peripheral tolerance.
- **Antigen-presenting cell (APC)** — dendritic cells, macrophages, activated B cells displaying antigen on class II MHC.
- **Clonal selection theory** — antigen selects a pre-existing lymphocyte clone for expansion.
- **Active immunity** — primary-exposure-induced specific protection (natural infection or vaccination).
- **Passive immunity** — transfer of preformed antibodies (placental IgG, breast milk IgA, antitoxin, IVIG).
- **Hybridoma / monoclonal antibody** — B cell-myeloma fusion producing a single antibody specificity; therapeutic and diagnostic use.
- **Natural killer (NK) cell** — innate lymphoid cell with cytotoxic capability; recognizes loss of self class I MHC.
- **Immune checkpoint blockade** — antibody therapy (anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1) that releases T-cell inhibition in cancer; 2018 Nobel.
- **CAR-T therapy** — patient T cells engineered with chimeric antigen receptor; pediatric B-cell ALL treatment.
- **Immunological tolerance** — failure to respond to self-antigens; central (clonal deletion/anergy in thymus/bone marrow) and peripheral (Treg-mediated) components.
- **Autoimmunity** — pathologic immune attack on self-antigens (Hashimoto's, Graves', SLE, RA, MS, type 1 diabetes).
- **Immune complex disease** — pathology from circulating antigen-antibody complexes (SLE, RA, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis).
- **Immediate hypersensitivity (Type I)** — IgE-mediated allergy with mast-cell histamine release; minutes onset.
- **Delayed hypersensitivity (Type IV)** — T cell-mediated; hours-to-days onset (contact dermatitis, tuberculin skin test).
- **Anaphylaxis** — systemic immediate hypersensitivity; treated with intramuscular epinephrine.
