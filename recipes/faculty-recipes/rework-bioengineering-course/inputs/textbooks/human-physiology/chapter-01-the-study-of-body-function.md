## Section 1.1 — Introduction to Physiology (pp. 2–4)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Scientific Method** (p. 2)

### Core claims
- Physiology (Greek *physis* = nature; *logos* = study) is the study of biological function — of how the body works from molecular mechanisms within cells through tissues, organs, and systems, framed by cause-and-effect questions starting with *how*.
- **Pathophysiology** is concerned with how physiological processes are altered in disease or injury; pathophysiology and normal physiology complement each other (the Nobel category is "Physiology or Medicine").
- **Comparative physiology** studies invertebrates and other vertebrate groups; humans and other mammals are more alike than different, so animal experiments transfer to humans.
- The **scientific method** has three attributes: (1) belief the natural world is explainable; (2) explanations grounded in observation and falsifiable; (3) humility — willingness to be wrong.
- Hypothesis testing pipeline: observation → hypothesis → experiment → statistical evaluation (often via a **null hypothesis**) → support or rejection → integration into a **theory**. "Proof" in science is always provisional.
- Peer review and reproducibility are required for findings to become accepted; experiments must include **controls** to isolate the variable being tested. Double-blind designs prevent observer bias.
- **Drug-development pipeline (4 phases of clinical trials):** Phase I — small healthy-volunteer toxicity; Phase II — target patient population; Phase III — large multi-center, broad demographic; Phase IV — post-marketing surveillance. Animal research precedes Phase I and is the scientific foundation. Less than 10% of tested drugs reach market.
- **Animal models** are strains of rats/mice genetically susceptible to specific diseases. Phase III population must include sufficient numbers of both sexes and of different ethnic groups.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Human physiology is the study of how the human body functions, with emphasis on specific cause-and-effect mechanisms." (p. 2)
- > "Pathophysiology and the study of normal physiology complement one another." (p. 2)
- > "All of the information in this text has been gained by people applying the scientific method." (p. 2)
- > "Scientific theories are thus not simply conjectures; they are statements about the natural world that incorporate a number of hypotheses that have been supported by scientific evidence." (p. 2)
- > "Because of the nature of the scientific method, 'proof' in science is always provisional." (p. 3)
- > "In **phase I clinical trials**, the drug is tested on healthy human volunteers." (p. 4)

- **Positive Feedback** (p. 8)

- **Feedback Control of Hormone Secretion** (pp. 9–10)

### Core claims
- **Historical lineage:** Aristotle (384–322 BC); Erastistratus (304–250 BC, considered first physiologist); Galen (AD 130–210); William Harvey (1578–1657, circulation); **Claude Bernard (1813–1878)** — *milieu intérieur*; **Walter B. Cannon (1871–1945)** in *The Wisdom of the Body* (1932) coined **homeostasis**; James Hardin (1950s) formalized control variables and set points.
- **Homeostasis** = the dynamic constancy of the *internal environment*, maintained by negative feedback loops. Not absolute constancy — values oscillate around the set point.
- **The negative feedback loop has at minimum:**
  - A **sensor (receptor)** that detects deviation in the regulated variable.
  - An **integrating center** that compares the sensor signal against a set point.
  - An **effector** (muscle or gland) that acts to oppose the deviation.
  - Negative feedback is depicted with dashed lines and negative signs throughout the textbook.
- **Set point analogy:** a thermostat. The body defends set points "more efficiently than a house thermostat" because effectors are continuously modulated (not on/off).
- **Antagonistic effectors**: most parameters are defended by opposing mechanisms — e.g., body temperature by **sweating vs. shivering**, blood glucose by **insulin vs. glucagon/epinephrine/cortisol**, heart rate by sympathetic vs. parasympathetic nerve fibers. Antagonistic control is "push-pull" and produces finer regulation than on/off.
- **Quantitative measurements** define normal ranges; deviations identify disease. Table 1.2 (p. 6) lists reference ranges for fasting blood values.
- **Positive feedback** amplifies a stimulus and is **not** a homeostatic mechanism in isolation; it operates as a step within a larger negative feedback process. Classic examples:
  - **Blood clotting** — a cascade in which each clotting factor activates many others; clot formation then arrests bleeding (closing a negative loop).
  - **Estrogen → LH surge → ovulation.** (After ovulation, estrogen flips to negative feedback on LH — the basis of combined oral contraceptives.)
  - **Oxytocin → uterine contraction → more oxytocin during parturition.**
- **Neural and endocrine regulation** form the two extrinsic regulatory systems:
  - **Intrinsic** regulation = built into the organ (e.g., paracrine molecules in blood vessel walls).
  - **Extrinsic** regulation = via nerves or hormones reaching the organ from elsewhere.
- Nerve fibers **innervate** target organs and conduct electrochemical impulses to muscles or glands. Hormones travel in blood, but only **target organs** with the receptor can respond.
- **Worked example:** standing from supine → blood pressure transiently falls → baroreceptors fire less → medulla oblongata (integrating center) → motor nerves to heart → ↑ heart rate → blood pressure restored (Fig 1.6).
- **Feedback control of hormone secretion (insulin example):**
  - Eating → ↑ plasma glucose → pancreatic islets secrete **insulin** → cellular glucose uptake → ↓ plasma glucose → ↓ insulin secretion. This closed-loop is called **negative feedback inhibition**.
  - During fasting, the antagonistic hormone **glucagon** rises and stimulates the liver to secrete glucose, keeping plasma glucose from falling too far.
- **Insulin demonstration experiment (Fig 1.5):** intravenous insulin in 5 healthy subjects drops blood glucose rapidly; antagonistic hormones restore it within ~80 minutes.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Walter Cannon (1871–1945), who coined the term **homeostasis** to describe this internal constancy." (p. 4)
- > "Cannon further suggested that the many mechanisms of physiological regulation have but one purpose — the maintenance of internal constancy." (p. 4)
- > "Homeostasis is best conceived as a state of **dynamic constancy** in which conditions are stabilized above and below the set point." (p. 6)
- > "Because the activity of the effectors is influenced by the effects they produce, and because this regulation is in a negative, or reverse, direction, this type of control system is known as a **negative feedback loop**." (p. 6)
- > "Control by antagonistic effectors is sometimes described as 'push-pull,' where the increasing activity of one effector is accompanied by decreasing activity of an antagonistic effector. This affords a finer degree of control than could be achieved by simply switching one effector on and off." (p. 7)
- > "It is clear that homeostasis must ultimately be maintained by negative rather than by positive feedback mechanisms." (p. 8)
- > "These target organs can be muscles or glands that may function as effectors in the maintenance of homeostasis." (p. 8)
- > "This closed-loop control system is called **negative feedback inhibition**." (p. 8)
- > "Homeostasis of blood glucose is too important — the brain uses blood glucose as its primary source of energy — to entrust to the regulation of only one hormone, insulin." (p. 8)

### Equations / quantitative
- No formal equations, but the chapter introduces the **set-point arithmetic** used in the Review Activities: given oscillation between $T_{\min}$ and $T_{\max}$, the **set point = average** $\frac{T_{\min}+T_{\max}}{2}$; the **normal range** is $T_{\max}-T_{\min}$. Worked example: 36.6 °C and 37.7 °C → set point 37.15 °C, range 1.1 °C.

### Core claims
- **Four primary tissues:** muscle, nerve, epithelial, connective. Cells with similar functions are grouped into **tissues**; tissues into **organs**; organs into **systems**.

- **Muscle tissue** is specialized for contraction. Three subtypes:
  - **Skeletal muscle** — striated, voluntary, **multinucleated** (many peripheral nuclei per fiber), forms graded contractions by recruiting more muscle fibers.
  - **Cardiac muscle** — striated, involuntary, **intercalated discs** electrically and mechanically couple cells so the heart contracts as a syncytium ("wholehearted" contraction). The heart cannot grade contraction by recruiting more cells.
  - **Smooth muscle** — non-striated, involuntary, **single central nucleus** per cell, found in digestive tract, blood vessels, bronchioles, urinary/reproductive ducts. Circular smooth muscle produces lumen constriction; longitudinal layers shorten the tube.

- **Nerve tissue** consists of:
  - **Neurons** — specialized for generating and conducting impulses; cell body + **dendrites** (input branches) + **axon** (output, can be feet long).
  - **Neuroglia (glia)** — support cells that bind neurons together, modify the extracellular environment, contribute to chemical neurotransmission, and remain mitotically active throughout life (whereas mature neurons do not divide).

- **Epithelial tissue** = membranes + glands.
  - **Epithelial membranes** classified by **number of layers** (simple = 1; stratified = >1) and **cell shape** (squamous = flat; cuboidal = roughly cubical; columnar = taller than wide).
  - Goblet cells in columnar epithelium secrete mucus.
  - Simple ciliated columnar epithelium lines uterine tubes; pseudostratified ciliated columnar lines respiratory passageways.
  - Stratified squamous **nonkeratinized** epithelium lines oral cavity, vagina, esophagus (moist, pliable).
  - Stratified squamous **keratinized** epithelium is the **epidermis** (dry, tough).
  - **Transitional epithelium** lines bladder, ureters: rounded nonkeratinized cells that stretch.
  - All epithelial membranes sit on a **basement membrane** (extracellular structural proteins) that anchors epithelium to the underlying connective tissue. Basement membranes are clinical landmarks — cancer cells become metastatic when they break through.
  - **Exocrine glands** (Greek *exo* = outside) develop from epithelial invaginations that retain a duct to the surface (e.g., sweat, salivary). **Endocrine glands** (Greek *endon* = within) pinch off, losing the duct, and secrete hormones directly into blood.
  - Exocrine gland architecture classified by duct branching (simple vs. compound) and secretory unit shape (tubular vs. acinar).

- **Connective tissue** is characterized by large extracellular space:
  - **Loose (areolar) connective tissue** — matrix of collagen + elastic + reticular fibers in a ground substance; resident cells include fibroblasts, mesenchymal cells, macrophages.
  - **Dense regular connective tissue** — parallel collagen bundles, e.g., tendons (muscle-to-bone) and ligaments (bone-to-bone).
  - **Adipose tissue** — fat-storing tissue dominated by adipocytes containing one large central lipid droplet with peripheral nuclei.
  - **Cartilage** — chondrocytes in a flexible collagen matrix with **ground substance ("gristle")**; avascular; nourished by diffusion from connective tissue (perichondrium).
  - **Bone** — calcified matrix; concentric lamellae arranged around a central canal; osteocytes in **lacunae** connected by tiny canals (**canaliculi**); blood vessels run in central canals.
  - **Blood** is classified as a connective tissue because cells are widely spaced in extracellular fluid (plasma).

## Section 1.4 — Organs and Systems (pp. 17–20)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **An Example of an Organ: The Skin** (pp. 18–19)
- **Stem Cells** (p. 19)
- **Systems** (pp. 19–20)
- **Body-Fluid Compartments** (p. 20)

### Core claims
- An **organ** contains at least two — usually all four — primary tissues. **Skin** is the largest organ by surface area and is the chapter's model: epidermis (epithelial); dermis (connective); hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands (epithelial-derived exocrine glands within the dermis); sensory and motor nerves (nerve); arrector pili muscle (muscle). The epidermis also synthesizes vitamin D, making the skin an endocrine gland.
- Most organs share the architecture: epithelium → basement membrane → connective tissue with vessels and nerves → ± muscle.

- **Stem cells (developmental potency hierarchy):**
  - **Totipotent** — the zygote; can produce *every* body cell type plus placenta.
  - **Pluripotent** — embryonic stem cells (after implantation stage); produce all body cells except placental tissue.
  - **Multipotent** — **adult stem cells**; can form a limited variety of related cell types.
  - Genes are not lost during differentiation. Demonstrated by Gurdon (1960s, nuclear transfer in frogs) and by Yamanaka (2006, induced pluripotent stem cells from differentiated fibroblasts via a few transcription factors) — 2012 Nobel.
- **Adult stem-cell reservoirs:** red bone marrow (blood cells), brain (Ch 8), skeletal muscle (Ch 12), intestine (Ch 18), and **the bulge region of the hair follicle** (Fig 1.23) — these produce keratinocytes, sebaceous gland cells, and melanocyte stem cells; gray hair results from loss of bulge melanocyte stem cells.

- **Body-fluid compartments:**
  - **Intracellular compartment** ≈ 65% of total body water.
  - **Extracellular compartment** ≈ 35% of total body water; subdivided into:
    - **Plasma** — fluid portion of blood.
    - **Interstitial fluid** — fluid bathing the cells of organs; communicates freely with plasma through capillary walls.
  - The plasma membrane separates intracellular from extracellular; the capillary endothelium separates plasma from interstitial fluid. The kidneys regulate plasma volume/composition and thus indirectly the entire extracellular compartment.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "An **organ** is a structure composed of at least two, and usually all four, primary tissues. The largest organ in the body, in terms of surface area, is the skin." (p. 18)
- > "The architecture of most organs is similar to that of the skin." (p. 19)
- > "The zygote is totipotent — it can produce all of the different specialized cells of the body." (p. 19)
- > "In 2006 and later, Japanese scientists found that they could transform differentiated fibroblasts into a pluripotent state, similar to embryonic stem cells, by treating the fibroblasts with a few specific regulatory molecules." (p. 19)
- > "Adult stem cells can form a variety of related cell types; the adult stem cells are therefore described as multipotent." (p. 19)
- > "About 65% of the total body water is in the intracellular compartment, while about 35% is in the extracellular compartment." (p. 20)
- > "In most parts of the body, blood plasma and interstitial fluid communicate freely through blood capillaries." (p. 20)

- **Clinical Investigation: Linda** — opens the chapter. Linda's body temperature and fasting glucose are checked; the case asks whether homeostasis is intact. Summary at end of chapter: normal values indicate homeostasis is being maintained; abnormal fasting glucose would suggest **diabetes mellitus** (insulin's lowering effect is inadequate).
- **Clinical Application: Basement membranes** (p. 13) — basement membranes consist of structural proteins (laminin, collagen IV); cancer cells become metastatic when they break through basement membranes. **Exfoliative cytology** (Pap smear) examines shed surface cells to detect early-stage cancers before basement membrane invasion.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Physiology** — study of biological function; how the body works at all scales.
- **Pathophysiology** — study of how physiological processes are altered in disease.
- **Comparative physiology** — physiology of nonhuman animals; informs human physiology.
- **Scientific method** — observation → hypothesis → controlled experiment → statistical analysis → support/rejection → theory.
- **Null hypothesis** — the hypothesis that an observed difference is due to chance.
- **Clinical trials (Phases I–IV)** — sequential drug testing in healthy volunteers, target patients, large multi-center cohorts, and post-marketing surveillance.
- **Homeostasis** — dynamic constancy of the internal environment (Cannon, 1932).
- **Milieu intérieur** — internal environment of the body (Claude Bernard, 1813–1878).
- **Set point** — the target value defended by a homeostatic loop.
- **Negative feedback loop** — sensor + integrating center + effector; output opposes the disturbance.
- **Positive feedback** — output amplifies the disturbance; in physiology, used selectively (clotting, LH surge, parturition).
- **Sensor (receptor)** — detector of deviation in the regulated variable.
- **Integrating center** — compares sensor input to set point; computes corrective output.
- **Effector** — muscle or gland that acts to correct the deviation.
- **Antagonistic effectors** — two effectors with opposing actions on the same parameter (e.g., insulin vs. glucagon; sweat vs. shiver).
- **Dynamic constancy** — oscillation of a regulated variable above and below a set point.
- **Intrinsic regulation** — regulation built into the organ itself (e.g., paracrine signaling within a vessel wall).
- **Extrinsic regulation** — regulation by nerves or hormones from outside the organ.
- **Target organ** — organ whose cells bear receptors for a specific hormone.
- **Negative feedback inhibition** — a hormone whose own downstream effect inhibits further secretion of itself (e.g., insulin / blood glucose).
- **Primary tissues** — muscle, nerve, epithelial, connective.
- **Skeletal / cardiac / smooth muscle** — voluntary striated multinucleated / involuntary striated with intercalated discs / involuntary nonstriated with single nuclei.
- **Intercalated discs** — junctions that electrically and mechanically couple cardiac muscle cells.
- **Neurons / neuroglia** — impulse-conducting cells / supporting glia of nervous tissue.
- **Epithelial membrane** — sheet of tightly joined cells covering or lining body surfaces.
- **Simple vs. stratified epithelium** — one layer vs. multiple layers.
- **Squamous / cuboidal / columnar** — flat / cube / column-shaped epithelial cells.
- **Goblet cell** — mucus-secreting cell within columnar epithelium.
- **Basement membrane** — extracellular protein layer anchoring epithelium to underlying connective tissue; barrier breached by metastatic cancer.
- **Exocrine gland** — secretes via a duct (e.g., sweat, salivary, pancreatic exocrine).
- **Endocrine gland** — secretes hormones directly into blood.
- **Connective tissue** — tissue with abundant extracellular matrix; includes loose, dense regular, adipose, cartilage, bone, blood.
- **Fibroblast** — collagen-producing cell of connective tissue.
- **Adipocyte** — fat-storing connective tissue cell.
- **Chondrocyte** — cartilage cell.
- **Osteocyte / lacuna / canaliculi** — bone cell housed in a lacuna, communicating through canaliculi.
- **Organ** — multi-tissue functional unit (e.g., skin).
- **System** — anatomically dispersed organs with a shared function (e.g., circulatory, urinary).
- **Totipotent / pluripotent / multipotent** — increasingly restricted developmental potency (zygote / embryonic / adult stem cells).
- **Induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cell** — adult differentiated cell reprogrammed to pluripotency (Yamanaka, 2006).
- **Bulge region** — stem-cell reservoir within the hair follicle.
- **Intracellular vs. extracellular compartment** — fluid inside vs. outside cells.
- **Plasma / interstitial fluid** — extracellular subcompartments; plasma is the fluid portion of blood, interstitial fluid bathes tissue cells.
- **Exfoliative cytology** — cytologic examination of shed surface cells for early cancer detection (e.g., Pap smear).
