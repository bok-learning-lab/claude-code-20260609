### Core claims
- **Endocrine glands** secrete **hormones** directly into the blood (no ducts). Hormones travel to **target cells** bearing specific receptor proteins. The system has dual nature: organs whose *primary* role is endocrine (pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreatic islets, gonads, pineal) and organs with mixed roles (heart, liver, adipose, kidneys, GI tract, placenta, skin) (Table 11.1).
- **Three regulatory requirements** apply to *both* hormones and neurotransmitters: (1) specific receptors on target cells; (2) defined intracellular consequences; (3) a turn-off mechanism (rapid removal or chemical inactivation).
- **Four chemical classes of hormones:**
  - **Amines** — derived from tyrosine (catecholamines, thyroid hormones) or tryptophan (melatonin).
  - **Polypeptides/proteins** — ranging from 9 aa ADH/oxytocin to 191 aa GH.
  - **Glycoproteins** — protein + carbohydrate (FSH, LH, TSH).
  - **Steroids** — derived from cholesterol; secreted by adrenal cortex and gonads (Fig 11.2).
- **Lipophilic vs. polar** hormones (Table 11.2):
  - **Lipophilic** (steroids, thyroid hormones, melatonin) — small, nonpolar; pass through plasma membranes; carried by plasma carrier proteins; receptors are intracellular nuclear receptors. Active orally as pills.
  - **Polar / water-soluble** (peptides, glycoproteins, catecholamines) — cannot cross membranes; bind cell-surface receptors; trigger second messengers. Inactive if swallowed because digested.
- **Prohormones** — inactive precursors processed within the endocrine cell into the active hormone (preproinsulin → proinsulin → insulin).
- **Prehormones** — inactive secreted forms that target cells must convert into active derivatives (Table 11.3):
  - **T4 → T3** (most peripheral tissues by deiodinase).
  - **Vitamin D₃ (cholecalciferol) → 1,25-(OH)₂-vitamin D₃** (sequential hydroxylation in liver then kidney).
  - **Testosterone → DHT** (5α-reductase in prostate, skin).
  - **Testosterone → estradiol-17β** (aromatase, brain).
- **Hormone interactions:**
  - **Synergistic** — additive (E + NE on heart) or complementary (estrogen + cortisol + prolactin + oxytocin for lactation).
  - **Permissive** — one hormone prepares the target for another (estradiol induces progesterone receptors in uterus; PTH increases the hydroxylating enzymes needed to convert vitamin D₃ to its active form).
  - **Antagonistic** — opposing effects (estrogen inhibits prolactin during pregnancy; insulin vs. glucagon on adipose).
- **Effect of concentration:**
  - **Half-life** ranges from minutes to hours (thyroxine ~7 days). Steroids are degraded in liver to polar derivatives excreted in urine and bile.
  - **Physiologic vs. pharmacologic** concentrations produce different effects. Pharmacologic doses of androgens → abnormal estrogen production (gynecomastia). Long-term cortisone → osteoporosis.
  - **Upregulation / priming** — increased receptor density after pulsatile hormone exposure (e.g., GnRH).
  - **Downregulation / desensitization** — receptor numbers fall with continuous high hormone (e.g., LH on testicular Leydig cells). **Pulsatile secretion** prevents desensitization; continuous-infusion GnRH paradoxically suppresses gonadal function.

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

- **Hormones That Use Second Messengers** (pp. 325–330)
  - **Adenylate Cyclase–Cyclic AMP Second-Messenger System** (pp. 325–327)
  - **Phospholipase C–Ca²⁺ Second-Messenger System** (pp. 327–328)
  - **Tyrosine Kinase Second-Messenger System** (pp. 328–330)

### Core claims
- **Receptor location depends on solubility:**
  - **Lipophilic hormones** (steroids, T3/T4, vitamin D, retinoic acid) → cytoplasmic or nuclear receptors that function as **ligand-activated transcription factors**.
  - **Polar hormones** (peptides, glycoproteins, catecholamines) → plasma-membrane receptors → second messengers.
- **Nuclear receptor architecture** (Fig 11.5): two domains, a **ligand-binding domain** and a **DNA-binding domain**. Receptors bind a **hormone response element** of DNA — two half-sites separated by a 3-nucleotide spacer.
- **Steroid receptor mechanism** (Fig 11.4): hormone dissociates from carrier protein → diffuses through plasma membrane → binds cytoplasmic receptor → heat-shock-protein chaperone dissociates → hormone-receptor complex translocates to nucleus → two complexes **homodimerize** on the hormone response element → recruit coactivators → transcribe → mRNA → protein synthesis. Takes ≥30 min (the **genomic action**).
- **Nongenomic steroid action** (seconds-minutes): plasma-membrane GPCR binding (e.g., progesterone hyperactivates sperm in seconds; Ch 20).
- **Thyroid hormone mechanism** (Figs 11.6, 11.7): T4 enters cell from blood, is converted to **T3** intracellularly (the active form). T3 enters nucleus. The **T3 receptor (TR)** is *already bound to DNA* even without ligand, recruiting **corepressors** that silence the gene. T3 binding swaps corepressors for **coactivators**. The TR forms a **heterodimer** with **RXR (9-cis-retinoic acid receptor)**, linking thyroid action to vitamin A metabolism. The vitamin D receptor also forms RXR heterodimers.
- **SERMs (selective estrogen receptor modulators)** like **tamoxifen** are agonists in bone/endometrium, antagonists in breast — context-dependent recruitment of >20 coactivators/corepressors. Used in ER+ breast cancer.
- **Three second-messenger systems** for peptide and catecholamine hormones:

  **(1) Adenylate cyclase / cAMP** (Fig 11.8, Table 11.4):
  - Hormone binds GPCR → α-subunit of Gₛ dissociates → activates **adenylate cyclase** → ATP → **cAMP + PPi**.
  - cAMP binds the regulatory subunit of **protein kinase A (PKA)** → catalytic subunit dissociates and phosphorylates target enzymes (activating or inactivating them).
  - Phosphodiesterase degrades cAMP, returning the system to baseline.
  - **cGMP** is an analogous second messenger; nitric oxide signaling uses cGMP (penile vasodilation; Viagra inhibits its phosphodiesterase).
  - Used by β-adrenergic, glucagon, FSH, LH, TSH, ACTH, PTH, ADH (V2), and many others.

  **(2) Phospholipase C / IP₃ / DAG / Ca²⁺ / calmodulin** (Fig 11.9, Table 11.5):
  - Hormone binds GPCR → Gq α-subunit dissociates → activates **phospholipase C** at the membrane → cleaves PIP₂ into **IP₃** and **DAG**.
  - **IP₃** diffuses to smooth ER → opens IP₃-receptor Ca²⁺ channels → cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ rises.
  - Ca²⁺ binds **calmodulin** → activates calmodulin-dependent protein kinases → phosphorylate targets.
  - **DAG** activates protein kinase C.
  - Used by α₁-adrenergic, oxytocin, ADH (V1), TRH, GnRH, angiotensin II.
  - **Epinephrine uses both cAMP and Ca²⁺ pathways** (Fig 11.10): β receptors → cAMP → PKA → glycogen phosphorylase; α₁ receptors → IP₃/Ca²⁺ → calmodulin → same glycogenolysis output. Two parallel mechanisms converge on glycogen breakdown.

  **(3) Tyrosine kinase** (Fig 11.11): used by insulin and growth factors. The receptor is *itself* an enzyme.
  - **Insulin receptor** is a preformed homodimer of two half-receptors (disulfide-linked).
  - Insulin binding → conformational change → **autophosphorylation** of the receptor on tyrosine residues → activates the intrinsic tyrosine kinase.
  - The active kinase phosphorylates **insulin receptor substrate (IRS) proteins** → IRS docks signaling molecules → cascade promotes GLUT4 vesicle translocation, glycogen synthesis, lipogenesis, protein synthesis.
  - **Growth factors** (EGF, PDGF, IGF) typically dimerize upon ligand binding to allow cross-phosphorylation between two half-receptors.

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

### Core claims
- **Two embryologic origins** make the pituitary two glands in one:
  - **Adenohypophysis (anterior pituitary)** — grows up from the embryonic mouth (Rathke's pouch); **glandular epithelium**; secretes its own hormones under hypothalamic *hormonal* control.
  - **Neurohypophysis (posterior pituitary)** — grows down from the diencephalon; **neural tissue**; stores and releases hypothalamic hormones via *neural* signals.
- **Anterior pituitary hormones (six)** (Table 11.6, Fig 11.14):
  - **Growth hormone (GH)** — 191 aa protein; stimulates somatic growth via IGF-1 (somatomedin) from liver; targets bone, muscle, adipose.
  - **TSH (thyrotropin)** — glycoprotein; stimulates thyroid follicular cells.
  - **ACTH** — 39 aa peptide; stimulates adrenal cortex (cortisol).
  - **FSH (follicle-stimulating)** — glycoprotein; stimulates gonadal gametogenesis.
  - **LH (luteinizing)** — glycoprotein; stimulates gonadal sex steroid secretion; triggers ovulation.
  - **PRL (prolactin)** — protein; promotes mammary milk synthesis.
  - **Trophic hormones**: those that stimulate growth and secretion of other endocrine glands (TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH).
- **Posterior pituitary hormones (two)** are 9-aa neuropeptides synthesized in hypothalamic cell bodies (**supraoptic** and **paraventricular nuclei**) and transported down axons of the **hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract** to be released into capillaries:
  - **ADH (vasopressin)** — water retention by kidney collecting ducts; vasoconstriction.
  - **Oxytocin** — uterine smooth muscle contraction during labor; mammary myoepithelial contraction for milk ejection.
- **Hypothalamic control of the anterior pituitary** uses a **portal blood system**:
  - Hypothalamic neurons in median eminence release releasing/inhibiting hormones into a primary capillary bed.
  - Portal veins descend the pituitary stalk to a second capillary bed in the anterior pituitary.
  - Anterior pituitary cells are stimulated/inhibited locally without dilution into systemic circulation.
- **Releasing and inhibiting hormones** (Table 11.7):
  - **TRH** (TSH-RH) — also stimulates PRL.
  - **CRH** — stimulates ACTH.
  - **GnRH** — stimulates FSH and LH.
  - **GHRH** — stimulates GH.
  - **GHIH (somatostatin)** — inhibits GH (and many others).
  - **PIH (prolactin-inhibiting hormone)** = **dopamine** — tonically inhibits PRL.
- **Feedback loops:**
  - **Long-loop negative feedback** — target gland hormone (e.g., thyroxine, cortisol, gonadal steroids) inhibits both hypothalamus (releasing hormone) and anterior pituitary (trophic hormone).
  - **Short-loop negative feedback** — anterior pituitary hormone (e.g., TSH) inhibits the hypothalamic releasing hormone (TRH).
  - **Positive feedback** — mid-cycle estradiol → LH surge → ovulation (the sole physiological positive feedback in endocrine regulation, switching back to negative feedback after ovulation).
- **Higher brain influences:** the hypothalamus integrates input from limbic system, amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and olfactory bulb. Emotions, stress, and even smell can perturb the menstrual cycle. Circadian rhythms in GH (sleep) and ACTH/cortisol (peaking just before waking) are driven by suprachiasmatic input.

## Section 11.4 — Adrenal Glands (pp. 336–340)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Functions of the Adrenal Cortex** (pp. 337–338)
- **Functions of the Adrenal Medulla** (pp. 338–339)
- **Stress and the Adrenal Gland** (pp. 339–340)

### Core claims
- Each adrenal gland is two glands stacked: **outer cortex** (mesodermal origin; steroidogenic) and **inner medulla** (neural-crest origin; modified sympathetic ganglion).
- **Adrenal cortex** has three zones (Fig 11.18), each producing a different steroid class via different enzymatic branches from cholesterol (Fig 11.19):
  - **Zona glomerulosa** (outermost) — **mineralocorticoids**, primarily **aldosterone**. Targets kidney distal tubule/collecting duct: ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption, ↑ K⁺ excretion. Regulated by **angiotensin II** (RAAS) and ↑ plasma K⁺, *not* by ACTH.
  - **Zona fasciculata** (middle) — **glucocorticoids**, primarily **cortisol** (hydrocortisone). Regulated by **ACTH** (Fig 11.20).
  - **Zona reticularis** (innermost) — **adrenal androgens**, primarily **DHEA**. Weak androgens that supplement gonadal steroids.
- **Cortisol effects:** ↑ protein breakdown; ↑ gluconeogenesis; ↓ glucose utilization → **stress hyperglycemia**; ↑ lipolysis. Suppresses immune response and inflammation — basis for the medical use of glucocorticoids in asthma, autoimmune disease, organ-transplant immunosuppression.
- **Cushing's syndrome** — chronic glucocorticoid excess: buffalo hump, moon face, central obesity, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, immunosuppression. Causes: (1) iatrogenic (most common; prednisone/prednisolone/dexamethasone); (2) pituitary tumor secreting excess ACTH (Cushing's *disease*); (3) adrenal tumor secreting cortisol autonomously.
- **Addison's disease (adrenal insufficiency)** — inadequate cortisol & aldosterone secretion → hypoglycemia, Na⁺/K⁺ imbalance, dehydration, hypotension. **Primary** = autoimmune destruction of cortex → very high ACTH → skin hyperpigmentation (ACTH stimulates melanocytes). **Secondary** = chronic exogenous glucocorticoids → ACTH suppression → cortex atrophy → adrenal crisis if drug stopped abruptly. **Hence: taper, never stop suddenly.**
- **Adrenal medulla** — innervated by preganglionic sympathetic axons (no postganglionic neuron interposed). Secretes **epinephrine (~85%) + norepinephrine (~15%)** in a 4:1 ratio (per the chapter; varies with state). Effects mirror sympathetic activation but last ~10× longer because hormones must be cleared from blood.
- **Pheochromocytoma** — usually benign catecholamine-secreting tumor of adrenal medulla. Hypertension, tachycardia, sweating, headache. Treated with α + β blockade, then surgery.
- **Selye's general adaptation syndrome (GAS)** (1936): three stages of response to stressors:
  1. **Alarm reaction** — sympathoadrenal "fight or flight."
  2. **Stage of resistance** — CRH → ACTH → glucocorticoid secretion to readjust to chronic stressor.
  3. **Stage of exhaustion** — if readjustment fails; illness, possibly death.
- **Stress and memory:** glucocorticoids can both enhance LTP/memory consolidation during acute stress and impair memory retrieval during chronic stress; they also act on the amygdala in PTSD (PTSD patients sometimes treated with glucocorticoids to suppress retrieval of fearful memories). Chronic stress → glucocorticoid-driven insulin resistance, worsening diabetes.

## Section 11.5 — Thyroid and Parathyroid Glands (pp. 340–344)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Production and Action of Thyroid Hormones** (pp. 341–342)
  - **Diseases of the Thyroid** (pp. 341–343)
- **Parathyroid Glands** (pp. 343–344)

### Core claims
- The **thyroid gland** sits below the larynx as a butterfly with two lobes connected by an isthmus (Fig 11.21). 20–25 g, the largest purely endocrine gland.
- Histologically the thyroid is composed of spherical **follicles** lined by cuboidal **follicular cells** surrounding a lumen filled with **colloid** (a protein-rich gel of thyroglobulin) (Fig 11.22). **Parafollicular cells (C cells)** between follicles secrete **calcitonin**.
- **Thyroid hormone biosynthesis** (Fig 11.23):
  1. Follicular cells actively transport **iodide (I⁻)** from blood into the cell, then into colloid.
  2. **Thyroid peroxidase** oxidizes I⁻ to I and attaches it to tyrosine residues of **thyroglobulin** within the colloid.
  3. One iodine per tyrosine = **monoiodotyrosine (MIT)**; two = **diiodotyrosine (DIT)**.
  4. **DIT + DIT → T4 (tetraiodothyronine, thyroxine)**.
  5. **MIT + DIT → T3 (triiodothyronine)**.
  6. T3 and T4 remain bound to thyroglobulin within the colloid until needed.
  7. On TSH stimulation, follicular cells take up colloid by **pinocytosis**, hydrolyze T3/T4 off thyroglobulin, and release **free T3 and T4** into blood (~9:1 T4:T3 ratio).
- T3 and T4 travel in plasma bound to **thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG)** — 99.96% bound. The small free fraction enters target cells, where T4 is locally converted to the active T3 by deiodinase.
- **T3 effects:** ↑ basal metabolic rate (BMR) via stimulation of mitochondrial respiration in most cells; ↑ protein synthesis; promotes nervous-system maturation. **Critical during late prenatal and first 6 months postnatal life.** Untreated congenital hypothyroidism → **cretinism** (severe intellectual disability + dwarfism); treatable to normal IQ if thyroxine given within first month.
- **Calcitonin** (parafollicular cells) inhibits osteoclasts and ↑ urinary Ca²⁺ excretion → ↓ blood Ca²⁺. Considered to have negligible physiologic importance in normal humans.
- **Endemic goiter** (Fig 11.24, Fig 11.25): dietary iodine deficiency → inadequate T3/T4 → loss of negative feedback → excessive TSH → thyroid hypertrophy. Reversible with iodine supplementation.
- **Hypothyroidism** — lethargy, weight gain, cold intolerance, slow reflexes, depression, dry skin (Table 11.8). Severe adult form: **myxedema** (subcutaneous mucoprotein/fluid accumulation; periorbital edema).
- **Graves' disease** — autoimmune hyperthyroidism. Autoantibodies against the **TSH receptor** mimic TSH stimulation → ↑ T3/T4 + thyroid growth (goiter). TSH itself is *low* (negative feedback). Symptoms: heat intolerance, palpitations, weight loss, nervousness, **exophthalmos** (Fig 11.26). 5–10× more common in women.
- **Parathyroid glands** — four small glands embedded in the posterior surface of the thyroid lobes (two superior, two inferior). Secrete **parathyroid hormone (PTH)** — the dominant regulator of plasma Ca²⁺. PTH (Fig 11.28):
  - Bone: ↑ osteoclast activity → release Ca²⁺ from hydroxyapatite.
  - Kidney: ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption; activate 1α-hydroxylase to make active vitamin D (1,25-(OH)₂-D₃) → ↑ intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption.
  - Net effect: ↑ plasma Ca²⁺. Negative feedback via plasma Ca²⁺.

- > "These follicles are lined with a simple cuboidal epithelium composed of follicular cells that synthesize the principal thyroid hormone, thyroxine." (p. 340)
- > "The thyroid follicles actively accumulate iodide (I−) from the blood and secrete it into the colloid." (p. 341)
- > "Through the activation of genes, thyroid hormones stimulate protein synthesis, promote maturation of the nervous system, and increase the rate of cell respiration in most tissues of the body." (p. 341)
- > "Hypothyroidism during this time may result in cretinism." (p. 342)

- > "Graves' disease is an autoimmune disorder … in which autoantibodies bind to the receptors for TSH on thyroid gland follicular cells." (p. 343)

## Section 11.6 — Pancreas and Other Endocrine Glands (pp. 344–348)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Pancreatic Islets** (pp. 344–346)
- **Pineal Gland** (pp. 346–347)
- **Gastrointestinal Tract** (p. 348)
- **Gonads and Placenta** (p. 348)

### Core claims
- The **pancreas** is ~99% exocrine (acinar cells secreting digestive enzymes into the duodenum). The 1% endocrine portion is the ~1 million **pancreatic islets of Langerhans** scattered through the gland, most densely in the body and tail (Fig 11.29).
- **Three main islet cell types:**
  - **Alpha (α) cells** — ~35–40%; secrete **glucagon**.
  - **Beta (β) cells** — ~50%; secrete **insulin** and amylin.
  - **Delta (δ) cells** — ~10–15%; secrete **somatostatin** (inhibits both insulin and glucagon).
- **Insulin** is synthesized from **preproinsulin → proinsulin → insulin + C-peptide**. The receptor is a tyrosine kinase (Fig 11.11).
- **Insulin action** (Fig 11.30): triggers translocation of intracellular vesicles containing **GLUT4** carriers to the plasma membrane of skeletal muscle and adipose cells → facilitated diffusion of glucose into the cell. Also activates **glycogen synthase** in liver and muscle, promoting glycogen storage. Anabolic effects: ↑ lipogenesis, ↑ protein synthesis.
- **Glucagon** has opposite effects: stimulates hepatic **glycogenolysis** and **gluconeogenesis**, raising blood glucose. Also stimulates lipolysis and ketogenesis during fasting.
- **Glucose homeostasis** (Fig 11.31): the insulin/glucagon antagonism is the master glucose-control loop.
  - **After a meal:** ↑ plasma glucose → β cells release insulin (α cells inhibited) → tissue uptake → ↓ plasma glucose.
  - **During fasting:** ↓ plasma glucose → α cells release glucagon (β cells inhibited) → liver glycogenolysis + gluconeogenesis → ↑ plasma glucose.
- **Diabetes mellitus** — fasting hyperglycemia + glucosuria.
  - **Type 1** (formerly IDDM) — autoimmune destruction of β cells → insulin deficiency. Insulin-dependent.
  - **Type 2** (formerly NIDDM) — insulin resistance; β cells initially compensate by hypersecretion, eventually fail. Most common form.
  - **Gestational** — ~4% of pregnancies; insulin secretion inadequate for fetal demand + placental anti-insulin hormones.
- **Pineal gland** — small (0.2 g, 5–8 mm), cone-shaped; in the roof of the third ventricle. Lacks direct neural input from the rest of the brain but is innervated by sympathetic postganglionic axons from the **superior cervical ganglion**.
- **Melatonin** is synthesized from tryptophan → serotonin → **N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine = melatonin** (Fig 11.32).
- **Circadian regulation** (Fig 11.33): **suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** of the hypothalamus is the master clock; entrained to light by **melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells** via the **retinohypothalamic tract** (Ch 10). Daylight (especially 460–480 nm blue light) inhibits melatonin via SCN → superior cervical ganglion → pineal. Melatonin therefore peaks at night.
- **Melatonin functions:** circadian entrainment; sleep induction; possible role in pubertal timing (high in childhood, drops sharply at puberty); jet lag pills shift the clock; phototherapy treats seasonal affective disorder (SAD).
- **GI tract hormones** (detailed in Ch 18, Table 18.5): gastrin, secretin, CCK, GIP, GLP-1, motilin, ghrelin. Several (incretins like GIP, GLP-1) stimulate insulin release in anticipation of a meal.
- **Gonads** — testes secrete **testosterone** (Leydig cells); ovaries secrete **estradiol-17β** (granulosa cells) during follicular phase and **progesterone** (corpus luteum) during luteal phase.
- **Placenta** — endocrine organ: hCG, somatomammotropin, large amounts of estrogens (estriol is dominant) and progesterone.

- > "The pancreas is mostly (99% by mass) an exocrine gland, but it is also an important endocrine gland." (p. 344)
- > "A human islet contains about 50% beta cells, 35% to 40% alpha cells, and 10% to 15% delta cells, which secrete the hormone somatostatin." (p. 344)
- > "Insulin then binds to its receptors in the plasma membrane of its target cells … causes intracellular vesicles containing GLUT4 carrier proteins to translocate to the plasma membrane." (pp. 344–345)
- > "Insulin thereby causes glucose to leave the plasma and enter the target cells, where it is converted into the energy storage molecules of glycogen (in skeletal muscles and liver) and fat (in adipose tissue)." (p. 345)

## Section 11.7 — Paracrine and Autocrine Regulation (pp. 348–352)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Examples of Paracrine and Autocrine Regulation** (pp. 349–350)
- **Prostaglandins** (pp. 350–352)
  - **Examples of Prostaglandin Actions** (pp. 350–351)
  - **Inhibitors of Prostaglandin Synthesis** (pp. 351–352)

### Core claims
- **Paracrine regulators** — locally acting chemicals that affect neighboring cells.
- **Autocrine regulators** — act on the same cell type that produces them.
- Distinguished from hormones (which travel in blood) and neurotransmitters (which cross a narrow synaptic cleft) only by *function*, not by molecular structure (e.g., norepinephrine fits all categories).
- **Cytokines** = secreted regulators that act on immune cells; produced by macrophages, lymphocytes (the latter called **lymphokines**, with specific examples called **interleukins**). **Adipokines** from adipose, **myokines** from muscle. Many cytokines are also growth factors.
- **Growth factors** — PDGF (platelet-derived), EGF (epidermal), IGF (insulin-like / somatomedins), neurotrophins (e.g., NGF).
- **Vascular endothelial paracrine regulators:**
  - **Nitric oxide (NO)** — vasodilator; also neurotransmitter; produced by endothelium and macrophages.
  - **Endothelins** (specifically endothelin-1) — vasoconstrictors.
  - **Bradykinin** — vasodilator.
  - These regulate blood flow, pressure, and atherosclerosis development.
- **Prostaglandins** are 20-carbon **eicosanoids** with a 5-membered carbon ring. All eicosanoids derive from **arachidonic acid** released from plasma-membrane phospholipids on stimulation (Fig 11.34):
  - **Cyclooxygenase (COX)** pathway → PGG₂ → PGH₂ → PGI₂ (prostacyclin), PGE₂, PGF₂α, TXA₂ (thromboxane).
  - **Lipoxygenase (LOX)** pathway → leukotrienes.
- **Antagonistic prostaglandin actions** within a single physiological domain — illustrated by the clotting balance:
  - **Platelet thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂)** — promotes platelet aggregation and vasoconstriction (prothrombotic).
  - **Endothelial prostacyclin (PGI₂)** — inhibits platelet aggregation and causes vasodilation (antithrombotic).
  - This antagonism keeps clotting localized to injury sites.
- **Prostaglandin roles by system:** immune (inflammation, fever, pain); reproductive (ovulation, uterine contraction); GI (inhibit gastric acid secretion); respiratory (bronchoconstriction by PGF₂α and leukotrienes — asthma); circulatory (clotting, blood pressure, fetal ductus arteriosus patency); urinary (renal vasodilation, ↑ urine output).
- **COX isoenzymes:**
  - **COX-1** — constitutive in gastric mucosa, kidneys, platelets. Produces protective prostaglandins.
  - **COX-2** — induced by cytokines in inflammation.
  - **COX-3** — found in brain; target of acetaminophen (Tylenol).
- **NSAIDs (aspirin, indomethacin, ibuprofen)** inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2 → reduce inflammation but cause GI bleeding (loss of gastric PGI₂/PGE₂ protection), kidney issues, and prolonged clotting time.
- **COX-2-selective inhibitors** (celecoxib, formerly rofecoxib/Vioxx) preserve gastric mucosa but tilt the platelet-endothelium balance toward clotting (because endothelial PGI₂ is reduced but platelet TXA₂ is preserved) → increased MI and thrombotic stroke risk. Many withdrawn from market.
- **Aspirin's antithrombotic benefit:** low-dose ("baby aspirin" 81 mg) **irreversibly inhibits platelet COX-1**, blocking TXA₂ synthesis for the platelet lifespan. Endothelial cells (nucleated, can replace COX) recover quickly. Net antithrombotic effect.
- **Anti-leukotriene drugs** (Zyflo, Singulair) — inhibit 5-LOX or block LT receptors; used for asthma.

Arachidonic acid is released from membrane phospholipids and channeled into two branches: **lipoxygenase** → leukotrienes (bronchoconstriction, vasoconstriction, capillary permeability, inflammation); **cyclooxygenase** → PGG₂ → PGH₂ → branched products **PGI₂** (antiplatelet, vasodilation), **PGE₂** (smooth muscle relaxation, vasodilation), **PGF₂α** (smooth muscle contraction, vasoconstriction), **TXA₂** (platelet aggregation, vasoconstriction).

## Clinical apparatus

- **Clinical Investigation: Rachel** — chapter-opening case. Rachel takes **prednisolone** for arthritis, then abruptly stops it. Develops puffiness (Cushingoid features from the prednisolone), then on stopping has insomnia, heat intolerance, sweating, tachycardia, hypertension, bulging eyes, and low blood glucose. ## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Hormone** — regulatory molecule secreted into blood by an endocrine gland.
- **Endocrine gland** — ductless gland secreting hormones into blood.
- **Target cell** — cell bearing specific receptors for a hormone.
- **Amine / polypeptide / glycoprotein / steroid** — the four chemical classes of hormones.
- **Lipophilic hormone** — steroid or thyroid hormone; crosses plasma membrane.
- **Polar hormone** — peptide/glycoprotein/catecholamine; bound at cell surface.
- **Prohormone** — inactive precursor processed within endocrine cell (e.g., proinsulin).
- **Prehormone** — secreted inactive form requiring target-cell conversion (T4 → T3; vitamin D₃ → 1,25-(OH)₂-D₃; testosterone → DHT).
- **Synergistic / permissive / antagonistic** — categories of hormone interaction.
- **Half-life** — time for plasma concentration to fall by 50%.
- **Pulsatile secretion** — bursts that avoid receptor desensitization.
- **Upregulation / downregulation** — receptor number rise/fall in response to ligand exposure.
- **Nuclear receptor** — intracellular receptor with ligand-binding and DNA-binding domains.
- **Hormone response element** — short DNA sequence (two half-sites + spacer) bound by activated nuclear receptor dimers.
- **Homodimer / heterodimer** — two identical (steroid receptor) or two different (TR + RXR) receptors on the DNA half-sites.
- **Heat shock protein** — chaperone that masks nuclear receptors until ligand binds.
- **Coactivator / corepressor** — regulatory proteins that promote or block transcription.
- **SERM** — selective estrogen receptor modulator (e.g., tamoxifen).
- **Adenylate cyclase / cAMP** — second-messenger system used by β-adrenergic, glucagon, PTH, ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH, ADH(V2).
- **Protein kinase A (PKA)** — cAMP-activated kinase that phosphorylates substrates.
- **Phosphodiesterase** — degrades cAMP/cGMP.
- **Phospholipase C / IP₃ / DAG / Ca²⁺ / calmodulin** — second-messenger system used by α₁-adrenergic, oxytocin, ADH(V1), TRH, GnRH.
- **Tyrosine kinase receptor** — receptor with intrinsic kinase activity (insulin; growth factor receptors).
- **GLUT4** — insulin-responsive glucose transporter in skeletal muscle and adipose.
- **Pituitary gland / adenohypophysis / neurohypophysis** — anterior (glandular) and posterior (neural) lobes.
- **Hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system** — venous link from median eminence to anterior pituitary; delivers releasing/inhibiting hormones.
- **Hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract** — axonal pathway from supraoptic/paraventricular hypothalamic nuclei to posterior pituitary.
- **ADH (vasopressin) / oxytocin** — posterior pituitary 9-aa peptides.
- **GH / TSH / ACTH / FSH / LH / PRL** — anterior pituitary hormones.
- **TRH / CRH / GnRH / GHRH / somatostatin / dopamine (PIH)** — hypothalamic regulators.
- **Trophic hormone** — promotes growth and secretion of another endocrine gland.
- **Long-loop / short-loop feedback** — target gland hormone on hypothalamus + pituitary / pituitary hormone on hypothalamus.
- **Adrenal cortex zones** — glomerulosa (aldosterone), fasciculata (cortisol), reticularis (DHEA).
- **Adrenal medulla** — modified sympathetic ganglion; secretes epinephrine, norepinephrine.
- **Sympathoadrenal system** — sympathetic nerves + adrenal medulla acting together.
- **Cortisol** — main human glucocorticoid; ↑ gluconeogenesis, lipolysis, protein breakdown, anti-inflammation, immunosuppression.
- **Aldosterone** — main mineralocorticoid; ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption, ↑ K⁺ excretion.
- **Cushing's syndrome** — chronic glucocorticoid excess.
- **Addison's disease** — adrenal insufficiency.
- **Pheochromocytoma** — catecholamine-secreting adrenal medullary tumor.
- **General adaptation syndrome (GAS)** — Selye's three stages (alarm, resistance, exhaustion).
- **Thyroid follicle / colloid / thyroglobulin** — storage architecture for T3/T4.
- **MIT / DIT** — mono- / diiodotyrosine intermediates.
- **TBG (thyroxine-binding globulin)** — main plasma carrier for thyroid hormones.
- **BMR (basal metabolic rate)** — resting calorie expenditure; raised by T3.
- **Cretinism** — untreated congenital hypothyroidism; severe intellectual disability + dwarfism.
- **Myxedema** — severe adult hypothyroidism with subcutaneous mucoprotein edema.
- **Graves' disease** — autoimmune hyperthyroidism; anti-TSH-receptor antibodies; exophthalmos.
- **Endemic goiter** — iodine-deficiency-driven thyroid hypertrophy.
- **Calcitonin** — parafollicular C-cell hormone; minor role in human Ca²⁺ regulation.
- **PTH (parathyroid hormone)** — main regulator of plasma Ca²⁺ (bone, kidney, intestine).
- **Pancreatic islet** — endocrine cluster within pancreas; α, β, δ cells.
- **Insulin / glucagon / somatostatin** — β / α / δ cell hormones.
- **Diabetes mellitus types 1 / 2 / gestational** — insulin lack / insulin resistance / pregnancy-onset.
- **Pineal gland / melatonin** — circadian rhythm hormone; tryptophan-derived.
- **Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** — hypothalamic master circadian clock.
- **hCG / somatomammotropin** — placental peptide hormones.
- **Estradiol-17β / progesterone / testosterone** — main gonadal steroids.
- **Paracrine / autocrine regulator** — local-acting / self-acting non-blood-borne signaling molecule.
- **Cytokine / interleukin / lymphokine / adipokine / myokine** — paracrine regulators by tissue source.
- **Growth factor** — proliferation-stimulating regulator (PDGF, EGF, IGF).
- **Prostaglandin / leukotriene / thromboxane** — 20-carbon eicosanoid mediators from arachidonic acid.
- **COX-1 / COX-2 / COX-3** — cyclooxygenase isoenzymes; constitutive / inflammation-induced / brain.
- **NSAID** — nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug; COX inhibitor.
