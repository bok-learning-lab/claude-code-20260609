## Section 8.1 — Structural Organization of the Brain (pp. 205–207)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
*(no formal subsection breaks; section flows directly)*

### Core claims
- **CNS** comprises the brain and spinal cord. Embryologically derived from the dorsal **neural tube** (becomes CNS) and adjacent **neural crest** (becomes PNS ganglia and several other structures), both from the ectoderm.
- **Three primary brain vesicles** (week 4) → **five secondary vesicles** (week 5):
  - Forebrain (prosencephalon) → **telencephalon** + **diencephalon**.
  - Midbrain (mesencephalon) → **mesencephalon** (unchanged).
  - Hindbrain (rhombencephalon) → **metencephalon** + **myelencephalon**.
- **Adult derivatives:** telencephalon → cerebral hemispheres + lateral ventricles; diencephalon → thalamus + hypothalamus + third ventricle; mesencephalon → midbrain + cerebral aqueduct; metencephalon → pons + cerebellum + upper fourth ventricle; myelencephalon → medulla oblongata + lower fourth ventricle. The spinal cord continues from the myelencephalon and contains the central canal.
- **CSF** is produced in each ventricle by the **choroid plexus** (vascular epithelium with tight junctions), at ~500 mL/day. Total CSF volume 140–200 mL; normal intracranial pressure 5–15 mmHg. Flow: lateral ventricles → interventricular foramina → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct → fourth ventricle → central canal and subarachnoid space → arachnoid villi and granulations → dural venous sinuses → blood. CSF is part of the blood-brain barrier (choroid epithelial tight junctions exclude paracellular transport).
- **Gray vs. white matter:** gray = cell bodies and dendrites (cortex; nuclei deep in brain; central spinal cord); white = myelinated axon tracts.
- The adult brain holds ~10¹¹ neurons, weighs ~1.5 kg (~3–3.5 lb), and receives ~15% of resting cardiac output — driven by metabolism, not cooling (the chapter explicitly rebuts Aristotle's view).
- **Adult neurogenesis** occurs in two regions:
  - **Subventricular zone** (adjacent to lateral ventricular ependyma) — new interneurons migrate to the striatum in humans (to olfactory bulbs in non-human mammals).
  - **Subgranular zone of the hippocampus** — robust neurogenesis throughout adulthood in rodents and demonstrated in human adults; the dentate-gyrus interneurons contribute to learning and memory.

### Core claims
- The cerebrum (~80% of brain mass) is the telencephalon, two hemispheres connected by the **corpus callosum** (~200 million fibers).
- **Cerebral cortex** = 2–4 mm of gray matter folded into **gyri** (ridges), **sulci** (grooves), and deeper **fissures**. Five lobes per hemisphere: **frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital, insula** (deep, covered by other lobes). Modern noninvasive imaging has identified 180 separate cortical compartments per hemisphere.
- **Frontal lobe** — anterior to the **central sulcus**; contains the **precentral gyrus** = primary motor cortex (upper motor neurons; Ch 12). Includes prefrontal cortex (higher functions) and motor speech (Broca's) area.
- **Parietal lobe** — posterior to central sulcus; **postcentral gyrus** = primary somatosensory cortex (cutaneous, joint, tendon, muscle sensation; Ch 10).
- **Sensory and motor homunculi** (Fig 8.7): cortical territory is proportional to receptor density and motor innervation density, not body size — face and hands are vastly over-represented.
- **Temporal lobe** — auditory and language; interprets and stores auditory and visual experiences.
- **Occipital lobe** — primary visual cortex and eye-movement coordination.
- **Insula** — integrates sensory info with visceral responses; receives olfactory, gustatory, auditory, somatosensory (pain) input; implicated in interoception and the bodily-state component of emotion; also activated during empathic responses.
- **Mirror neurons** — in frontal and parietal lobes; fire both when performing a goal-directed action and when observing the same action in another (originally in macaques; demonstrated in humans by fMRI). Implicated in imitation, theory of mind, empathy, and possibly **autism spectrum disorder** (impaired mirror-neuron function hypothesis).
- **Visualizing the brain (Table 8.2):**
  - **CT** — X-ray computed tomography (density-based slices).
  - **PET** — positron emission tomography; radioisotope-labeled tracer (often fluorodeoxyglucose); detects local metabolism and blood flow.
  - **SPECT** — single photon emission CT; like PET but with single-photon isotopes.
  - **MRI** — magnetic resonance imaging; aligns water protons with an external magnet; distinguishes gray vs. white matter and CSF; contrast agents available.
  - **fMRI** — functional MRI; the BOLD response (blood oxygenation level dependent contrast). Active regions get more oxyhemoglobin → measurable signal change.
  - **MEG** — magnetoencephalography; SQUIDS sensing the magnetic fields of postsynaptic currents; millisecond temporal resolution; used in epilepsy/tumor surgical planning.
  - **EEG** — scalp electrodes measure summed postsynaptic potentials, action potentials, and glial currents.
- **EEG bands:**
  - **Alpha** — 10–12 Hz; relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed; parietal/occipital. (Child alpha 4–7 Hz.)
  - **Beta** — 13–25 Hz; frontal lobes; mental activity, evoked by visual stimuli.
  - **Theta** — 5–8 Hz; temporal/occipital; newborns, sleep, attention/memory tasks in adults; emotional stress.
  - **Delta** — 1–5 Hz; high amplitude; deep sleep; infants. Pathological in awake adults (brain damage).
- **Sleep:** two categories — **non-REM** (4 stages, including slow-wave with delta) and **REM** (theta-dominated EEG, vivid dreams, irregular breathing/HR; limbic system and amygdala active). Cycles ~90 min, ~5 per night. Slow-wave dominates first half, REM dominates second half.
  - **Consolidation:** non-REM benefits declarative (especially spatial) memories; REM benefits nondeclarative; both stages and natural cycling are needed for optimal consolidation. Memory consolidation improves after a nap; greater after a full sleep; shorter delays between learning and sleep work better.
  - **Glymphatic system** (2012): brain's waste-clearance system, primarily active during deep sleep. Aging compromises both deep sleep and glymphatic function — relevant to Alzheimer's risk.
- **Basal nuclei** (more anatomically correct than "basal ganglia"): subcortical gray-matter masses in the cerebrum involved in voluntary motor control. The **corpus striatum** = **caudate nucleus** + **lentiform nucleus** (**putamen** + **globus pallidus**); also the subthalamic nucleus and the substantia nigra (midbrain) are often included.
- **Motor circuit** (Fig 8.12): cortex (glutamate, excitatory) → putamen (GABA, inhibitory) → globus pallidus + substantia nigra (GABA, inhibitory) → thalamus → motor cortex (excitatory). Substantia nigra also supplies dopamine (excitatory) directly to the striatum (the nigrostriatal pathway). The circuit "allows intended movements to occur while inhibiting unintended movements."
- **Huntington disease** (Clinical Application): autosomal dominant CAG-repeat expansion in **HTT** gene on chromosome 4. Glutamine aggregates in caudate and putamen → death of GABAergic neurons → loss of inhibition → **chorea** (rapid involuntary movements), **athetosis** (writhing of hands), expansion of lateral ventricles, eventual cortical neuron loss; psychiatric symptoms; decreased ACh.
- **Parkinson's disease** (Clinical Application): degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra → loss of nigrostriatal dopamine → dyskinesia, muscle tremor and rigidity, slow movement (bradykinesia), speech impairment. Some cases have **α-synuclein** mutations forming **Lewy bodies**. Treatment: L-dopa (Levodopa), dopamine agonists, MAO inhibitors; deep brain stimulation of subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus.
- **Cerebral lateralization** (Fig 8.13): each hemisphere controls contralateral motor and receives contralateral somatosensory and visual hemifield input. Hemispheres communicate via the corpus callosum.
  - **Split-brain studies** — corpus callosum surgically cut for severe epilepsy; each hemisphere can be tested independently.
  - **Left hemisphere** specializes in language and analytical ability in 97% of all people (all right-handers, 70% of left-handers).
  - **Right hemisphere** specializes in visuospatial tasks (face recognition, navigation, map reading, block arrangement) and musical composition.
  - Cerebral **lateralization** is now preferred to **dominance** (complementary specialization, not hierarchy).
- **Language** (Fig 8.14): four key regions, mainly in the left hemisphere:
  - **Broca's area** (motor speech area; inferior frontal gyrus) — articulation. Damage → **nonfluent (Broca's) aphasia**: slow, labored, poorly articulated speech; **comprehension intact**; right-arm weakness common.
  - **Wernicke's area** (superior temporal gyrus) — language comprehension. Damage → **fluent (Wernicke's) aphasia**: fluent but meaningless "word salad"; **comprehension destroyed**; patient unaware of deficit.
  - **Arcuate fasciculus** — connects Wernicke's to Broca's. Damage → **conduction aphasia**: fluent speech, intact comprehension, but poor repetition; patient aware of deficit.
  - **Angular gyrus** — integrates auditory, visual, somatosensory information; projects to Wernicke's. Damage → aphasias involving reading/writing.
  - Recovery: faster in children (language can transfer to right hemisphere), reduced after adolescence; Wernicke's damage gives more severe and permanent aphasia than Broca's.
- **Limbic system** (Fig 8.15): a ring of forebrain nuclei and tracts around the brain stem (limbus = ring). Components: **cingulate gyrus**, **amygdaloid body**, **hippocampus**, **septal nuclei**, plus the olfactory bulb/tract (historical "rhinencephalon" / "smell brain"). Older than the cortex evolutionarily — explains limited conscious control of emotion.
  - **Papez circuit:** hippocampus → fornix → mammillary bodies (hypothalamus) → anterior thalamic nuclei → cingulate gyrus → back to hippocampus.
  - Functions (electrical stimulation / ablation): aggression (amygdala, hypothalamus); fear (amygdala — patient "S.M." with bilateral amygdala calcification showed no fear); feeding (hypothalamic feeding and satiety centers); sex; reward and punishment (electrodes between frontal cortex and hypothalamus deliver self-stimulation reward in rats more powerful than food or sex).
- **Memory categories:**
  - **Short-term vs. long-term:** short-term is electrical/reverberating; long-term requires new RNA, protein synthesis, new synapses (memory consolidation). ECT and head trauma disrupt recent (short-term) memory but spare older long-term memory.
  - **Nondeclarative (implicit)** — skills, conditioning (basal nuclei, cerebellum, supplementary motor areas).
  - **Declarative (explicit)** — verbalizable. Subdivides into **semantic** (facts; inferior temporal lobes) and **episodic** (events; medial temporal lobes, thalamus, fornix, prefrontal cortex).
  - **Working memory** — short-term retention (seconds–minutes); requires prefrontal cortex (verbal — Broca, Wernicke; spatial — prefrontal + visual association).
- **Hippocampus and medial temporal lobes** are required for short-term → long-term declarative memory consolidation:
  - Famous case **H.M.** — bilateral medial temporal lobectomy → preserved memory of pre-surgical events and nondeclarative skills, but inability to form any new declarative memories.
  - Famous case **E.P.** — bilateral medial temporal damage; remembered 50-year-old neighborhood, not current one.
  - 2014 Nobel: **place cells** in the hippocampus; "cognitive maps" with orbitofrontal cortex.
  - Hippocampus is shrunken in amnesic patients on MRI; PTSD subjects have hippocampal atrophy.
  - Sleep is required for optimal consolidation; old memories are erased from the hippocampus during sleep after being transferred to cortex.
- **Cerebral cortex** stores long-term factual information; verbal memories lateralized left, visuospatial right. Inferior temporal lobes store long-term visual memories. **Prefrontal cortex** — working memory, planning, judgment, inhibition of inappropriate actions; does not fully mature until after young adulthood (risky-behavior implication).
- **Alzheimer's disease** — most common dementia; > 1/3 incidence over age 85. Pathology:
  - Loss of brain weight and cortical thickness; loss of specific neurons (hippocampus, cortex); loss of dendritic spines and synapses.
  - **Amyloid senile plaques** (extracellular): amyloid precursor protein (APP) is cleaved by β-secretase and γ-secretase into **amyloid beta (Aβ)** peptides. The soluble Aβ dimers/oligomers (42-aa form) are likely more toxic than the mature plaques.
  - **Neurofibrillary tangles** (intracellular): hyperphosphorylated **tau** protein aggregates. Soluble intermediate tau forms appear most toxic.
  - Excitotoxicity, mitochondrial ROS, microglial inflammation amplify damage.
  - **APOE4** allele increases risk of late-onset (sporadic) Alzheimer's 2–4× (one copy) or substantially more (two copies); recent evidence implicates a combination of APOE4 + herpes simplex virus exposure.
  - **Early-onset** (<1% of cases) — mutations in APP gene or presenilin (γ-secretase) genes.
  - Drugs: AChE inhibitors; glutamate antagonist (memantine); SSRIs/anxiolytics; cautious antipsychotic use. Tau- and microglia-targeting drugs are in development. Lifestyle: cognitive and physical activity, social engagement, low-cholesterol diet.
- **Synaptic mechanisms of memory** (Fig 8.16):
  - **LTP** — glutamate binds **AMPA** (Na⁺ EPSP) → membrane depolarizes → Mg²⁺ leaves **NMDA** pore → glutamate (+ glycine or D-serine coagonist) opens NMDA → Ca²⁺ enters → binds **calmodulin** → activates **CaMKII** → inserts more AMPA receptors at the synapse and increases AMPA conductance. Persistent change involves nuclear Ca²⁺, **CREB**, new mRNA and protein synthesis, and histone acetylation (epigenetic).
  - **Dendritic spines** (Fig 8.17): on pyramidal cells of cortex, hippocampus, amygdala. LTP grows spines; LTD shrinks/eliminates them. Spines that grow are selectively stabilized by subsequent training.
  - **Retrograde messengers** — NO and endocannabinoid 2-AG from postsynaptic neuron to presynaptic terminal; can increase glutamate release (NO) or suppress GABA release (2-AG; **depolarization-induced suppression of inhibition** = DSI).
- **Neural stem cells in learning and memory:** subgranular zone neurogenesis in dentate nucleus of hippocampus stimulated by exercise and enriched environment; correlates with spatial learning in rodents.
- **Emotion and memory:**
  - The **amygdaloid body** enhances emotional memory; bilateral amygdala damage abolishes the emotion-enhancement of memory.
  - **Stress** activates cortisol (Ch 11), which targets hippocampus and amygdala (both rich in steroid receptors) — high stress impairs hippocampal consolidation and prefrontal working memory; PTSD shows hippocampal atrophy. Corticosteroids suppress hippocampal neurogenesis.
- **Prefrontal cortex** (Fig 8.18):
  - **Orbitofrontal area** — pleasure and reward; interfaces with limbic system (orbitofrontal + amygdala + cingulate gyrus = emotional reward triangle for goal-directed behavior). Lesions cause impulsive, sociopathic-leaning behavior with preserved memory and cognition. Classic case **Phineas Gage** (1848) — 1.25-inch tamping rod through left frontal lobe; recovery with profound personality change.
  - **Lateral prefrontal area** — cognitive function and motivation. Lesions cause motivational and cognitive deficits.

#### Figure 8.12 — The motor circuit *(viewed)*

A frontal (coronal) section of the deep brain with the basal-nuclei motor circuit overlaid as colored arrows. A three-line legend at upper left codes the neurotransmitters: **green = glutamate (excitatory)**, **blue = dopamine (excitatory)**, **red = GABA (inhibitory)**. Labels down the right side identify, from superior to inferior, the **caudate nucleus**, **putamen**, **thalamus**, **globus pallidus**, **subthalamic nucleus**, and (at the base of the section) **substantia nigra**. The arrows form a closed loop: excitatory green projections descend from the cortical motor areas into the striatum (caudate + putamen); a dense tangle of **red GABAergic arrows radiates from the globus pallidus** to multiple downstream targets (the visually dominant feature of the figure — the globus pallidus is the circuit's main inhibitory hub); and **blue dopaminergic arrows ascend from the substantia nigra** to the striatum (the nigrostriatal pathway). The thalamus sits medially and relays back up toward the cortex, closing the cortico-striato-pallido-thalamo-cortical loop.

> Vision note: The visual takeaway is that the globus pallidus is the inhibitory (red) center of gravity and the substantia nigra is the lone dopaminergic (blue) input — exactly the node that degenerates in Parkinson's. ## Section 8.3 — Diencephalon (pp. 224–226)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Thalamus and Epithalamus** (p. 224)
- **Hypothalamus and Pituitary Gland** (pp. 224–226)
  - **Regulation of the Autonomic System** (pp. 224–225)
  - **Regulation of the Pituitary Gland** (p. 225)
  - **Regulation of Circadian Rhythms** (pp. 225–226)

### Core claims
- The **diencephalon** is the part of the forebrain that contains the **epithalamus, thalamus, hypothalamus**, and part of the **pituitary gland**. It forms the walls of the third ventricle and is almost completely covered by the cerebrum.
- **Thalamus** = ~80% of the diencephalon; paired gray-matter masses; relay center for nearly all sensory pathways (except smell) on their way to the cortex.
  - **Lateral geniculate nuclei** relay visual to occipital cortex.
  - **Medial geniculate nuclei** relay auditory to temporal cortex.
  - **Intralaminar nuclei** integrate sensory modalities and project widely; basis of arousal-from-sleep response to strong stimuli.
- **Epithalamus** — dorsal segment of diencephalon; contains a **choroid plexus** (third ventricle) and the **pineal gland** (secretes **melatonin**, a circadian-rhythm regulator; Ch 11).
- **Hypothalamus** — inferior diencephalon below the thalamus; floor of the third ventricle. Functions:
  - Hunger and satiety centers (lateral vs. medial hypothalamus).
  - Body temperature **thermostat** (preoptic area cooling → shivering and nonshivering thermogenesis; heating → vasodilation, sweating, hyperventilation).
  - Osmoreceptors → thirst and ADH release.
  - Sleep, wakefulness, sexual arousal.
  - Emotion (working with the limbic system; visceral component via medulla).
  - Endocrine control (next bullets).
- **Hypothalamic control of posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis):** neurons in **supraoptic** and **paraventricular** nuclei produce **ADH (vasopressin)** and **oxytocin**; axons travel via the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract to the posterior pituitary where the hormones are stored and released into blood. ADH → kidney water retention; oxytocin → uterine contraction during labor (Ch 11, 17, 20).
- **Hypothalamic control of anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis):** hypothalamic **releasing** and **inhibiting hormones** travel via blood (hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system; Ch 11) to regulate anterior pituitary hormone secretion → downstream endocrine glands.
- **Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** — anterior hypothalamus, ~20,000 "clock cells" with intrinsic ~24-h oscillation. **Master regulator** of circadian rhythms (metabolism, sleep, temperature, blood pressure, hormones).
  - **Retinohypothalamic tract** from photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (using **melanopsin**, not rods/cones) entrains the SCN to environmental light/dark.
  - 2017 Nobel: discovery of circadian clock genes (in fruit flies; later in SCN and many peripheral organs).
  - SCN entrains peripheral "clock genes" in liver and other organs via autonomic nerves.
  - SCN controls pineal melatonin secretion via sympathetic nerves (Ch 11) — melatonin is highest at night and influences insulin secretion and blood pressure rhythms.

- > "The thalamus acts primarily as a relay center through which all sensory information (except smell) passes on the way to the cerebrum." (p. 224)
- > "This small but extremely important brain region contains neural centers for hunger and thirst, the regulation of body temperature, and hormone secretion from the pituitary gland." (p. 224)
- > "Neurons within the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus produce two hormones — antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which is also known as vasopressin, and oxytocin." (p. 225)
- > "Within the anterior hypothalamus are bilaterally located suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN). These nuclei contain about 20,000 neurons that function as 'clock cells.'" (p. 225)
- > "The scientists who discovered circadian clock genes in fruit flies, and the molecular mechanisms required for circadian rhythms, won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine." (p. 226)

## Section 8.4 — Midbrain and Hindbrain (pp. 227–230)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Midbrain** (pp. 227–228)
- **Hindbrain** (pp. 228–229)
  - **Metencephalon** (pp. 228–229)
  - **Myelencephalon** (p. 229)
- **Reticular Activating System in Sleep and Arousal** (p. 230)

### Core claims
- **Midbrain (mesencephalon):**
  - **Corpora quadrigemina** = four rounded elevations: **superior colliculi** (visual reflexes); **inferior colliculi** (auditory relay).
  - **Cerebral peduncles** — ascending and descending tracts.
  - **Red nucleus** — coordination link between cortex and cerebellum.
  - **Substantia nigra** — dark-pigmented (melanin) midbrain nucleus; source of the **nigrostriatal dopamine system** for motor coordination.
  - **Mesolimbic dopamine system** — from substantia nigra and **ventral tegmental area** to limbic targets, including the **nucleus accumbens** (in ventral striatum), prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hippocampus. Reward, motivation, addiction.
- **Reward and addiction** (Clinical Application):
  - Nicotine stimulates midbrain dopaminergic neurons via nicotinic ACh receptors; chronic exposure desensitizes them (tolerance, dependence).
  - Opioids and cannabinoids reduce GABA inhibition of midbrain dopamine neurons.
  - Cocaine and amphetamines inhibit dopamine reuptake at the nucleus accumbens.
  - Chronic exposure downregulates D2 receptors → escalating doses needed.
  - Relapse from withdrawal involves stress circuits in the amygdala and failure of prefrontal control over the nucleus accumbens.
  - Alcohol stimulates mesolimbic dopamine and acts as CNS depressant (GABA release; NMDA inhibition).
- **Hindbrain — metencephalon:**
  - **Pons** — surface fibers to cerebellum; deep fibers in motor/sensory tracts; nuclei of cranial nerves V (trigeminal), VI (abducens), VII (facial), VIII (vestibulocochlear); **apneustic** and **pneumotaxic** respiratory centers (Ch 16). Ventral pons damage → **locked-in syndrome**.
  - **Cerebellum** — two hemispheres; outer gray cortex (contains more neurons than the rest of the brain combined) and inner white matter with nuclei. Coordinates smooth goal-directed movements, posture and balance, motor learning, and contributes to autonomic and cognitive functions. **Purkinje cells** carry cerebellar output (GABAergic, inhibitory) to deep cerebellar nuclei, which then project to motor cortex (via thalamus), pons, medulla, spinal cord. Disorders cause **ataxia** — uncoordinated gait, balance issues, eye-movement defects, **intention tremor**. Causes include trauma, stroke, TIA, viruses (often self-resolving), and alcohol intoxication.
- **Hindbrain — myelencephalon (medulla oblongata):**
  - ~3 cm long, continuous with pons above and spinal cord below.
  - All ascending and descending tracts pass through it; most decussate in the medullary **pyramids** (hence "pyramidal tracts").
  - Cranial-nerve nuclei for VIII, IX, X (vagus), XI, XII.
  - **Vital centers:** vasomotor (autonomic control of vessels), cardiac control (autonomic control of heart), respiratory (with pontine centers).
- **Reticular activating system (RAS)** — interconnected neurons of the reticular formation from pons through midbrain.
  - **Cholinergic** RAS neurons project to the thalamus → enhance sensory relay to cortex.
  - **Monoaminergic** RAS neurons (dopamine, NE, histamine, serotonin) in hypothalamus and basal forebrain project to cortex → cortical arousal.
  - **GABAergic** neurons in the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) of the hypothalamus inhibit RAS arousal → sleep. VLPO and monoamine RAS neurons mutually inhibit each other → a "switch" between waking and sleeping.
  - **Orexin (hypocretin)** neurons in the lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) promote wakefulness; ~70,000 neurons; autoimmune destruction → **narcolepsy** (~1/2,000). Orexin also raises metabolic rate, blood pressure, and HR via the sympathoadrenal system, and increases food craving and physical activity.
- **Hypnotic drugs** (Clinical Application): Benadryl (antihistamine, inhibits RAS histamine arousal); barbiturates, anesthetic gases, benzodiazepines (potentiate GABA → ↑ inhibition of RAS); Ambien-like non-benzodiazepines (same mechanism, different chemistry); amphetamines (block dopamine reuptake → ↑ arousal); caffeine (antagonizes adenosine A1 receptor → less sleep drive).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The nigrostriatal system projects from the substantia nigra to the corpus striatum of the basal nuclei; this system is required for motor coordination, and it is the degeneration of these fibers that produces Parkinson's disease." (p. 227)
- > "The rewarding effects of many addictive drugs are believed to be due to a rapid increase of dopamine released in the nucleus accumbens. This is the 'dopamine hypothesis' of addiction." (p. 228)
- > "The cerebellum … is required for movements that are smooth and goal-directed, such as touching your finger to your nose, bringing a fork of food to your mouth, or finding keys by touch in your pocket or purse." (p. 228)
- > "The medulla contains groupings of neurons required for the regulation of breathing and of cardiovascular responses; hence, they are known as the vital centers." (p. 229)
- > "The activity of VLPO and other GABA-releasing neurons is increased with the depth of sleep, and these neurons are believed to both cause and stabilize sleep." (p. 230)
- > "People with narcolepsy have a loss of LHA neurons that release a particular polypeptide neurotransmitter that promotes wakefulness. This neurotransmitter is known as orexin (also called hypocretin)." (p. 230)

## Section 8.5 — Spinal Cord Tracts (pp. 231–234)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Ascending Tracts** (pp. 231–232)
- **Descending Tracts** (pp. 232–234)

### Core claims
- The spinal cord extends from the foramen magnum to the L1 vertebra. Unlike the brain, gray matter is **central** (H-shaped) and white matter is **peripheral** (six **funiculi** of columns). The H has dorsal (posterior) and ventral (anterior) horns.
- **Tract naming:** ascending (sensory) tracts use a `spino-` prefix and end with the brain region of synapse (e.g., spinothalamic). Descending (motor) tracts use a brain-region prefix and end with `-spinal` (e.g., corticospinal).
- **Ascending tracts** (Table 8.4):
  - **Anterolateral spinothalamic** — pain, temperature; second-order neurons decussate in spinal cord; project to thalamus → cortex.
  - **Dorsal column–medial lemniscus** — touch, pressure, proprioception; ipsilateral ascent in fasciculus gracilis (lower body) and cuneatus (upper body); first synapse in medulla (nucleus gracilis / cuneatus); second-order decussates and ascends as medial lemniscus to thalamus → cortex.
  - **Posterior spinocerebellar** — proprioception to cerebellum, ipsilateral.
  - **Anterior spinocerebellar** — proprioception to cerebellum from both sides.
- **Descending tracts** (Table 8.5):
  - **Pyramidal (corticospinal) tracts** — primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus) → medulla pyramids → cross over (80–90%) → **lateral corticospinal tract** controlling contralateral fine voluntary movement of limb musculature; the uncrossed ~10% form the **anterior corticospinal tract** controlling axial musculature, crossing at the spinal level. The supplementary motor complex contributes ~10% of pyramidal fibers.
  - **Extrapyramidal motor tracts** — originate in brain-stem nuclei controlled by basal nuclei, substantia nigra, cerebellum, thalamus, cortex:
    - **Rubrospinal** (from red nucleus) — crossed.
    - **Tectospinal** (from superior colliculus) — crossed.
    - **Vestibulospinal** (from vestibular nuclei) — uncrossed.
    - **Reticulospinal** (from reticular formation) — crossed; major extrapyramidal tract.
  - Cerebellum has no direct descending tracts to spinal cord; it influences motor activity indirectly via the vestibular nuclei, red nucleus, and basal nuclei.
- The motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease are referred to clinically as **extrapyramidal symptoms** because they arise from disrupted basal-nuclei–substantia-nigra circuitry, not from direct pyramidal-tract damage.
- **Babinski reflex** (Clinical Application) — sole-of-foot stroke evokes upward toe extension and toe fanning in infants and children under 2; normally suppressed by mature descending pyramidal tracts. In adults, normal response is curling/flexion. Adult Babinski indicates CNS damage (spinal cord injury, ALS, MS, brain tumors, stroke).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Unlike the brain, in which the gray matter forms a cortex over white matter, the gray matter of the spinal cord is located centrally, surrounded by white matter." (p. 231)
- > "From 80% to 90% of the pyramidal fibers decussate in the pyramids of the medulla oblongata (hence the name 'pyramidal tracts') and descend as the lateral corticospinal tracts." (p. 232)
- > "The cerebellum can influence motor activity only indirectly by its effect on the vestibular nuclei, red nucleus, and basal nuclei." (p. 234)
- > "A Babinski reflex in an adult can indicate CNS damage due to such conditions as spinal cord injury, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple sclerosis (MS), brain tumors, and others." (p. 234)

## Section 8.6 — Cranial and Spinal Nerves (pp. 234–237)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Cranial Nerves** (pp. 234–235)
- **Spinal Nerves** (p. 235)
  - **Reflex Arc** (p. 235)

### Core claims
- **12 pairs of cranial nerves**, numbered I–XII from anterior to posterior (Table 8.6). 2 pairs (I, II) arise from forebrain; 10 pairs (III–XII) arise from midbrain and hindbrain. Each is sensory, motor, or mixed.
- Special sensory cranial nerves (I olfactory, II optic, VIII vestibulocochlear) are sensory only; cell bodies of these sensory neurons are in peripheral ganglia, not the brain.
- Motor cranial nerves contain small proprioceptive fibers for muscle position but are functionally motor.
- **31 pairs of spinal nerves:** 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal. Each is mixed.
- **Dorsal vs. ventral root:** the dorsal root carries **sensory** fibers; cell bodies are clustered in the **dorsal root ganglion**. The ventral root carries **motor** fibers; somatic motor neuron cell bodies are in spinal cord gray matter (not in a ganglion). Autonomic motor cell bodies are in ganglia outside the cord (Ch 9).
- **Reflex arc** (Fig 8.29): sensory neuron → (interneuron) → somatic motor neuron → effector. The simplest reflexes (knee-jerk muscle stretch) are monosynaptic (sensory directly to motor). More complex reflexes engage multiple interneurons and may produce bilateral and multilevel motor responses. Skeletal muscle reflexes are detailed in Ch 12; autonomic reflexes in Ch 9.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Telencephalon / diencephalon / mesencephalon / metencephalon / myelencephalon** — five secondary brain vesicles.
- **Choroid plexus / cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)** — vascular epithelium producing CSF / clear fluid bathing ventricles and subarachnoid space.
- **Arachnoid villi / granulations** — CSF return to dural venous sinuses.
- **Gyrus / sulcus / fissure** — fold / groove / deep groove of cortex.
- **Frontal / parietal / temporal / occipital / insular lobes** — five cerebral lobes.
- **Precentral gyrus / postcentral gyrus** — primary motor / primary somatosensory cortex.
- **Mirror neuron** — fires both when an action is performed and when observed.
- **EEG bands** — alpha (10–12 Hz), beta (13–25 Hz), theta (5–8 Hz), delta (1–5 Hz).
- **REM / non-REM sleep** — paradoxical (theta EEG, dreams) / restful (delta in stages 3–4, slow-wave).
- **Glymphatic system** — brain waste-clearance system, active in deep sleep.
- **Basal nuclei (basal ganglia)** — caudate, putamen, globus pallidus; subthalamic and substantia nigra often included.
- **Corpus striatum** — caudate + lentiform (putamen + globus pallidus).
- **Motor circuit** — cortex (glutamate) → striatum (GABA) → globus pallidus / substantia nigra (GABA) → thalamus → cortex.
- **Huntington's disease** — autosomal dominant HTT CAG-repeat expansion; chorea, athetosis.
- **Parkinson's disease** — nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration; tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia.
- **Cerebral lateralization / split-brain** — hemispheric specialization / surgically cut corpus callosum.
- **Broca's / Wernicke's / conduction / angular-gyrus aphasias** — speech motor / comprehension / repetition / reading-writing deficits.
- **Limbic system** — cingulate gyrus, amygdaloid body, hippocampus, septal nuclei.
- **Papez circuit** — hippocampus → fornix → mammillary body → anterior thalamus → cingulate gyrus → hippocampus.
- **Memory categories** — short-term vs. long-term; declarative (semantic, episodic) vs. nondeclarative (procedural); working.
- **Memory consolidation** — short-term → long-term storage; requires hippocampus + sleep + protein synthesis.
- **Place cells** — hippocampal neurons firing at specific spatial locations (2014 Nobel).
- **Alzheimer's disease / amyloid-β / tau / APOE4** — late-onset dementia / aggregating plaque protein / tangle protein / risk allele.
- **LTP / LTD** — long-term potentiation / depression; NMDA-AMPA-CaMKII-dependent.
- **Dendritic spine** — postsynaptic protrusion; grows in LTP, shrinks in LTD.
- **Thalamus** — sensory relay center; lateral and medial geniculate, intralaminar nuclei.
- **Hypothalamus** — visceral, endocrine, autonomic, emotional, thermoregulatory control center.
- **Supraoptic / paraventricular nuclei** — produce ADH and oxytocin for posterior pituitary.
- **Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)** — master circadian clock entrained by retinal melanopsin.
- **Pineal gland / melatonin** — circadian rhythm hormone, secreted at night.
- **Corpora quadrigemina (superior / inferior colliculi)** — visual / auditory reflex relays.
- **Substantia nigra / ventral tegmental area** — midbrain dopamine sources.
- **Nigrostriatal / mesolimbic dopamine systems** — motor coordination / reward and motivation.
- **Nucleus accumbens** — limbic reward target of mesolimbic dopamine.
- **Pons (apneustic / pneumotaxic)** — pontine respiratory centers.
- **Cerebellum / Purkinje cell / ataxia / intention tremor** — motor coordination / output neuron / loss of coordination / oscillating limb during reaching.
- **Medulla oblongata / pyramids / vital centers** — caudal hindbrain / decussation site / vasomotor, cardiac, respiratory centers.
- **Reticular activating system (RAS)** — ascending arousal system; cholinergic, monoaminergic, orexin.
- **VLPO** — ventrolateral preoptic nucleus; GABA-releasing sleep-promoting region.
- **Orexin / hypocretin** — LHA peptide; narcolepsy when destroyed.
- **Pyramidal (corticospinal) tracts** — direct cortex-to-spinal motor; lateral (crossed) and anterior (uncrossed).
- **Extrapyramidal tracts** — rubrospinal, tectospinal, vestibulospinal, reticulospinal.
- **Babinski reflex** — toe extension and fanning on sole stroke; abnormal in adults.
- **Dorsal root ganglion** — cell bodies of sensory neurons in the PNS.
- **Reflex arc** — sensory neuron → (interneuron) → motor neuron → effector.
