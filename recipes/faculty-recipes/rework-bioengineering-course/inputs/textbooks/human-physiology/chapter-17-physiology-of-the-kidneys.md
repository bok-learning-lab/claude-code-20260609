6. **17.6 Diuretics and Renal Function Tests** (pp. 610–612) — loop diuretics (furosemide / Lasix), thiazides (hydrochlorothiazide), carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide), osmotic diuretics (mannitol), potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, triamterene, amiloride); hypokalemia as a diuretic complication; renal insufficiency, uremia, chronic kidney disease stages.

## Section 17.1 — Structure and Function of the Kidneys (pp. 581–586)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Gross Structure of the Urinary System** (pp. 581–583)
- **Control of Micturition** (p. 583)
- **Microscopic Structure of the Kidney** (pp. 583–586)
  - **Renal Blood Vessels** (p. 584)
  - **Nephron Tubules** (pp. 585–586)

### Core claims
- **Primary function:** regulation of extracellular fluid (plasma + interstitial fluid) via urine formation, including:
  1. Blood plasma volume → blood pressure regulation.
  2. Plasma waste concentration (urea, creatinine, uric acid).
  3. Plasma electrolytes (Na⁺, K⁺, HCO₃⁻, Ca²⁺, etc.).
  4. Plasma pH (acid-base balance, in concert with lungs).
  - Additionally: secretion of **erythropoietin** (RBC production stimulation).
- **Gross anatomy (Fig 17.1, 17.2):**
  - **Renal cortex** — outer reddish-brown layer; granular; contains glomeruli and convoluted tubules.
  - **Renal medulla** — striped inner region; contains 8–15 renal pyramids separated by renal columns; pyramids contain nephron loops and collecting ducts.
  - Each pyramid drains into a **minor calyx** → major calyces → **renal pelvis** → ureter → bladder → urethra.
  - **Ureter** undergoes peristalsis (pacemaker in renal calyces and pelvis); transports urine; intense pain when kidney stone obstructs.
- **Micturition (voiding):**
  - **Detrusor muscle** (smooth muscle of bladder wall) contracts upon parasympathetic ACh (muscarinic) stimulation.
  - **Two urethral sphincters:** internal (smooth muscle, autonomic); external (skeletal muscle, somatic via pudendal nerve).
  - **Guarding reflex:** during bladder filling, spinal interneurons (S2–S4) inhibit detrusor parasympathetic outflow + stimulate external sphincter.
  - **Voiding reflex:** sufficient bladder stretch → pontine micturition center → parasympathetic activation + sympathetic inhibition → detrusor contraction, internal sphincter relaxation. Voluntary control of external sphincter develops between ages 2–3.
- **Microscopic anatomy (Fig 17.5):** each kidney has > 1 million **nephrons**. Each nephron consists of:
  - **Glomerular (Bowman's) capsule** + **glomerulus** = renal corpuscle (in cortex).
  - **Proximal convoluted tubule** (cortex; cuboidal epithelium with dense microvilli brush border).
  - **Nephron loop of Henle** — descending limb (cortex → medulla) + ascending limb (medulla → cortex), with thin and thick segments.
  - **Distal convoluted tubule** (cortex).
  - **Collecting duct** (cortex → medulla) — receives fluid from multiple nephrons; drains into minor calyx.
- **Cortical vs. juxtamedullary nephrons (Fig 17.6):** cortical (outer 2/3, short loops, ~85% of nephrons); juxtamedullary (inner 1/3, long loops extending deep into medulla, critical for urine concentration).
- **Renal vasculature (Figs 17.4, 17.5):** renal artery → interlobar → arcuate → interlobular → **afferent arteriole** → **glomerulus** → **efferent arteriole** → **peritubular capillaries** (around cortical convoluted tubules) and **vasa recta** (long parallel loops around juxtamedullary nephron loops in medulla). This is the only place in the body where a capillary bed (glomerulus) is drained by an arteriole and feeds a second capillary bed downstream.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The primary function of the kidneys is regulation of the extracellular fluid (plasma and interstitial fluid) environment in the body. This is accomplished through the formation of urine, which is a modified filtrate of plasma." (p. 581)
- > "The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney responsible for the formation of urine. Each kidney contains more than a million nephrons." (p. 583)
- > "This arrangement of blood vessels is unique. It is the only one in the body in which a capillary bed (the glomerulus) is drained by an arteriole rather than by a venule and delivered to a second capillary bed located downstream (the peritubular capillaries)." (p. 584)
- > "The juxtamedullary nephrons play an important role in the ability of the kidney to produce a concentrated urine." (p. 585)

## Section 17.2 — Glomerular Filtration (pp. 586–589)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Glomerular Ultrafiltrate** (pp. 587–588)
- **Regulation of Glomerular Filtration Rate** (pp. 588–589)
  - **Sympathetic Nerve Effects** (p. 588)
  - **Renal Autoregulation** (pp. 588–589)

### Core claims
- **Three filtration barriers (Fig 17.8, 17.9):**
  1. **Capillary fenestrae** (200–500 Å diameter; ~100–400× more permeable than skeletal-muscle capillaries; surrounded by negative charges).
  2. **Glomerular basement membrane** (collagen IV + proteoglycans; ~5× thicker than other vascular BMs).
  3. **Slit diaphragms** between podocyte foot processes (30–50 nm wide; the major barrier to plasma proteins).
- **Genetic defects:** Alport's syndrome (collagen IV mutation) → glomerulonephritis; slit-diaphragm mutations → massive proteinuria (nephrotic syndrome).
- **Glomerular ultrafiltrate composition:** essentially plasma minus most proteins. A small amount of filtered albumin is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule by receptor-mediated endocytosis.
- **Net filtration pressure ≈ 10 mmHg** = glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure − (capsule hydrostatic + plasma colloid osmotic).
- **GFR (glomerular filtration rate):**
  - Female: **115 ml/min** = 7.5 L/hr = **180 L/day**.
  - Male: 125 ml/min.
  - Total plasma volume filtered every ~40 min. Of this, **99% reabsorbed**, ~1–2 L excreted as urine.
- **GFR regulation:**
  - **Sympathetic nerves** constrict afferent arterioles → ↓GFR; preserves blood volume in shock and exercise.
  - **Renal autoregulation:** GFR constant over MAP 70–180 mmHg via:
    - **Myogenic mechanism** — afferent arteriole smooth muscle stretches → mechanoreceptor Ca²⁺ entry → vasoconstriction. Protects glomerular capillaries from pressure surges.
    - **Tubuloglomerular feedback** — increased flow of NaCl past the **macula densa** (in the thick ascending limb where it meets the afferent arteriole) → ATP/adenosine released → afferent arteriole vasoconstriction → ↓GFR.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Glomerular capillaries are 100 to 400 times more permeable to plasma water and dissolved solutes than are the capillaries of skeletal muscles." (p. 586)
- > "The slit diaphragm poses the major barrier to the passage of plasma proteins into the filtrate." (p. 586)
- > "The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is the volume of filtrate produced by both kidneys per minute. The GFR averages 115 ml per minute in women and 125 ml per minute in men. This is equivalent to 7.5 L per hour or 180 L per day (about 45 gallons)!" (p. 588)
- > "This ability of the kidneys to maintain a relatively constant GFR in the face of fluctuating blood pressures is called renal autoregulation." (p. 588)

### Equations / quantitative

**Net filtration pressure:**
$$P_{\text{net filtration}} = P_{\text{glomerular cap}} - (P_{\text{capsule}} + \pi_{\text{plasma}}) \approx 10 \text{ mmHg}$$

**GFR (approximate calculation by inulin clearance):**
$$\text{GFR} = \frac{V \cdot U_{\text{inulin}}}{P_{\text{inulin}}}$$

where $V$ = urine flow rate (ml/min), $U$ = urine inulin concentration, $P$ = plasma inulin concentration.

**Daily filtrate volume:**
$$\text{GFR} \times 1440\,\text{min/day} = 125 \times 1440 \approx 180{,}000\,\text{ml/day} = 180\,\text{L/day}$$

## Section 17.3 — Reabsorption of Salt and Water (pp. 589–597)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Reabsorption in the Proximal Tubule** (pp. 590–591)

  - **Significance of Proximal Tubule Reabsorption** (pp. 590–591)
- **The Countercurrent Multiplier System** (pp. 591–594)
  - **Ascending Limb of the Nephron Loop** (p. 591)
  - **Descending Limb of the Nephron Loop** (p. 592)
  - **Countercurrent Multiplication** (pp. 592–593)
  - **Vasa Recta** (p. 593)
  - **Effects of Urea** (pp. 593–594)
- **Collecting Duct: Effect of Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)** (pp. 594–597)

### Core claims
- **Filtrate volume challenge:** 180 L/day filtered; only 1–2 L excreted → 99% reabsorbed. Even maximally hydrated, 16 ml/min urine (23 L/day). Severely dehydrated, 0.3 ml/min (400 ml/day **obligatory water loss** — the minimum needed to excrete metabolic waste).
- **Proximal tubule (~65% of reabsorption; Fig 17.13):**
  - **Na⁺ active transport** from filtrate to peritubular blood drives the rest. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pumps on the basolateral membrane create the gradient; apical membrane is freely permeable to Na⁺.
  - **Cl⁻** follows passively (electrical attraction); transcellular in early proximal tubule, paracellular in late.
  - **Water** follows by osmosis through **aquaporin channels** in proximal tubule cells.
  - **Glucose, amino acids** completely reabsorbed by Na⁺-cotransport.
  - **HCO₃⁻** reabsorbed via Na⁺/H⁺ exchange + carbonic anhydrase (Section 17.5).
- **Filtrate stays isotonic (300 mOsm)** at proximal-tubule exit because water and salt are reabsorbed in proportion.
- **Countercurrent multiplier system (Fig 17.14, 17.15):**
  - **Thick ascending limb of Henle's loop** actively extrudes NaCl into the interstitium via a **Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter** (secondary active transport powered by the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase). Impermeable to water.
  - **Descending limb** is permeable to water (aquaporins) but impermeable to NaCl. Water leaves passively → fluid concentrates as it descends.
  - **Positive-feedback loop:** the more salt the ascending limb extrudes → the more concentrated the interstitium → the more water leaves the descending limb → the more concentrated the fluid arriving in the ascending limb → the more salt can be pumped. This **multiplies** the interstitial concentration from 300 mOsm (cortex) to **1,200 mOsm** (deep medulla).
  - **Filtrate exiting the ascending limb is hypotonic (~100 mOsm)** because salt was removed without water.
- **Vasa recta countercurrent exchange (Fig 17.16):** vessels parallel the loop; descending vasa recta gain salt + urea while losing water; ascending vasa recta lose salt + urea while gaining water. Net effect: water is removed from the medullary interstitium while salt and urea are preserved → maintains the hypertonic medulla.
- **Urea recycling (Fig 17.17):** urea diffuses out of the inner medullary collecting duct (urea-permeable terminal segment) into the interstitial fluid → re-enters the thin ascending limb → recycles. Contributes substantially to medullary hypertonicity in inner medulla.
- **Collecting duct + ADH (Figs 17.18, 17.19, 17.20; Table 17.3):**
  - Collecting duct is **impermeable to NaCl** but its water permeability is regulated by **ADH (arginine vasopressin)** secreted from posterior pituitary.
  - **ADH stimulates** insertion of **aquaporin water channels** from intracellular vesicles into the apical plasma membrane of collecting duct cells via cAMP → fusion → more water reabsorption → concentrated urine (up to 1,200 mOsm).
  - **Without ADH:** vesicles endocytosed back → no aquaporins in apical membrane → no water reabsorbed → dilute urine (50 mOsm; up to 18 L/day).
  - **Osmoreceptors** in third-ventricle hypothalamus (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei) detect plasma osmolality changes as small as 1% above normal (280–295 mOsm).
- **Diabetes insipidus** (Clinical Application):
  - **Central** — inadequate ADH secretion; treat with desmopressin.
  - **Nephrogenic** — kidney resistant to ADH; often caused by lithium (used for bipolar disorder) or genetic aquaporin/receptor defects; treat by adequate hydration.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Approximately 65% of the salt and water in the original glomerular ultrafiltrate is reabsorbed across the proximal tubule and returned to the vascular system." (p. 590)
- > "Active transport pumps create a concentration gradient that favors the diffusion of Na⁺ from the tubular fluid, across the apical plasma membranes, and into the epithelial cells of the proximal tubule. The Na⁺ is then extruded into the surrounding interstitial fluid by the Na⁺/K⁺ pumps." (p. 590)
- > "This positive feedback mechanism, which multiplies the concentration of interstitial fluid and descending limb fluid, is called the countercurrent multiplier system." (p. 592)
- > "When ADH binds to its membrane receptors in the cells of the collecting duct, it stimulates the production of cAMP as a second messenger … This initiates a chain of events that causes vesicles with aquaporins in their membranes to travel from the Golgi apparatus and fuse with the plasma membrane." (p. 596)
- > "In the absence of ADH, urine concentration is as low as 50 mOsm and urine production is 750 mL per hour (18 L per day, if it could be maintained)." (p. 596)

### Equations / quantitative

**Filtered load of a substance per minute:**
$$\text{Filtered load} = \text{GFR} \cdot [X]_{\text{plasma}}$$

**Urinary excretion as net of three processes:**
$$\dot{Q}_{\text{excretion}} = \dot{Q}_{\text{filtration}} + \dot{Q}_{\text{secretion}} - \dot{Q}_{\text{reabsorption}}$$

**Obligatory water loss:** ~400 ml/day, set by maximum urine osmolality (≈ medullary interstitial osmolality of 1,200 mOsm).

## Section 17.4 — Renal Plasma Clearance (pp. 597–603)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Transport Process Affecting Renal Clearance** (pp. 598–599)
  - **Tubular Secretion of Drugs** (pp. 598–599)
- **Renal Clearance of Inulin: Measurement of GFR** (pp. 599–600)
- **Renal Clearance Measurements** (pp. 600–601)
  - **Clearance of Urea** (p. 601)
  - **Clearance of PAH: Measurement of Renal Blood Flow** (p. 601)
- **Reabsorption of Glucose** (pp. 601–603)
  - **Glycosuria** (p. 602)

### Core claims
- **Renal plasma clearance** = volume of plasma completely cleared of a substance per minute. Units: ml/min.
$$C_X = \frac{V \cdot U_X}{P_X}$$
where $V$ = urine flow rate (ml/min), $U_X$ = urine concentration, $P_X$ = plasma concentration.
- **Mass balance:** excretion = filtration + secretion − reabsorption.
- **Inulin clearance = GFR (Fig 17.22):** inulin is a fructose polymer; freely filtered; **neither reabsorbed nor secreted**; therefore amount excreted = amount filtered. Gold-standard GFR measurement.
- **Creatinine clearance ≈ GFR (slightly higher because of small tubular secretion).** Plasma creatinine is used clinically with age, sex, and weight in the **eGFR** (estimated GFR) equation. Stages of chronic kidney disease are defined by eGFR thresholds.
- **PAH (para-aminohippuric acid) clearance ≈ total renal plasma flow (Fig 17.23):** PAH is filtered AND completely secreted by **organic anion transporters (OATs)** in the proximal tubule basolateral membrane → essentially zero PAH in renal vein. PAH clearance ≈ 625 ml/min. Since GFR ≈ 120 ml/min, **filtration fraction = GFR / RPF ≈ 120/625 ≈ 20%** of renal plasma flow is filtered.
- **OAT family** is polyspecific (one carrier moves many drugs and metabolites): bile acids, steroids, penicillin and other antibiotics, NSAIDs, antiviral drugs, methotrexate, uric acid. **OCTs (organic cation transporters)** secrete metformin, cisplatin. Polymorphisms account for inter-individual drug-response variability.
- **Probenecid (Clinical Application):** inhibits OATs in the proximal tubule. Originally used in WWII to prolong penicillin half-life. Now used to treat **gout** by inhibiting tubular reabsorption of urate (one of OAT's many substrates) and promoting uric acid excretion.
- **Transport maximum (Tm)** and **renal plasma threshold:**
  - Glucose is reabsorbed by **Na⁺-glucose cotransport** (SGLT2) in the proximal tubule (Fig 17.24); facilitated diffusion (GLUT2) exits the basolateral side.
  - **Tm (glucose) ≈ 375 mg/min** = maximum reabsorption rate when all carriers are saturated.
  - **Renal plasma threshold** for glucose ≈ **180–200 mg/dL** (plasma concentration at which glycosuria appears).
  - **Glycosuria** = glucose in urine; appears in **diabetes mellitus** when fasting/postprandial glycemia exceeds threshold. Excess glucose carries water with it osmotically → polyuria, dehydration, thirst.
  - **Diabetes mellitus vs. diabetes insipidus:** "mellitus" (sweet urine; glucose) vs. "insipidus" (tasteless dilute urine; ADH failure). Both produce polyuria but via different mechanisms.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The process of reabsorption decreases renal clearance, while the process of secretion increases renal clearance." (p. 598)
- > "If a substance is neither reabsorbed nor secreted by the tubules, the amount excreted in the urine per minute will equal the amount that is filtered out of the glomeruli per minute." (p. 599)
- > "Because inulin is filtered but neither reabsorbed nor secreted, the inulin clearance rate equals the glomerular filtration rate (GFR)." (p. 599)
- > "The normal PAH clearance has been found to average 625 ml/min. Because the glomerular filtration rate averages about 120 ml/min, this indicates that only about 120/625, or roughly 20%, of the renal plasma flow is filtered." (p. 601)
- > "Glucose appears in the urine—a condition called glycosuria—when more glucose passes through the tubules than can be reabsorbed. This occurs when the plasma glucose concentration reaches 180 to 200 mg per 100 ml." (p. 602)

### Equations / quantitative

**Renal plasma clearance:**
$$C_X = \frac{V \cdot U_X}{P_X} \quad (\text{ml/min})$$

**Mass-balance accounting:**
$$\text{Excretion rate} = (\text{filtration rate} + \text{secretion rate}) - \text{reabsorption rate}$$

**GFR via inulin clearance:**
$$\text{GFR} = C_{\text{inulin}} = \frac{V \cdot U_{\text{inulin}}}{P_{\text{inulin}}}$$

Worked example: $V = 2$ ml/min, $U = 30$ mg/ml, $P = 0.5$ mg/ml → GFR = (2 × 30)/0.5 = **120 ml/min**.

**Filtration fraction:**
$$\text{FF} = \frac{\text{GFR}}{\text{RPF}} \approx \frac{120}{625} \approx 0.20$$

**Transport maximum (saturable carrier-mediated reabsorption):**
$$\text{If filtered load} > T_m \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{spillover into urine}$$

**Filtered load of glucose at fasting:**
$$\text{Filtered glucose} = \text{GFR} \cdot [G]_{\text{plasma}} \approx 125 \text{ ml/min} \times 1 \text{ mg/ml} = 125 \text{ mg/min}$$

(well below $T_m = 375$ mg/min; threshold is reached when plasma [glucose] ≈ 3× normal.)

#### Figure 17.24 — The mechanism of glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule *(viewed)*

A schematic single proximal tubule cell shown in cross-section between **kidney tubule lumen** (top) and **capillary** (bottom). The **apical membrane** (top, facing the lumen) and **basolateral membrane** (bottom, facing the capillary) flank the cell cytoplasm. **Step 1 (apical):** Glucose and Na⁺ enter the cell together via a **cotransporter** (secondary active transport — SGLT-type). Na⁺ moves down its electrochemical gradient (maintained by step 2); the cotransporter exploits this energy to push glucose into the cell against its own gradient. **Step 2 (basolateral, left):** The **Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump** ("primary active transport") extrudes 3 Na⁺ from the cell in exchange for 2 K⁺ into the cell, consuming ATP. This maintains the low intracellular Na⁺ that energizes step 1. **Step 3 (basolateral, right):** Glucose exits the cell into the capillary via **facilitated diffusion** (GLUT-type carrier) down its concentration gradient. The K⁺ that entered the cell at step 2 leaks back across the basolateral membrane via simple diffusion through K⁺ channels. The figure clearly shows the **secondary active transport** logic: the primary pump establishes the Na⁺ gradient, and the cotransporter rides that gradient to do useful work (concentrating glucose).

## Section 17.5 — Renal Control of Electrolyte and Acid-Base Balance (pp. 603–610)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Role of Aldosterone in Na⁺/K⁺ Balance** (pp. 603–605)
  - **Sodium Reabsorption** (p. 604)
  - **Potassium Secretion** (pp. 604–605)
- **Control of Aldosterone Secretion** (pp. 605–606)
  - **Juxtaglomerular Apparatus** (p. 605)
  - **Regulation of Renin Secretion** (pp. 605–606)
  - **Role of the Macula Densa** (p. 606)
- **Natriuretic Peptides** (pp. 606–607)
- **Relationship Between Na⁺, K⁺, and H⁺** (p. 607)
- **Renal Acid-Base Regulation** (pp. 607–610)
  - **Reabsorption of Bicarbonate and Secretion of H⁺** (pp. 608–609)
  - **Urinary Buffers** (pp. 609–610)

### Core claims
- ~90% of filtered Na⁺ and K⁺ reabsorbed in proximal tubule and loop. Fine-tuning by **aldosterone** in the late distal tubule and cortical collecting duct.
- **Aldosterone** (mineralocorticoid from adrenal cortex):
  - Stimulates **Na⁺ reabsorption** (via basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase + apical Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter or ENaC channels in cortical collecting duct).
  - Stimulates **K⁺ secretion** (into filtrate via apical K⁺ channels driven by negative luminal potential from Na⁺ uptake).
  - Stimulates **H⁺ secretion** (apical H⁺ ATPase pumps and H⁺/K⁺ pumps).
  - With max aldosterone: 0 Na⁺ excreted. Without aldosterone: ~30 g Na⁺ excreted/day.
- **K⁺ secretion (Fig 17.25)** comes entirely from the late distal tubule and cortical collecting duct (proximal-tubule reabsorption removes 90% of filtered K⁺ first; all urinary K⁺ is secreted). A K⁺-rich meal → modest plasma K⁺ ↑ → adrenal cortex senses directly and secretes aldosterone → enhanced K⁺ excretion. Independent K⁺-channel insertion mechanism also operates.
- **Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (Fig 17.27; Table 17.6):**
  - **Granular cells** in afferent arteriole (juxtaglomerular apparatus) secrete **renin** in response to: ↓blood volume (direct baroreceptor effect), sympathetic β1-adrenergic stimulation, or low macula-densa Na⁺ signal.
  - **Renin** cleaves **angiotensinogen** → **angiotensin I** (10 aa).
  - **ACE (angiotensin converting enzyme)** in lung capillaries converts AT-I → **angiotensin II** (8 aa).
  - **Angiotensin II:** (1) potent systemic vasoconstrictor → ↑BP; (2) stimulates aldosterone secretion → ↑Na⁺ and water retention; (3) directly stimulates renal Na⁺ reabsorption and constricts both afferent and efferent arterioles.
- **Macula densa** (in juxtaglomerular apparatus, Fig 17.26): a region where the thick ascending limb contacts the afferent arteriole. Senses NaCl content via Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporters. High NaCl in filtrate → release ATP/adenosine → afferent arteriole constricts (tubuloglomerular feedback) + inhibits renin release.
- **Natriuretic peptides:**
  - **ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide)** — secreted by atria in response to wall stretch (increased blood volume); promotes Na⁺ and water excretion; opposes aldosterone.
  - **BNP (B-type natriuretic peptide)** — secreted by ventricles in response to volume and pressure overload. Clinically used to diagnose congestive heart failure.
- **K⁺-H⁺ coupling:** K⁺ and H⁺ trade across cell membranes. Acidosis → H⁺ enters cells, K⁺ exits → **hyperkalemia**. Alkalosis → reverse → **hypokalemia**. Apical **H⁺/K⁺ ATPase** in distal tubule trades urinary H⁺ for K⁺ reabsorption (Fig 17.28).
- **Renal acid-base regulation (Fig 17.29):**
  - **Proximal tubule:** Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger pumps H⁺ into the filtrate; the H⁺ combines with filtered HCO₃⁻ to form H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O (catalyzed by apical carbonic anhydrase) → CO₂ diffuses into the tubule cell → cytoplasmic carbonic anhydrase reverses the reaction → HCO₃⁻ + Na⁺ moved across basolateral membrane into blood. **Net effect: HCO₃⁻ reabsorbed (80–90% of filtered).**
  - **Distal tubule** uses **primary active H⁺ ATPase** pumps for the remaining acidification; urine pH falls to as low as 4.5.
  - **Urinary buffers** (because urine pH cannot go below 4.5):
    - **Phosphate:** $\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-} + H^+ \to H_2PO_4^-}$.
    - **Ammonia:** generated from glutamine metabolism in proximal tubule cells: $\mathrm{NH_3 + H^+ \to NH_4^+}$ in the filtrate.
  - **Bicarbonate generation from glutamine:** acidosis stimulates glutamine catabolism in proximal tubule → yields **new bicarbonate** (above and beyond reabsorption) that enters blood + **ammonia** that buffers urinary H⁺.
- **Compensation:** primary disturbances in respiratory CO₂ (Ch 16) → renal HCO₃⁻ adjustment (days). Primary disturbances in metabolic HCO₃⁻ → ventilatory compensation (hours).
- **Acetazolamide** (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor): reduces proximal-tubule HCO₃⁻ reabsorption → mild diuresis + metabolic acidosis. Used to treat acute mountain sickness (counters respiratory alkalosis from hyperventilation; allows greater hypoxic ventilatory drive).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Renal reabsorption of Na⁺ and secretion of K⁺ are regulated by aldosterone, the principal mineralocorticoid secreted by the adrenal cortex." (p. 604)
- > "When aldosterone is secreted in maximal amounts, by contrast, all of the Na⁺ delivered to the distal tubule is reabsorbed. In this case urine contains no Na⁺ at all." (p. 604)
- > "Granular cells within the afferent arteriole secrete the enzyme renin into the blood." (p. 605)
- > "Angiotensin II … stimulates the adrenal cortex to secrete aldosterone." (p. 605)
- > "Atrial natriuretic peptide is produced by the atria of the heart and secreted in response to the stretching of the atrial walls by increased blood volume." (p. 607)
- > "Because the kidneys normally reabsorb almost all of the filtered bicarbonate and excrete H⁺, normal urine contains little bicarbonate and is slightly acidic (with a pH range between 5 and 7)." (pp. 607–608)

### Equations / quantitative

**Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransport (thick ascending limb):**
$$\mathrm{Na^+_{lumen}} + \mathrm{K^+_{lumen}} + 2\,\mathrm{Cl^-_{lumen}} \to \mathrm{Na^+_{cell}} + \mathrm{K^+_{cell}} + 2\,\mathrm{Cl^-_{cell}} \quad (\text{site of loop diuretic action})$$

**Renin-angiotensin cascade:**
$$\mathrm{Angiotensinogen} \xrightarrow{\text{renin}} \mathrm{Angiotensin\,I} \xrightarrow{\text{ACE (lung)}} \mathrm{Angiotensin\,II}$$

**Proximal-tubule bicarbonate reabsorption (Fig 17.29):**
$$\mathrm{HCO_3^-_{filtrate}} + \mathrm{H^+_{filtrate}} \xrightleftharpoons{\text{apical CA}} \mathrm{H_2CO_3} \to \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O}$$
$$\text{(CO}_2\text{ diffuses into cell, where:)}$$
$$\mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightleftharpoons{\text{cytoplasmic CA}} \mathrm{H_2CO_3} \to \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{HCO_3^-}$$
$$\mathrm{HCO_3^-_{cell}} + \mathrm{Na^+_{cell}} \to \mathrm{HCO_3^-_{blood}} + \mathrm{Na^+_{blood}} \quad (\text{basolateral cotransport})$$

**Urinary buffer reactions:**
$$\mathrm{NH_3} + \mathrm{H^+} \to \mathrm{NH_4^+}$$
$$\mathrm{HPO_4^{2-}} + \mathrm{H^+} \to \mathrm{H_2PO_4^-}$$

**Bicarbonate generation from glutamine (acidosis-stimulated):**
$$\mathrm{Glutamine} \to \alpha\text{-ketoglutarate} + 2\,\mathrm{NH_3} \to (\text{further metabolism}) \to 2\,\mathrm{HCO_3^-_{new}}$$

## Section 17.6 — Diuretics and Renal Function Tests (pp. 610–612)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Use of Diuretics** (pp. 610–612)
- **Renal Function Tests and Kidney Disease** (p. 612)

### Core claims
- **Diuretics** reduce blood volume to treat hypertension, edema, and congestive heart failure. Mechanism: inhibit Na⁺ (and Cl⁻ and water) reabsorption at a specific tubule site.
- **Five categories (Table 17.8; Fig 17.30):**
  1. **Loop diuretics** — **furosemide (Lasix)**. Inhibit Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter in **thick ascending limb**. Most powerful (up to 25% of filtered salt/water excreted). Eliminates the medullary osmotic gradient → impaired ADH response.
  2. **Thiazides** — **hydrochlorothiazide**. Inhibit Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter in **distal convoluted tubule** (~8% of salt/water).
  3. **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** — **acetazolamide**. Block proximal tubule HCO₃⁻ reabsorption. Weak diuretic; used for acute mountain sickness.
  4. **Osmotic diuretics** — **mannitol**. Non-reabsorbable solute increases tubular osmotic pressure → less water reabsorbed.
  5. **Potassium-sparing diuretics** — **spironolactone** (aldosterone receptor antagonist), **triamterene**, **amiloride** (Na⁺ channel blockers). Block Na⁺ reabsorption in cortical collecting duct without increasing K⁺ excretion.
- **Hypokalemia** is a common side effect of loop diuretics and thiazides (increased Na⁺ delivery to the distal tubule → increased aldosterone-driven K⁺ secretion). Often requires KCl supplementation or pairing with a K⁺-sparing diuretic.
- **Renal function tests:**
  - **eGFR (estimated GFR)** — from plasma creatinine, age, sex, weight.
  - **BUN (blood urea nitrogen)** + creatinine ratio — assesses kidney function and hydration status.
  - **Urinalysis** — protein (proteinuria suggests glomerular damage), glucose (diabetes), ketones, blood, casts.
- **Chronic kidney disease (CKD)** is staged by eGFR.
- **Uremia** = symptoms of severe renal failure: nausea, fatigue, pruritus, encephalopathy from accumulated nitrogenous waste. Treatment: dialysis or transplantation.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The loop diuretics, such as furosemide (Lasix), are the most powerful diuretics, reducing salt and water reabsorption by as much as 25% by inhibiting the Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ cotransporters in the thick segments of the ascending limb." (p. 610)
- > "Spironolactones (Aldactone) are aldosterone antagonists that compete with aldosterone for cytoplasmic receptor proteins in the cells of the cortical collecting duct. These drugs thus block the aldosterone stimulation of Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ secretion." (p. 611)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Nephron** — functional unit of the kidney; > 1 million per kidney.
- **Glomerulus** — capillary tuft in cortex; site of filtration.
- **Glomerular (Bowman's) capsule** — cup-shaped collector of filtrate.
- **Proximal convoluted tubule** — cortex; ~65% reabsorption; brush border.
- **Nephron loop (loop of Henle)** — descending limb (water permeable) + ascending limb (NaCl pumping).
- **Distal convoluted tubule** — fine-tuning of Na⁺, K⁺, H⁺.
- **Collecting duct** — water permeability controlled by ADH.
- **Cortical vs. juxtamedullary nephrons** — short loops (~85%) vs. long loops (concentrate urine).
- **Afferent / efferent arteriole** — into / out of glomerulus.
- **Peritubular capillaries / vasa recta** — second capillary bed around tubules; vasa recta parallel the loop.
- **Glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** — 115–125 ml/min; 180 L/day.
- **Filtration fraction** — GFR/RPF ≈ 20%.
- **Filtration barrier** — fenestrated endothelium + basement membrane + podocyte slit diaphragm.
- **Podocyte** — visceral epithelial cell of glomerular capsule with foot processes.
- **Tubuloglomerular feedback** — macula densa-mediated afferent arteriole constriction in response to high NaCl flow.
- **Renal autoregulation** — myogenic + tubuloglomerular feedback maintaining constant GFR over MAP 70–180 mmHg.
- **Reabsorption / secretion / excretion** — transport from tubule to blood / blood to tubule / urine output.
- **Aquaporin** — water channel protein.
- **ADH (arginine vasopressin)** — posterior pituitary hormone; increases collecting duct water permeability via aquaporin insertion.
- **Diabetes insipidus** — ADH deficiency (central) or resistance (nephrogenic) → polyuria.
- **Countercurrent multiplier** — positive-feedback NaCl extrusion from ascending limb + water exit from descending limb → 1,200 mOsm medulla.
- **Vasa recta** — long parallel medullary capillaries with countercurrent exchange.
- **Urea recycling** — terminal collecting duct urea diffusion → re-enters ascending limb → contributes to medullary hypertonicity.
- **Obligatory water loss** — ~400 ml/day minimum urine to excrete metabolic waste.
- **Inulin** — fructose polymer; ideal GFR marker (filtered, not reabsorbed/secreted).
- **Creatinine clearance / eGFR** — clinical surrogate for GFR.
- **PAH (para-aminohippuric acid)** — measures total renal plasma flow (filtered + completely secreted by OATs).
- **Organic anion / cation transporter (OAT, OCT)** — polyspecific tubular secretion carriers for drugs and metabolites.
- **Probenecid** — OAT inhibitor; treats gout and prolongs penicillin.
- **Transport maximum (Tm)** — saturable carrier rate; e.g., glucose 375 mg/min.
- **Renal plasma threshold** — plasma concentration at which a substance appears in urine; glucose 180–200 mg/dL.
- **Glycosuria** — glucose in urine; diabetes mellitus.
- **Aldosterone** — adrenal mineralocorticoid; ↑Na⁺ reabsorption, ↑K⁺ secretion, ↑H⁺ secretion in late distal tubule/cortical collecting duct.
- **Juxtaglomerular apparatus** — afferent arteriole granular cells + macula densa.
- **Renin** — enzyme from granular cells; cleaves angiotensinogen.
- **Angiotensin I / II** — peptide products; AT-II vasoconstricts, stimulates aldosterone, promotes Na⁺ reabsorption.
- **ACE (angiotensin converting enzyme)** — lung capillary enzyme; AT-I → AT-II. Target of ACE inhibitors.
- **Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) / BNP** — heart-derived; ↑Na⁺ and water excretion; opposes aldosterone.
- **Hyperkalemia / hypokalemia** — high / low plasma K⁺; ECG and neuromuscular consequences.
- **Carbonic anhydrase** — RBC and renal-tubule enzyme; central to bicarbonate handling.
- **Bicarbonate reabsorption** — proximal tubule via indirect Na⁺/H⁺ exchange + CA shuttle.
- **Bicarbonate generation** — proximal tubule glutamine metabolism produces new HCO₃⁻ + ammonia (urinary buffer) under acidosis.
- **Urinary buffers** — phosphate (HPO₄²⁻) and ammonia (NH₃).
- **Loop diuretic** — furosemide; Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter inhibitor.
- **Thiazide diuretic** — hydrochlorothiazide; Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter inhibitor in distal tubule.
- **CA inhibitor** — acetazolamide; proximal-tubule HCO₃⁻ reabsorption inhibitor.
- **Osmotic diuretic** — mannitol.
- **K⁺-sparing diuretic** — spironolactone (aldosterone antagonist), triamterene/amiloride (ENaC blockers).
- **Uremia** — symptoms of accumulated nitrogenous waste in renal failure.
- **Chronic kidney disease (CKD)** — staged by eGFR; managed by RAAS blockade, dietary restriction, dialysis, transplant.
