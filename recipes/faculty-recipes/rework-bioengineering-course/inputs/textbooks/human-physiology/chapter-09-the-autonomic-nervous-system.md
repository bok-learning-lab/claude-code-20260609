## Section 9.1 — Neural Control of Involuntary Effectors (pp. 243–244)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Autonomic Neurons** (p. 243)
- **Visceral Effector Organs** (p. 244)

### Core claims
- **Autonomic motor pathway uses two neurons** (vs. one in the somatic motor system): a **preganglionic neuron** with its cell body in the CNS (brain or spinal cord grey matter) synapses with a **postganglionic neuron** whose cell body lies in an **autonomic ganglion** outside the CNS; the postganglionic axon innervates the visceral effector cell (Fig 9.1, Table 9.1).
- **Targets:** cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, and glands — i.e., visceral organs and blood vessels. Skeletal muscle is *not* an autonomic target.
- **Origin sites:** preganglionic fibers originate in the midbrain/hindbrain and in the upper thoracic to fourth sacral spinal levels. Autonomic ganglia lie in the head, neck, and abdomen, and in chains paralleling the spinal cord.
- **Sensory side:** visceral afferents typically enter the cord on dorsal roots, but important inputs (blood pressure, plasma pH, O₂) enter the brain via cranial nerves IX (glossopharyngeal) and X (vagus) — mixed nerves carrying sensory and parasympathetic motor axons.
- **Visceral effectors are partly autonomous:** smooth muscle maintains a resting tone in the absence of nerve stimulation; cardiac muscle and many smooth muscles contract rhythmically via gap-junction-conducted depolarization initiated by the cells themselves. **Autonomic innervation simply modulates intrinsic activity**, raising or lowering a baseline firing rate (resting tone).
- **Denervation hypersensitivity:** unlike skeletal muscle (which becomes flaccid and atrophies when its motor nerve is cut), an autonomically denervated tissue becomes *more* sensitive to its neurotransmitter — explaining why gastric acid secretion can be restored after vagotomy.
- **Unique to autonomic effectors:** there is **no specialized motor end plate**; receptor proteins are distributed across the smooth muscle cell membrane. Effects can be excitatory *or* inhibitory (e.g., vagal ACh slows the heart).

## Section 9.2 — Divisions of the Autonomic Nervous System (pp. 245–250)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Sympathetic Division** (pp. 245–246)
  - **Collateral Ganglia** (p. 246)
  - **Adrenal Glands** (p. 246)
- **Parasympathetic Division** (pp. 246–250)

### Core claims
- **Sympathetic = thoracolumbar division.** Preganglionic cell bodies sit in the **lateral horn** of the spinal cord from **T1 to L2**. Myelinated preganglionic axons exit on ventral roots, branch off as **white rami communicantes**, and enter the **sympathetic trunk** (the bilateral chain of **paravertebral ganglia**) (Fig 9.2). Within the trunk, axons can ascend or descend many segments before synapsing; unmyelinated postganglionic axons rejoin spinal nerves as **gray rami communicantes** (Fig 9.3).
- **Divergence and convergence.** Each preganglionic fiber branches to many postganglionic neurons (divergence); each postganglionic neuron receives input from multiple preganglionic fibers (convergence). This architecture enables **mass activation** of the sympathetic division characteristic of fight-or-flight responses — but also permits tailored, organ-specific regulation (e.g., heart and kidneys).
- **Collateral (prevertebral) ganglia.** Preganglionic fibers exiting below the diaphragm typically pass through the sympathetic trunk without synapsing, form **splanchnic nerves**, and synapse in **celiac, superior mesenteric, and inferior mesenteric ganglia** (Fig 9.4). Postganglionic fibers innervate digestive, urinary, and reproductive organs.
- **Adrenal medulla = modified sympathetic ganglion.** Its cells derive from the same embryonic neural crest as postganglionic sympathetic neurons. It is innervated by **preganglionic sympathetic fibers** (no postganglionic neuron interposed) and secretes hormones directly: **epinephrine (~85%) and norepinephrine (~15%)**. The medulla and the sympathetic nerves are therefore considered a single **sympathoadrenal system**.
- **Parasympathetic = craniosacral division.** Preganglionic fibers originate in the brain (oculomotor nuclei in midbrain; nuclei in pons and medulla oblongata) and in **sacral levels S2–S4**. Preganglionic fibers travel in cranial nerves **III (oculomotor), VII (facial), IX (glossopharyngeal), X (vagus)** and in pelvic spinal nerves to **terminal ganglia** located *next to or within* the innervated organ.
- **Vagus nerve (CN X)** is the workhorse: long preganglionic fibers traverse the neck, thorax, and abdomen, providing parasympathetic innervation to the heart, lungs, esophagus, stomach, pancreas, liver, small intestine, and upper large intestine (Fig 9.6). **Sacral parasympathetic** fibers innervate the lower large intestine, rectum, urinary bladder, and reproductive organs.
- **Postganglionic distribution differs.** Sympathetic nerves to viscera contain *postganglionic* fibers (because the synapse occurred upstream in the chain or collateral ganglion); parasympathetic nerves to viscera contain *preganglionic* fibers (synapse happens in the organ itself).
- **Cutaneous effectors get sympathetic only.** Because most parasympathetic fibers don't travel in spinal nerves, blood vessels in the skin, sweat glands, and arrector pili muscles receive sympathetic but no parasympathetic input.

### Core claims
- **Two functional themes.** Sympathetic = **"fight or flight"** (mass adrenergic activation: ↑ heart rate, ↑ blood glucose, blood shunted to skeletal muscle, pupils dilate, bronchioles dilate). Parasympathetic = **"rest and digest"** or **"repast and repose"** (slowing the heart, dilating visceral blood vessels, ↑ GI secretion and motility). Sympathetic also has **tonic** baseline activity and tailored organ-specific modulation; it is not only an emergency system.
- **Neurotransmitter rules (Fig 9.7, Fig 9.10):**
  - **All** preganglionic autonomic axons (sympathetic *and* parasympathetic) release **ACh**.
  - **All** postganglionic parasympathetic axons release **ACh** ("cholinergic").
  - **Most** postganglionic sympathetic axons release **norepinephrine (NE)** ("adrenergic").
  - **Exceptions:** sympathetic fibers to **sweat glands** and to **blood vessels in skeletal muscle** release ACh (cholinergic sympathetic).
  - Adrenal medulla, stimulated by ACh from preganglionic sympathetic fibers, releases **epinephrine (~85%) and NE (~15%)** as hormones into blood.
- **Catecholamine family (Fig 9.8):** all derived from tyrosine. Pathway: tyrosine → DOPA → dopamine → norepinephrine → epinephrine. Epinephrine differs from NE only by an added **methyl (–CH₃)** on the amine nitrogen.
- **Synapses en passant (Fig 9.9):** postganglionic autonomic axons have multiple **varicosities** along their length that release neurotransmitter onto target cells — not a single terminal bouton. Sympathetic and parasympathetic varicosities often impinge on the same target cell with opposing effects.
- **Adrenergic receptors** are all **G-protein coupled**. Major classes and subtypes:
  - **α₁** — postsynaptic; raises cytosolic Ca²⁺ (IP₃/Ca²⁺ second messenger); contracts smooth muscle (vasoconstriction in skin/viscera; pupil dilation via radial iris muscle; GI sphincter contraction; urethral sphincter; ejaculation).
  - **α₂** — presynaptic on adrenergic terminals (negative feedback inhibition of NE release); also CNS receptors (target of **clonidine**, an antihypertensive).
  - **β₁** — mostly heart: ↑ rate, ↑ contractility; also renal (renin release).
  - **β₂** — relaxes smooth muscle of bronchioles (bronchodilation), uterus (during pregnancy), GI tract, skeletal-muscle vasculature; mediates epinephrine-driven vasodilation.
  - **β₃** — adipose; promotes lipolysis & heat production.
  - All β receptors signal through **cAMP**.
- **Generalities** (Table 9.4): α₁ activation contracts smooth muscle uniformly; vasoconstriction by sympathetic nerves is always α-mediated. β activation has diverse outputs — relaxes most smooth muscle but *increases* cardiac force and rate.
- **α-adrenergic receptors are more sensitive to NE; β-adrenergic receptors are more sensitive to circulating epinephrine.** This sensitivity split distinguishes neural sympathetic effects from circulating-epinephrine effects.
- **Cholinergic receptors** (Fig 9.11, Table 9.6) come in two pharmacologically distinct families:
  - **Nicotinic (N)** — **ligand-gated cation channel** within the receptor protein. Located on **all autonomic ganglion postganglionic cell bodies**, on **all neuromuscular junctions of skeletal muscle**, and in some CNS pathways. ACh opens the channel → Na⁺ influx (steep gradient) and K⁺ efflux → net **depolarization → excitation**. Nicotinic receptors are *always* excitatory. Blocked by **curare (tubocurarine)**.
  - **Muscarinic (M)** — **G-protein coupled**. Located on the targets of postganglionic parasympathetic axons (heart, smooth muscle, glands). Can be excitatory or inhibitory depending on subtype. In the **heart (M₂)**: ACh → G-protein → opens K⁺ channels → **hyperpolarization → slowed pacemaker depolarization → ↓ heart rate**. In the **intestine and other smooth muscle (M₃, M₅)**: ACh → G-protein → IP₃/Ca²⁺ pathway → smooth muscle contraction and glandular secretion. Blocked by **atropine** (from *Atropa belladonna*) and pirenzepine.
- **Drug examples (Table 9.5):**
  - **α₁ agonist** phenylephrine (decongestant), pseudoephedrine.
  - **α₂ agonist** clonidine (antihypertensive).
  - **β₁ agonist** dobutamine (raises cardiac output).
  - **β₂ agonist** albuterol/salbutamol, terbutaline (asthma).
  - **α antagonist** phentolamine.
  - **β antagonist** propranolol (nonselective), atenolol & metoprolol (β₁-selective, "cardioselective"; treat hypertension).
  - **Muscarinic agonist** pilocarpine (constricts pupils; treats glaucoma).
  - **Muscarinic antagonist** atropine (dilates pupils for eye exam; treats bradycardia; reduces gastric acid; treats organophosphate poisoning).
  - **Nicotinic antagonist** tubocurarine (surgical muscle relaxation).
- **Nonadrenergic, noncholinergic (NANC) fibers** release **ATP**, **vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)**, or **nitric oxide (NO)**. NO is not stored in vesicles — it's synthesized on demand from L-arginine by **NO synthase** when Ca²⁺ enters the terminal, then diffuses across the cleft and relaxes the postsynaptic smooth muscle. NO mediates **penile erection** (parasympathetic vasodilation of penile vessels — see Ch 20, Fig 20.21) and **cerebral artery vasodilation**.
- **Dual innervation produces three response types:**
  - **Antagonistic** — heart pacemaker (sympathetic β₁ ↑ rate; vagal M₂ ↓ rate); iris (sympathetic radial fibers / α₁ dilate; parasympathetic circular fibers / M constrict); GI motility.
  - **Complementary** — salivary secretion: parasympathetic produces watery saliva; sympathetic constricts salivary blood vessels and yields thicker, viscous saliva. Both increase total secretion.
  - **Cooperative** — male sexual response: parasympathetic NO-mediated vasodilation → erection; sympathetic α₁ → ejaculation. Micturition: parasympathetic M (M₃) → detrusor contraction; sympathetic α₁ → urethral sphincter; coordinated to allow bladder emptying.
- **Organs without dual innervation** (sympathetic only): **adrenal medulla**, **arrector pili muscles**, **sweat glands**, and **most blood vessels**. Regulation occurs by varying the tonic firing rate of the sympathetic fibers. Sweat glands and sympathetic vasodilator fibers to skeletal muscle blood vessels use **ACh** rather than NE — they are cholinergic sympathetic fibers.
- **Thermoregulation:** the sympathoadrenal system is required for non-shivering thermogenesis and for active heat dissipation. Cold → sympathetic activation, cutaneous vasoconstriction. Heat → sympathetic withdrawal, cutaneous vasodilation. Exercise → sympathetic activation: cutaneous vasoconstriction in limbs, but sweat glands (cholinergic sympathetic) produce dilute sweat and **bradykinin** to locally dilate the small surface vessels near the sweat glands — heat radiates from the trunk skin even while limb skin vessels are constricted.
- **Higher-center control hierarchy:**
  - **Medulla oblongata** — the executive center; contains cardiovascular, pulmonary, urinary, reproductive, and digestive control nuclei; receives vagal afferent input (Table 9.8 — lung stretch and J receptors; aortic chemoreceptors and baroreceptors; atrial and ventricular stretch receptors; GI stretch receptors).
  - **Hypothalamus** — coordinates temperature, hunger, thirst, pituitary regulation; widely regarded as *the* major regulator of the autonomic system.
  - **Limbic system** (cingulate gyrus, hypothalamus, fornix, hippocampus, amygdala) — drives the visceral correlates of emotion (blushing, pallor, cold sweat, racing heart, "butterflies").
  - **Cerebellum** influences ANS during motion sickness; **frontal and temporal cortex** modulate emotional autonomic responses.
  - **Aging** is associated with increased tonic sympathetic activity, possibly a compensation for higher adiposity but raising hypertension risk.
- **Biofeedback** can train limited voluntary control over autonomic functions (heart rate, blood pressure, skin conductance) — used for chronic pain, headache, incontinence, anxiety.

#### Figure 9.7 — Neurotransmitters of the autonomic motor system *(listed)*

Block diagram with four CNS-outflow rows feeding into target effectors. Top row: **cranial parasympathetic nerves** release ACh at the terminal ganglion; postganglionic releases ACh at the visceral effector. Next two rows: **sympathetic (thoracolumbar) nerves** — ACh at the paravertebral ganglion, then NE at the visceral effector; in parallel, ACh at the adrenal medulla which releases E (~85%) and NE (~15%) hormonally into the circulation; and ACh at the collateral ganglion, then NE at the effector. Bottom row: **sacral parasympathetic nerves** — ACh at the terminal ganglion, ACh at the effector. Establishes the four "ACh, ACh, ACh, NE-or-E" combinatorics.

#### Figure 9.8 / 9.9 — Catecholamine family and synapses en passant *(viewed)*

**Left panel (Fig 9.8):** vertical biosynthetic ladder from **tyrosine** at the top (amino acid; hydroxyphenyl + α-C-CH₂-NH₂-COOH) → **DOPA** (added hydroxyl on the ring) → **dopamine** (carboxyl lost, side chain becomes ethylamine) → **norepinephrine** (β-hydroxyl added — "noradrenaline") → **epinephrine** (N-methyl group highlighted in yellow as the *sole* structural difference from NE). DOPA, dopamine, NE shown unlabeled-coloured arrows downward; the methyl group on epinephrine is circled.

**Right panel (Fig 9.9):** **(a)** A smooth muscle cell with both a sympathetic neuron and a parasympathetic neuron running along its surface. Both axons exhibit beaded **varicosities** that form **synapses en passant** with the muscle cell — i.e., transmitter is released from many points along the axon rather than from a single terminal. **(b)** Enlarged detail of two varicosities on the same smooth muscle cell: the sympathetic varicosity contains synaptic vesicles with **NE** that binds **adrenergic receptors** on the muscle membrane; the parasympathetic varicosity contains vesicles with **ACh** that binds **cholinergic receptors**. The label "Antagonistic effects" sits between them, showing that the two transmitters bind nearby receptors and produce opposing intracellular cascades on the same target cell.

> Vision note: Anchor for catecholamine biosynthesis (CMS — Catechol-O-methylation order — the methyl group as the NE→E distinguishing feature), and for the structural basis of autonomic dual innervation acting on the same cell.

#### Figure 9.10 — Receptors involved in autonomic regulation *(viewed)*

A four-column diagram comparing parasympathetic and sympathetic outputs at the receptor level. **Left two columns: parasympathetic.** Top: a preganglionic neuron in yellow synapses onto a postganglionic neuron via **ACh** binding **nicotinic ACh receptors** (purple ion-channel icons). Bottom: the postganglionic neuron releases **ACh** onto the target via **muscarinic ACh receptors** (purple icons). Resulting box: "Parasympathetic nerve effects."

**Right three columns: sympathetic.** Each shows the same two-tier structure — preganglionic ACh on **nicotinic** receptors of a postganglionic neuron, then **norepinephrine** released from the postganglionic varicosities onto a target. The three target columns differ by adrenergic receptor type: **α₁** (target: viscera and skin → vasoconstriction), **β₁** (target: heart → ↑ HR & contractility), **β₂** (target: bronchioles of lung & blood vessels → dilation).

Color coding: neurons in yellow, **nicotinic receptors** (always at the ganglion synapse) drawn distinctly as the receptor symbol for the ion-channel ACh receptor; the postganglionic terminals release purple ACh dots (parasympathetic) or pink NE dots (sympathetic).

> Vision note: This is the canonical "ACh-nicotinic at the ganglion, ACh-muscarinic *or* NE-α/β at the target" summary diagram. Anchor for any "which receptor does this drug hit" question.

#### Figure 9.11 — Nicotinic vs. muscarinic acetylcholine receptors *(viewed)*

Three-panel comparison of receptor architecture.

**Left panel: Nicotinic ACh receptor.** A membrane diagram of a multi-subunit channel protein. ACh binds at the top; the channel within the receptor opens; **Na⁺ flows in** (steep gradient, large arrow) and **K⁺ flows out** (smaller arrow). Net effect labeled: **depolarization → excitation**. Caption emphasizes the ligand-gated nature: the ion channel *is part of the receptor*. Located on postsynaptic membranes of all autonomic ganglia, all neuromuscular junctions, and some CNS pathways.

**Middle panel: Muscarinic ACh receptor on the heart (M₂).** ACh binds; a G-protein complex (α-βγ subunits drawn as colored shapes within the membrane) dissociates; the βγ subunit opens a separate **K⁺ channel** elsewhere in the membrane. K⁺ flows out → **hyperpolarization → inhibition → slower heart rate**.

**Right panel: Muscarinic ACh receptor on intestinal smooth muscle (M₃).** Same architecture: ACh-G-protein dissociation; βγ activates an intermediate second-messenger system that ultimately opens **Ca²⁺ channels**. Ca²⁺ flows in → **depolarization → stimulation → smooth muscle contraction**. Caption highlights that the same neurotransmitter (ACh) bound to the same receptor family (muscarinic) produces **opposite effects** in different tissues because of different downstream second-messenger architecture.

> Vision note: Anchor for any question about why ACh slows the heart but contracts the gut, or why nicotinic effects are always excitatory while muscarinic effects vary.

Sofia attempts biofeedback on her pulse, has dilated pupils (atropine from morning eye exam), takes a cold pill containing **pseudoephedrine** (α₁ agonist) despite hypertension, uses **atenolol** (β₁-selective antagonist) for blood pressure, and uses an **asthma inhaler** (likely albuterol, β₂ agonist). ## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Autonomic nervous system (ANS)** — efferent system controlling involuntary effectors (cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, glands) via a two-neuron pathway.
- **Preganglionic neuron** — autonomic neuron with cell body in CNS; axon synapses in an autonomic ganglion.
- **Postganglionic neuron** — autonomic neuron with cell body in an autonomic ganglion; axon innervates the visceral effector.
- **Sympathetic / thoracolumbar division** — preganglionic origin T1–L2; short pre/long postganglionic; mass activation; fight-or-flight.
- **Parasympathetic / craniosacral division** — preganglionic origin in brain (CN III, VII, IX, X) and S2–S4; long pre/short postganglionic; rest-and-digest.
- **Paravertebral (sympathetic chain) ganglia** — bilateral chains of sympathetic ganglia paralleling the spinal cord.
- **Collateral (prevertebral) ganglia** — celiac, superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric ganglia; receive splanchnic preganglionics.
- **Splanchnic nerves** — preganglionic sympathetic fibers that pass through the sympathetic chain without synapsing.
- **White ramus communicans / gray ramus communicans** — myelinated preganglionic entry / unmyelinated postganglionic exit between spinal nerve and sympathetic trunk.
- **Terminal ganglion** — parasympathetic ganglion located next to or within the effector organ.
- **Adrenal medulla** — modified sympathetic ganglion; secretes ~85% epinephrine and ~15% NE into blood.
- **Sympathoadrenal system** — combined sympathetic nervous system and adrenal medulla, activated together.
- **Vagus nerve (CN X)** — major parasympathetic supply to thoracic and most abdominal viscera.
- **Denervation hypersensitivity** — increased target sensitivity to neurotransmitter after autonomic nerve damage.
- **Mass activation** — simultaneous excitation of most sympathetic postganglionic neurons in emergencies.
- **Catecholamines** — tyrosine-derived molecules: dopamine, NE, epinephrine.
- **Adrenergic** — releasing or responding to norepinephrine/epinephrine.
- **Cholinergic** — releasing or responding to acetylcholine.
- **α-adrenergic receptors** — G-protein-coupled receptors more sensitive to NE; α₁ (postsynaptic, contracts smooth muscle, IP₃/Ca²⁺) and α₂ (presynaptic feedback inhibition; CNS).
- **β-adrenergic receptors** — G-protein-coupled receptors signaling through cAMP; β₁ (heart), β₂ (smooth muscle relaxation), β₃ (adipose lipolysis).
- **Nicotinic ACh receptor** — ligand-gated cation channel; always excitatory; located at ganglia, NMJ, some CNS.
- **Muscarinic ACh receptor** — G-protein-coupled; effect varies by subtype (M₁–M₅); M₂ hyperpolarizes the heart, M₃/M₅ depolarize smooth muscle and glands.
- **Atropine** — muscarinic antagonist; from *Atropa belladonna*.
- **Pilocarpine** — muscarinic agonist; pupil constriction.
- **Curare (tubocurarine)** — nicotinic antagonist; surgical muscle relaxation.
- **Clonidine** — α₂ agonist; CNS-mediated antihypertensive.
- **Pseudoephedrine / phenylephrine** — α₁ agonists; decongestants.
- **Propranolol / atenolol / metoprolol** — β-blockers; atenolol & metoprolol β₁-selective.
- **Albuterol (salbutamol) / terbutaline** — β₂ agonists; bronchodilators.
- **Sympathomimetic drugs** — drugs that mimic sympathoadrenal effects.
- **Synapse en passant** — neurotransmitter release from varicosities along the length of an autonomic axon rather than at a single terminal bouton.
- **Varicosity** — bead-like swelling along an autonomic postganglionic axon containing neurotransmitter vesicles.
- **Nonadrenergic, noncholinergic (NANC) fibers** — autonomic axons using ATP, VIP, or NO.
- **Nitric oxide (NO)** — gaseous neurotransmitter synthesized on demand from L-arginine by NO synthase; mediates penile erection and cerebral vasodilation.
- **Dual innervation** — receipt by an organ of both sympathetic and parasympathetic input; effects may be antagonistic, complementary, or cooperative.
- **Autonomic dysreflexia** — uninhibited sympathetic outflow below a high spinal cord lesion (≥ T6); hypertensive emergency.
- **Biofeedback** — instrument-aided voluntary modulation of autonomic variables.
