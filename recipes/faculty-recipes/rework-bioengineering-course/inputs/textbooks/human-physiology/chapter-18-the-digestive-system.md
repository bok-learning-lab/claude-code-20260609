## Section 18.1 — Introduction to the Digestive System (pp. 619–622)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Layers of the Alimentary Tract** (pp. 620–621)
- **Regulation of the Alimentary Tract** (pp. 621–622)

### Core claims
- The alimentary (GI) tract is an ~9 m (30 ft) tube open at both ends. Because the lumen is continuous with the environment, "the harsh conditions required for digestion occur outside the body." Indigestible material (e.g., cellulose) traverses the tract without entering the body.
- Six functions of the digestive system: (1) **motility** (ingestion, mastication, deglutition, peristalsis & segmentation); (2) **secretion** (exocrine — water, HCl, bicarbonate, enzymes; endocrine — gut hormones); (3) **digestion** (hydrolysis to monomers); (4) **absorption** into blood or lymph; (5) **storage & elimination**; (6) **immune barrier** (columnar epithelium with tight junctions and subjacent immune cells).
- **Four tunics** of the GI tract, inside out:
  - **Mucosa** — simple columnar epithelium (tight + zonula adherens junctions), lamina propria with lymph nodules, muscularis mucosae.
  - **Submucosa** — vascular connective tissue with glands and the **submucosal (Meissner's) plexus**.
  - **Muscularis (externa)** — inner circular + outer longitudinal smooth muscle separated by the **myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus**, the major nerve supply of the tract.
  - **Serosa** — areolar tissue + visceral peritoneum.
- **Two regulatory layers, two modes:**
  - *Extrinsic*: autonomic nerves (vagal parasympathetic = "rest and digest", stimulates motility/secretion; sympathetic = inhibits) + endocrine hormones from gut mucosa.
  - *Intrinsic*: the **enteric nervous system** ("enteric brain") of interneurons and intrinsic sensory neurons within the gut wall + local paracrine regulators.
- About **80% of vagus nerve fibers are afferent**, carrying visceral sensation to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) and onward to the hypothalamus.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Within the lumen of the alimentary tract, large food molecules are hydrolyzed into their monomers (subunits). These monomers pass through the inner layer, or mucosa, of the small intestine to enter the blood or lymph in a process called absorption." (p. 619)
- > "Indigestible materials, such as cellulose from plant walls, pass from one end to the other without crossing the epithelial lining of the alimentary tract; because they are not absorbed, they do not enter the body." (p. 620)
- > "The four tunics of the alimentary tract, from the inside out, are the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and serosa." (p. 620)
- > "The myenteric plexus (Auerbach's plexus), located between the two muscle layers, provides the major nerve supply to the entire alimentary tract." (p. 621)
- > "The digestive system is regulated extrinsically by the autonomic nervous system and endocrine system, and intrinsically by the enteric nervous system and various paracrine regulators." (p. 622)

## Section 18.2 — From Mouth to Stomach (pp. 622–628)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Esophagus** (p. 623)
- **Stomach** (pp. 624–625)
- **Pepsin and Hydrochloric Acid Secretion** (pp. 624–627)
- **Digestion and Absorption in the Stomach** (p. 627)
- **Gastritis and Peptic Ulcers** (pp. 627–628)

### Core claims
- **Deglutition (swallowing)** is divided into oral, pharyngeal, and esophageal phases; coordinated contractions of ~25 paired muscles. The oral phase is voluntary; the pharyngeal and esophageal phases are reflexive and controlled by the swallowing center of the brain stem.
- **Esophagus**: ~25 cm muscular tube, lined by stratified squamous (nonkeratinized) epithelium. Upper third = skeletal muscle; lower third = smooth muscle. **Peristalsis** advances a bolus at ~2–4 cm/s. The **lower esophageal sphincter** is not a true sphincter in humans — its incompetence (esp. with hiatal hernia, obesity, caffeine, alcohol) underlies **GERD** and the burning sensation called heartburn.
- **Stomach** = J-shaped, most distensible part of the GI tract; regions are cardia, fundus, body, and pylorus (antrum + sphincter). Functions: store food, initiate protein digestion, kill bacteria, and deliver chyme to the duodenum.
- **Gastric glands** are exocrine; their cell types and products:
  - **Mucous neck / surface mucous cells** → mucus.
  - **Parietal cells** → **HCl** (pH down to ~0.8) and **intrinsic factor** (the only stomach product essential for life — needed for ileal absorption of vitamin B₁₂).
  - **Chief (zymogenic) cells** → pepsinogen.
  - **ECL cells** → histamine (paracrine).
  - **G cells** → gastrin (hormone).
  - **D cells** → somatostatin.
  - **P/D1 cells** → ghrelin (rises before meals; appetite signal; see Ch 19).
- **Parietal-cell HCl mechanism** (Fig 18.7): apical **H⁺/K⁺ ATPase** pumps H⁺ against a million-to-one gradient in exchange for K⁺; K⁺ recycles back to the lumen. CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic anhydrase) → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; the HCO₃⁻ leaves basolaterally in exchange for Cl⁻ (the "alkaline tide"), and the Cl⁻ then diffuses apically. Net: HCl into lumen, HCO₃⁻ into blood.
- **HCl secretion stimulation:** ACh from vagus, gastrin from G cells, and histamine from ECL cells (via H₂ receptors). The dominant pathway is *gastrin → ECL → histamine → parietal cell*. H₂-blockers (Tagamet, Pepcid, Zantac) target this paracrine step; proton-pump inhibitors (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole) block the apical H⁺/K⁺ ATPase directly.
- **Three functions of the strong acidity** (pH < 2): (1) denatures ingested protein; (2) autocatalytically activates pepsinogen → pepsin; (3) provides pepsin's pH optimum (~2.0).
- **Mucosal defense**: adherent alkaline mucus + tight junctions + rapid (3-day) epithelial turnover. Most peptic ulcers (esp. duodenal) are caused by **Helicobacter pylori** (Nobel 2005); treatment = PPI + two antibiotics. Most non-H. pylori ulcers are caused by **NSAIDs** (which inhibit prostaglandins that maintain mucus and HCO₃⁻).
- Only alcohol and aspirin are absorbed across the stomach wall (by lipid solubility).
- **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome**: gastrin-secreting tumor → excessive acid → duodenal ulcers.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The lower esophageal sphincter is not a true sphincter muscle in humans. This fact allows us to regurgitate, unlike some mammals, such as rodents, which have a true gastroesophageal sphincter and cannot regurgitate." (p. 623)
- > "Despite its numerous cell types and secretions, the only stomach function essential for life is the production of intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein needed for the intestinal absorption of vitamin B12." (p. 624)
- > "The parietal cells secrete H+ (protons), at a pH as low as 0.8, into the gastric lumen by primary active transport (involving carriers that function as an ATPase). These carriers, known as H+/K+ ATPase pumps, transport H+ uphill against a million-to-one concentration gradient into the lumen of the stomach while they transport K+ in the opposite direction." (p. 624)
- > "Gastrin stimulates ECL cells to secrete histamine, and histamine acts as a paracrine regulator to stimulate the parietal cells to secrete HCl." (p. 626)
- > "The 2005 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to two scientists who discovered that this common bacterium, rather than emotional stress or spicy food, is the cause of most cases of peptic ulcers of the stomach and duodenum." (p. 627)
- > "Almost all of the products of digestion are absorbed through the wall of the small intestine; the only commonly ingested substances that can be absorbed across the stomach wall are alcohol and aspirin." (p. 627)

### Equations / quantitative
- Parietal cell apical pump: $\mathrm{H^+}_{cell} + \mathrm{K^+}_{lumen} \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H^+/K^+\,ATPase}} \mathrm{H^+}_{lumen} + \mathrm{K^+}_{cell}$
- Cytosolic generation of acid + base: $\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O} \xrightleftharpoons[\mathrm{CA}]{} \mathrm{H_2CO_3} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+ + HCO_3^-}$
- Basolateral exchange (alkaline tide): $\mathrm{HCO_3^-}_{cell} \leftrightarrow \mathrm{Cl^-}_{blood}$; gastric juice net: HCl in lumen, HCO₃⁻ in blood.

#### Figure 18.7 — Secretion of gastric acid by parietal cells *(viewed)*

A schematic cross-section of a single **parietal cell** with the **lumen of stomach** on the upper boundary and **blood capillaries** on the lower (basolateral) boundary. Two transport pathways are drawn on the **apical** surface: (1) a **primary active transport ATPase carrier** that secretes H⁺ in exchange for K⁺ (the H⁺/K⁺ pump), with a small recycling arrow showing K⁺ leaking back into the lumen; (2) a separate facilitative diffusion route by which Cl⁻ exits the cell into the lumen. In the cytoplasm, **CO₂ + H₂O** combine under **carbonic anhydrase (CA)** to form **H₂CO₃**, which dissociates into H⁺ (joining the apical pump) and **HCO₃⁻**. On the **basolateral** surface, a secondary active transport carrier exchanges Cl⁻ in (against its gradient) for HCO₃⁻ out (down its gradient) into the blood capillaries — this is the alkaline-tide return that completes the net production of HCl in the lumen and bicarbonate in the blood.

## Section 18.3 — Small Intestine (pp. 628–632)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Villi and Microvilli** (pp. 628–629)
- **Intestinal Enzymes** (pp. 629–630)
- **Intestinal Contractions and Motility** (pp. 630–631)

### Core claims
- The small intestine is ~3 m long in the living person; divided into **duodenum** (~25 cm), **jejunum** (next two-fifths), and **ileum** (last three-fifths). Carbohydrate, lipid, amino-acid, calcium, and iron absorption are concentrated in the duodenum and jejunum; bile salts, vitamin B₁₂, water, and electrolytes are absorbed mainly in the ileum.
- **Three nested surface-area amplifications**: gross folds = **plicae circulares**; mucosal fingers = **villi**; apical membrane folds on each enterocyte = **microvilli** ("brush border").
- Each villus has a connective tissue core with blood capillaries (drain monosaccharides + amino acids → portal vein) and a central **lacteal** (drains absorbed fat → lymph). **Intestinal crypts of Lieberkühn** at the villus base contain **Paneth cells** (lysozyme + defensins), enteroendocrine cells, and **intestinal stem cells** that renew the epithelium every 5–7 days.
- **Brush border enzymes** are embedded in the microvillus plasma membrane, not free in the lumen (Table 18.1). They include the disaccharidases (sucrase, maltase, lactase); peptidases (aminopeptidase, **enterokinase / enteropeptidase** — activates trypsin); and phosphatases.
- **Lactose intolerance** = postweaning loss of lactase; undigested lactose draws water osmotically into the lumen → diarrhea, gas, bloating.
- **Celiac disease** = autoimmune villus destruction triggered by gluten in genetically susceptible people; gluten-free diet restores the villi.
- **Intestinal motility = peristalsis + segmentation.** Peristalsis is weaker than in the esophagus/stomach. **Segmentation** (rhythmic mixing constrictions) dominates and is paced by **slow waves**.
- **Slow waves** are generated by **interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC)** — neither neurons nor smooth muscle — that compose ~5% of the muscularis and act as an electrical syncytium via gap junctions. ICC depolarizations trigger Ca²⁺-spike action potentials in adjacent smooth muscle cells. **Slow waves are faster proximally**, creating a pressure head that pushes chyme distally.
- Autonomic nerves modulate intestinal contractions largely via the enteric nervous system: ACh (excitatory, parasympathetic); nitric oxide and VIP (inhibitory).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Microvilli are formed by foldings at the apical surface of each epithelial plasma membrane. These minute projections can be seen clearly only in an electron microscope." (p. 629)
- > "The brush border enzymes are not secreted into the lumen, but instead remain attached to the plasma membrane with their active sites exposed to the chyme." (p. 630)
- > "One brush border enzyme, enterokinase (also called enteropeptidase), is required for activation of the protein-digesting enzyme trypsin." (p. 630)
- > "The major contractile activity of the small intestine is segmentation. This term refers to muscular constrictions of the lumen, which occur simultaneously at different intestinal segments." (p. 630)
- > "In intestinal smooth muscle, however, the rhythm of contractions is paced by graded depolarizations called slow waves." (p. 630)

## Section 18.4 — Large Intestine (pp. 632–636)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Intestinal Microbiota** (pp. 633–635)
  - **Microbiota Colonization and Early Childhood** (pp. 633–634)
  - **Microbiota and Metabolic Function** (p. 634)
  - **Microbiota and Immune Function** (pp. 634–635)
- **Fluid and Electrolyte Absorption in the Intestine** (p. 635)
- **Defecation** (p. 636)

### Core claims
- The colon is ~1.5 m long; sequence is cecum → ascending → transverse → descending → sigmoid colon → rectum → anal canal. The outer longitudinal muscle is condensed into three bands (**taeniae coli**) shorter than the colon itself, which bunches it into pouches called **haustra**. **Diverticulitis** is inflammation of out-pouches (esp. sigmoid).
- The mucosa has crypts but **no villi** — large-intestine mucosa appears flat.
- The **appendix** is a blind tube with abundant lymphoid nodules; thought to reseed commensal flora; appendicitis can rupture → peritonitis.
- **Intestinal microbiota** (microflora / microbiome): ~10¹⁴ organisms, > 1.5 kg biomass in the colon; ~100× more genes than the human host. Mostly anaerobic; functionally commensal/mutualistic.
- Microbial functions:
  - Produce **vitamin K** and several B vitamins (riboflavin, thiamine, biotin, pantothenate, folate).
  - Ferment dietary fiber into **short-chain fatty acids** (acetate, propionate, **butyrate**) that fuel colonocytes, stimulate active Na⁺/Cl⁻ absorption (→ water absorption), and signal systemically to brain, adipose, and liver.
  - Stimulate gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) development, T-reg expansion, mucin production, and IgA secretion.
- **Obesity correlates with reduced microbial diversity**; germ-free mice colonized with obese-donor microbiota gain fat faster than mice colonized with lean-donor microbiota. Bariatric surgery alters the microbiome.
- **Vaginal vs. C-section delivery** seed different initial communities; breastfeeding adds milk microbes; rural / farm exposure increases diversity and reduces allergy risk.
- **Clostridium difficile** is the leading cause of nosocomial diarrhea; arises after antibiotics deplete commensals. **Fecal microbiota transplantation** (FMT) reseeds a normal microbiome.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)** = Crohn's disease + ulcerative colitis, with mucosal damage, altered tight junctions, microbiome shifts.
- **Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)** has no structural damage; it is a disorder of gut-brain neural regulation.
- **Fluid handling**: GI tract gets ~1.5 L/day from food/drink + 8–10 L/day from secretions; small intestine absorbs most → ~2 L enters colon → colon absorbs ~90% → < 200 mL excreted in feces. Colonic water absorption follows the osmotic gradient created by active Na⁺ and Cl⁻ absorption (aldosterone-stimulated, similar to renal tubules). Crypt cells can secrete Cl⁻ (and water) through **CFTR** channels; cholera toxin hyperactivates this pathway to produce secretory diarrhea.
- **Defecation reflex**: rectal distension → relaxation of internal anal sphincter + brain signaling. Voluntary external anal sphincter control completes or defers the act. Valsalva increases intra-abdominal pressure to assist excretion.

- > "Their numbers increase in the distal ileum and are greatest in the colon, where an estimated 10¹⁴ reside with a biomass greater than 1.5 kg." (p. 633)
- > "The microbiota are usually described as composed of commensal bacteria … however, mutualism may better describe the relationship between the intestinal microbiota and their human hosts." (p. 633)
- > "The bacteria ferment the sugars obtained from fiber into short-chain fatty acids—acetate, proprionate, and butyrate—which are used for energy by colonic epithelial cells." (p. 633)

### Core claims
- The liver is the largest internal organ (~1.3 kg) but only one to two cells thick: **hepatocytes** form **hepatic plates** separated by porous **sinusoids** with **fenestrae** 150–175 nm wide, no diaphragm, no basement membrane. The sinusoids contain phagocytic **Kupffer cells**. Hepatocytes can dedifferentiate to progenitor cells and the liver regenerates rapidly after injury.
- **Hepatic portal system** (capillaries → vein → capillaries): GI capillaries → hepatic portal vein → hepatic sinusoids → central vein → hepatic vein. Portal vein supplies 75–80% of hepatic blood flow; hepatic artery supplies the remaining 20–25% (adjusted compensatorily). Total hepatic flow ≈ 25% of cardiac output.
- **Portal lobule** is the functional unit: peripheral **portal triads** (branch of hepatic portal vein + branch of hepatic artery + bile ductule), central vein in the middle, **blood flows centripetally** through sinusoids while **bile flows centrifugally** through canaliculi inside hepatic plates.
- **Enterohepatic circulation**: a compound excreted by the liver into bile can be reabsorbed by the intestine into the hepatic portal vein and re-secreted by the liver. Bile salts recirculate 6–10× per day; only ~0.5 g/day are lost in feces.
- **Bile composition**: bile pigment (**bilirubin**), bile salts, phospholipids (mainly lecithin), cholesterol, inorganic ions. 250–1,500 mL/day produced by liver.
- **Heme → bilirubin pathway** (Fig 18.22): hemolysis liberates heme → biliverdin → bilirubin (free, lipid-soluble, albumin-bound in blood). The liver conjugates bilirubin with **glucuronic acid** to make it water-soluble for biliary excretion. Intestinal bacteria convert conjugated bilirubin → urobilinogen → stercobilin (brown feces); some urobilinogen recirculates enterohepatically and a fraction enters urine (amber color).
- **Bile acids** are amphipathic cholesterol derivatives. Primary bile acids = **cholic acid** + **chenodeoxycholic acid**, conjugated to glycine or taurine → bile salts. Gut bacteria deconjugate and convert primary → **secondary bile acids** (deoxycholic, lithocholic), 95% reabsorbed in the distal ileum. Bile salts form **micelles** that emulsify fats and incorporate cholesterol and lecithin.
- **Jaundice** = yellow staining from hyperbilirubinemia: prehepatic (hemolysis), hepatic (hepatitis, cirrhosis), posthepatic (gallstone, bile-duct stricture).
- **Detoxication**: (1) Kupffer-cell phagocytosis; (2) chemical alteration (mainly **cytochrome P450** family, induced via SXR nuclear receptor); (3) excretion in bile. Liver converts toxic NH₃ → urea (urea cycle, Ch 5; see also Ch 17), porphyrins → bilirubin, purines → uric acid.
- **Cytochrome P450 + grapefruit interaction**: grapefruit inhibits a CYP450 enzyme so that drugs like statins are metabolized more slowly and become more potent — the mechanism for many drug–drug and drug–food interactions.
- Liver synthesizes albumin (~70% of plasma protein), most globulins (except antibodies), clotting factors I (fibrinogen), II (prothrombin), III, V, VII, IX, XI, and angiotensinogen.
- Liver bidirectionally regulates blood glucose: glycogenesis & lipogenesis after a meal; glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and ketogenesis during fasting (Ch 5; Ch 19).
- **Gallbladder** stores and concentrates bile (35–100 mL); contracts in response to **CCK** to eject bile through the cystic → common bile duct → duodenum.
- **Gallstones**: mostly cholesterol; risk increased by pregnancy / OCPs (estrogen), cholesterol-lowering drugs, and rapid weight loss. Treated by laparoscopic cholecystectomy.
- **Pancreas** = both endocrine (**islets of Langerhans** → insulin & glucagon) and exocrine. Exocrine **acini** secrete inactive enzymes in saline; **ductule cells** secrete bicarbonate-rich fluid → 1–2 L pancreatic juice/day.
- **Bicarbonate secretion mechanism**: ductule cells take up CO₂ from blood, run carbonic anhydrase, exchange HCO₃⁻ out (apically) for Cl⁻ in; the Cl⁻ then re-exits through **CFTR** chloride channels. **Cystic fibrosis** patients (defective CFTR) have impaired bicarbonate/fluid secretion → pancreatic insufficiency.
- **Pancreatic enzyme activation cascade** (Fig 18.29): brush-border **enterokinase** activates trypsinogen → **trypsin**. Trypsin then activates the rest of the zymogens (chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidase, prophospholipase). Lipase, amylase, cholesterolesterase, ribonuclease, deoxyribonuclease are secreted in active form. **Pancreatic trypsin inhibitor** protects against premature activation in the pancreas.

### Core claims
- **Bayliss & Starling discovered the first hormone (secretin) in 1902** in a duodenal extract; they coined the term "hormone". Gastrin was discovered the same year.
- **Three phases of gastric secretion (Table 18.6):**
  - *Cephalic*: sight/smell/taste/anticipation → vagus → chief cells (pepsinogen) and ECL cells (histamine → HCl). Vagal stimulation also stimulates G cells (gastrin).
  - *Gastric*: distension + amino acids/peptides (esp. phenylalanine and tryptophan) in lumen → G cells (gastrin) → ECL (histamine) → parietal (HCl). **Positive feedback**: more HCl + pepsin → more peptides → more gastrin → more HCl. **Negative feedback** brake: when luminal pH falls below ~2.5, D cells secrete **somatostatin** which inhibits gastrin (and acid secretion directly). Above pH 1.0 gastrin secretion ceases.
  - *Intestinal*: chyme entering the duodenum increases osmolality, distends the wall, and (when fatty) triggers **enterogastrones** (CCK, secretin, GIP, GLP-1, somatostatin, PYY). Net effect: inhibition of gastric motility and secretion to give the duodenum time to process the load.
- **Incretins** are gut hormones (GIP, GLP-1) that potentiate glucose-stimulated insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells; they only act once blood glucose is rising, preventing inappropriate insulin release at low glucose. GIP = glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide; GLP-1 is the basis of modern type-2 diabetes drugs (Ch 19).
- **Enteric nervous system** has ~500 million neurons (about as many as the spinal cord) with intrinsic afferents, interneurons, and motor neurons in the myenteric (motor) and submucosal (secretory) plexuses. **Intrinsic afferents (~100 M)** outnumber **extrinsic afferents (~50 K)** ~2000-fold, emphasizing local autonomy.
- **Peristalsis schematic (Fig 18.31)**: a bolus triggers intrinsic afferents → interneurons → motor neurons; **excitation behind the bolus** via ACh and substance P (contraction); **inhibition in front** via NO and VIP (relaxation).
- **Enteric paracrines**: ECL cells secrete serotonin (5-HT) and motilin (contraction). **Guanylin** and **uroguanylin** activate guanylate cyclase → cGMP → CFTR-mediated Cl⁻/water secretion.
- **Pancreatic and bile reflexes** (post-meal):
  - Chyme arrival → duodenal mucosa secretes **CCK** (in response to fat + partial protein) and **secretin** (in response to pH < 4.5).
  - **CCK** → contraction of gallbladder, secretion of pancreatic juice enzymes, inhibition of gastric motility; possibly mediated indirectly through vagal ACh.
  - **Secretin** → bile-duct and pancreatic-ductule bicarbonate + water secretion (neutralizes acidic chyme; provides pH optimum for pancreatic enzymes).
  - Recent: secretin also stimulates brown-adipose-tissue metabolism (postprandial thermogenesis; Ch 19).
- **Intestinal reflexes**: gastroileal (↑gastric → ↑ileal motility & ileocecal passage); ileogastric (ileal distension → ↓gastric motility); intestino-intestinal (overdistention → relaxation throughout).
- **Trophic effects**: structure of gastric mucosa depends on **gastrin**; structure of exocrine pancreas depends on **CCK**. GI hormones maintain target-organ trophism, which may be their primary function; this is why both atrophy during starvation.

## Section 18.7 — Digestion and Absorption of Food (pp. 651–657)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

  - **Absorption of Lipids** (pp. 654–655)
  - **Transport of Lipids in the Blood** (pp. 655–656)

### Core claims
- Typical American diet: ~50% kcal from carbohydrate, ~16% from protein, balance from lipid.
- **Carbohydrate digestion**:
  - Salivary amylase begins starch hydrolysis in the mouth; inactivated by gastric acid.
  - **Pancreatic amylase** in the duodenum cleaves straight chains of starch into **maltose + maltriose + oligosaccharides** (cannot hydrolyze α-1,6 branch points).
  - **Brush-border disaccharidases** (sucrase, maltase, lactase) finish the job → glucose, fructose, galactose.
  - **Glucose absorption** = secondary active transport with 2 Na⁺ on apical SGLT1; basolateral exit via facilitated diffusion through GLUT2; powered by the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase. Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + water absorbed alongside (paracellular).
- **Protein digestion**:
  - Begins in stomach with pepsin → polypeptides (and some free amino acids). Pepsin digestion is not essential; gastrectomy patients still digest protein in the small intestine.
  - **Pancreatic endopeptidases** trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase cleave internal bonds. **Carboxypeptidase** (pancreatic) and **aminopeptidase** (brush border) are exopeptidases.
  - Products = free amino acids + dipeptides + tripeptides. Amino acids enter via Na⁺-coupled secondary active transporters; di- and tripeptides enter via a **H⁺-coupled** transporter, then are hydrolyzed intracellularly to free AAs.
  - In adults, only free AAs cross the basolateral membrane into the portal vein. Newborns can absorb intact proteins (including maternal antibodies in colostrum).
- **Lipid digestion** (Fig 18.35):
  - Up to 30% of fat digestion may begin in the mouth/stomach via lingual/gastric lipases, but the bulk occurs in the duodenum.
  - **Step 1 — emulsification**: bile-salt micelles break large fat droplets into small emulsified droplets (greater surface area).
  - **Step 2 — lipolysis**: **pancreatic lipase** (aided by **colipase**, which anchors lipase to the droplet) cleaves two of the three fatty acids from each triglyceride, yielding **free fatty acids + monoglycerides**. Phospholipase A cleaves fatty acids from lecithin to make lysolecithin.
  - **Step 3 — mixed micelles**: free fatty acids, monoglycerides, and lysolecithin enter the micelles of bile salts/lecithin/cholesterol, which ferry the lipids to the brush border.
- **Lipid absorption** (Fig 18.36):
  - Fatty acids and monoglycerides diffuse out of micelles into enterocytes.
  - Short-chain fatty acids (from microbiota) can exit directly into the portal vein.
  - Long-chain fatty acids are resynthesized to **triglycerides** in the smooth ER, packaged with phospholipids, cholesterol, and apolipoproteins into **chylomicrons**, and secreted by exocytosis into the **central lacteal** → thoracic duct → left subclavian vein.
- **Plasma lipoproteins** (Table 18.8): **chylomicrons** carry dietary lipids; **VLDL** carries endogenous (liver) triglycerides; **LDL** delivers cholesterol to peripheral organs (and into atheromas); **HDL** removes cholesterol back to the liver. Lipoprotein lipase on capillary endothelium (esp. muscle, adipose) hydrolyzes chylomicron and VLDL triglycerides to release fatty acids to tissues; chylomicron remnants are cleared by the liver.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Alimentary tract / GI tract** — ~9 m tube from mouth to anus; lumen continuous with environment.
- **Four tunics** — mucosa, submucosa, muscularis (externa), serosa.
- **Submucosal (Meissner's) and myenteric (Auerbach's) plexuses** — enteric/autonomic nerve nets in submucosa and between muscle layers.
- **Enteric nervous system (ENS)** — intrinsic gut "brain" of ~500 M neurons.
- **Peristalsis** — propulsive wave; circular muscle contracts behind bolus, relaxes ahead.
- **Segmentation** — local mixing contractions paced by slow waves.
- **Interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC)** — pacemaker cells of intestinal smooth muscle; generate slow waves.
- **Lower esophageal sphincter** — physiologic (not anatomic) sphincter; incompetence → GERD.
- **Parietal cell** — HCl + intrinsic factor secretion via apical H⁺/K⁺ ATPase.
- **Chief cell** — pepsinogen secretion.
- **G cell / gastrin** — antral endocrine cell; main hormonal driver of HCl secretion (via ECL/histamine).
- **ECL cell / histamine** — paracrine driver of parietal-cell HCl.
- **D cell / somatostatin** — pH-triggered brake on gastrin secretion.
- **P/D1 cell / ghrelin** — preprandial appetite signal.
- **Intrinsic factor** — gastric glycoprotein required for ileal vitamin B₁₂ absorption.
- **Pepsin / pepsinogen** — acid-activated protein endopeptidase.
- **H. pylori** — bacterial cause of most peptic ulcers (Nobel 2005).
- **Plicae circulares / villi / microvilli** — three nested surface amplifications.
- **Brush border enzymes** — membrane-bound disaccharidases, peptidases, phosphatases.
- **Enterokinase (enteropeptidase)** — brush-border activator of trypsinogen.
- **Paneth cells** — antimicrobial (defensins, lysozyme) crypt-base cells.
- **Intestinal microbiota** — ~10¹⁴ commensal organisms in the colon; > 1.5 kg biomass.
- **Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)** — acetate, propionate, butyrate; fermentation products that fuel colonocytes and signal systemically.
- **Taeniae coli / haustra / diverticula** — longitudinal bands, pouches, and abnormal outpouchings of the colon.
- **Hepatic portal vein** — drains GI capillaries to liver sinusoids; capillaries → vein → capillaries.
- **Portal triad** — peripheral set of branch of hepatic portal vein + hepatic artery + bile ductule.
- **Hepatocyte / sinusoid / Kupffer cell** — main liver parenchyma / fenestrated capillary / resident macrophage.
- **Enterohepatic circulation** — intestine → portal vein → liver → bile → intestine recycling loop.
- **Bilirubin / urobilinogen / stercobilin** — heme-derived pigments; conjugated bilirubin is the biliary form.
- **Bile salts** — amphipathic conjugates of cholic / chenodeoxycholic acid; emulsify fats; recycled ileal.
- **Cytochrome P450 (CYP)** — hepatic enzymes for drug & steroid metabolism.
- **Gallbladder / cystic duct / common bile duct** — bile storage and delivery; CCK-driven contraction.
- **Pancreatic acini vs. ductules** — enzymes vs. bicarbonate (CFTR-dependent) secretion.
- **Zymogens** — inactive enzyme precursors; trypsin activates the rest.
- **Pancreatic trypsin inhibitor** — protects pancreas from premature trypsin activity.
- **Cephalic / gastric / intestinal phases** — three control phases of gastric secretion.
- **Enterogastrone** — duodenal hormone that inhibits gastric activity (CCK, secretin, GIP, GLP-1).
- **Incretin** — gut hormone (GIP, GLP-1) that potentiates glucose-stimulated insulin secretion.
- **CCK / secretin** — duodenal hormones; CCK triggers gallbladder + pancreatic enzymes; secretin triggers bicarbonate.
- **Chylomicron / VLDL / LDL / HDL** — dietary fat carrier / liver TG carrier / cholesterol delivery / reverse cholesterol transport.
- **Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) / lipoprotein lipase** — chylomicron membrane protein / endothelial enzyme that liberates fatty acids.
- **GERD / Barrett esophagus / hiatal hernia** — reflux pathology spectrum.
- **CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator)** — Cl⁻ channel; pancreatic & colonic secretion.
