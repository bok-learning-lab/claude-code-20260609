## Section 6.1 — Extracellular Environment (pp. 129–131)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Body Fluids** (p. 129)
- **Extracellular Matrix** (pp. 129–130)
- **Categories of Transport Across the Plasma Membrane** (pp. 130–131)

### Core claims
- **Body water:** ~67% intracellular, ~33% extracellular. Of the extracellular pool, ~20% is **plasma** (vascular) and ~80% is **interstitial fluid** (within tissues), contained in the gel of the extracellular matrix. Interstitial fluid is continuously formed from and returns to plasma (mechanisms in Ch 14).
- **Extracellular matrix (ECM):** protein fibers — **collagen** (≈ 15 types; collagen IV forms the **basement membrane** under epithelia) and **elastin** — embedded in a **ground substance** of **glycoproteins** and **proteoglycans** (formerly mucopolysaccharides) that bind water as a hydrated gel. Analogy: "reinforcing iron bars in concrete."
- **Integrins:** transmembrane glycoproteins that bridge cytoskeleton (inside) to ECM (outside). They function as adhesion molecules and relay signals between the two compartments — hence the name. They confer cell polarity (apical vs. basal), affect adhesion, motility, and proliferation, and deliver growth factors to cell-surface receptors.
- **Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs):** zinc-dependent peptidases that digest ECM proteins. Required for **tissue remodeling** (development, wound healing) and **phagocyte migration**. Disregulated MMPs contribute to **tumor metastasis** (by digesting basement-membrane proteins) and to arthritis, cardiovascular, and neural disease. **TIMPs** are endogenous MMP inhibitors.
- **Plasma membrane** is **selectively permeable**. Five transport categories are organized along two cross-cutting axes:
  - *Carrier-mediated vs. non-carrier-mediated.*
  - *Passive (no ATP, down gradient) vs. active (ATP, against gradient).*
- Five categories (Fig 6.2 list):
  1. **Carrier-mediated transport:** (a) facilitated diffusion (passive), (b) active transport.
  2. **Non-carrier-mediated transport:** (a) simple diffusion of lipid-soluble molecules through the bilayer, (b) simple diffusion of ions through membrane channels, (c) osmosis (water) through aquaporins.

### Core claims
- **Diffusion** is the net random movement of solute from higher to lower concentration, driven by thermal energy, terminating at equilibrium. Per second law of thermodynamics, it converts low-entropy (segregated) to high-entropy (uniform) states.
- **Mean diffusion time scales with the square of distance.** Approximate values: ~10⁻⁷ s across a 10-nm plasma membrane, ~1.6 × 10⁻⁶ s across a 40-nm synapse, ~1–2 × 10⁻³ s across the 1–2 μm air–blood barrier in the lung. Beyond ~100 μm, diffusion is too slow to sustain exchange — hence the ~100-μm capillary spacing in most organs and the need for **axonal transport** in long neurons.
- **Diffusion through the plasma membrane:**
  - **Nonpolar / lipid-soluble** molecules (O₂, steroid hormones) cross the bilayer freely.
  - Small, polar but uncharged molecules (CO₂, ethanol, urea) also penetrate.
  - **Water** crosses to a limited degree by itself but mainly through **aquaporins**.
  - **Charged inorganic ions** (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) require ion channels, many of which are **gated** (e.g., voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels of axons; Ch 7).
- **Cystic fibrosis (Clinical Application):** mutations in **CFTR** (a chloride channel of the apical epithelial membrane) produce salty sweat and viscous mucus in lung and pancreas; ~2,000 known mutations; frequency ~1/2,500 births in Northern European populations.
- **Rate of diffusion** depends on: (1) concentration gradient, (2) membrane permeability, (3) temperature, (4) membrane surface area (enhanced by microvilli in intestine and kidney tubule). At rest, a neuron's plasma membrane is ~20× more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺.
- **Osmosis** = net diffusion of *water* across a selectively permeable membrane from the dilute (high water concentration) to the concentrated (low water concentration) side. Requires (1) a solute concentration difference, (2) membrane more permeable to water than to at least one solute. Solutes restricted by the membrane are **osmotically active**.
- **Osmotic pressure** is the force just sufficient to oppose osmosis; it scales with solute concentration. Animal cells lack rigid walls, so cells in pure water burst (hemolysis).
- **Molarity vs. molality:**
  - **Molarity (M):** moles solute per liter of solution (volume-based; depends on solute identity).
  - **Molality (m):** moles solute per kilogram of water (mass-based; cleanest for osmosis).
  - **Osmolality (Osm):** sum of molalities of all dissolved particles, *accounting for ionization*. NaCl ionizes 1 → 2, so 1 m NaCl = 2 Osm; 0.15 m NaCl ≈ 0.3 Osm ≈ plasma. Glucose at 1 m = 1 Osm; 0.3 m glucose ≈ plasma (5% dextrose = 0.3 m glucose).
- **Freezing-point depression:** 1 Osm depresses freezing point by **−1.86 °C**. Plasma freezes at ≈ −0.56 °C → osmolality = 0.56/1.86 ≈ 0.3 Osm = **300 mOsm**.
- **Tonicity** describes the effect of a solution on cell volume, *given* membrane permeability:
  - **Isotonic** = same effective osmotic pressure as plasma → no net water movement (NS 0.9% NaCl, D5W). Note: 0.3 m **urea** is iso-osmotic but *not* isotonic — urea penetrates the membrane and equilibrates, so intracellular osmotic activity pulls water in and bursts the cell.
  - **Hypotonic** → water enters cells → swelling/hemolysis.
  - **Hypertonic** → water leaves cells → shrinkage/**crenation**.
- **Clinical Application — plasma proteins and edema:** capillary pores exclude plasma proteins, so plasma has higher protein osmolality than interstitial fluid; this colloid osmotic gradient draws water back into capillaries. If plasma albumin falls (e.g., **cirrhosis**), water accumulates in tissues → **edema**.
- **Clinical Application — IV fluids:** **normal saline** and **5% dextrose** are isotonic; **Ringer's lactate** adds K⁺, Ca²⁺, lactate to better resemble plasma; **mannitol** is given hypertonically to draw water from edematous brain.
- **Regulation of plasma osmolality** (defended within 1–3%): osmoreceptors in the **hypothalamus** (Ch 8) sense rising plasma osmolality during dehydration, shrink, and fire more impulses → (1) **thirst** → drinking; (2) **ADH (vasopressin)** release from the posterior pituitary → water retention by kidneys. Negative-feedback loop restores blood volume and lowers plasma osmolality.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Net diffusion of a molecule or an ion through a membrane always occurs in the direction of its lower concentration." (p. 131)
- > "The mean diffusion time increases very rapidly with the square of the distance that the diffusing molecules or ions must travel." (p. 132)
- > "In most membranes, however, the passage of water is greatly aided by specific water channels (called aquaporins) that are inserted into the membrane in response to physiological regulation." (p. 133)

- > "Because plasma freezes at about −0.56 °C, its osmolality is equal to 0.56 ÷ 1.86 = 0.3 Osm, which is more commonly indicated as 300 milliosmolal (or 300 mOsm)." (p. 138)

### Equations / quantitative

**Mean diffusion time scaling:**
$$t \sim \frac{x^2}{2D}$$
(distance-squared scaling cited in the text; D is the diffusion coefficient.)

**Osmolality of a solution** (sum over species, accounting for ionization):
$$\mathrm{Osm} = \sum_i n_i \cdot m_i$$
where $n_i$ is the number of dissociated particles per formula unit and $m_i$ is molality.

**Freezing-point depression:**
$$\Delta T_f = -1.86\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C/Osm} \times \mathrm{Osm}$$

**Plasma osmolality estimate:**
$$\mathrm{Osm}_{\mathrm{plasma}} = \frac{0.56\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}}{1.86\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C/Osm}} \approx 0.30\,\mathrm{Osm} = 300\,\mathrm{mOsm}$$

## Section 6.3 — Carrier-Mediated Transport (pp. 140–147)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

- **Active Transport** (pp. 142–145)
  - **The Ca²⁺ Pump** (p. 143)
  - **The Sodium-Potassium Pump** (pp. 143–144)
  - **Secondary Active Transport (Coupled Transport)** (pp. 144–145)
- **Transport Across Epithelial Membranes** (pp. 145–146)

### Core claims
- **Three properties common to enzymes and carrier proteins:** (1) **specificity** (glucose carriers do not move other monosaccharides; particular AA carriers move only particular AAs); (2) **competition** (two substrates that share a carrier slow each other's transport); (3) **saturation** (rate plateaus at a **transport maximum, Tm**; above Tm, additional substrate cannot be transported).
- **Diabetes example of saturation:** in hyperglycemia of diabetes mellitus, the kidney glucose reabsorption carriers (SGLT2) are exceeded → unreabsorbed glucose appears in urine → **glycosuria**.
- **Facilitated diffusion** is **passive** (no ATP), with carrier specificity/competition/saturation. Glucose enters most cells via the **GLUT family** of transporters:
  - **GLUT1** — most brain regions; basal (insulin-independent) supply.
  - **GLUT2** — pancreatic β-cells (insulin secretion) and hepatocytes; high-capacity.
  - **GLUT3** — major neuronal transporter.
  - **GLUT4** — adipose and skeletal muscle; **insulin-** and **exercise-regulated** insertion from intracellular vesicles into the plasma membrane (analogous to exocytosis but no secretion). Central to insulin's action and to glucose homeostasis (Ch 12, 19).
- **Hypoglycemia** (Clinical Application): low plasma glucose reduces the gradient for facilitated diffusion into neurons; severe hypoglycemia (e.g., **insulin shock** in diabetics) can cause unconsciousness or death. Exercise can produce milder hypoglycemia by GLUT4 insertion.
- Fatty acids, long thought to enter cells by simple diffusion, also have **carrier-mediated facilitative diffusion** transporters, with insulin- and exercise-regulated insertion in muscle (analogous to GLUT4).
- **Active transport** moves substances *against* their concentration gradient and requires **ATP hydrolysis**. Active-transport carriers are called **pumps**. Cyanide (blocks oxidative phosphorylation) stops active transport but not passive transport.
- **Primary active transport** = ATP hydrolysis directly powers the carrier. The major pumps are:
  - **Ca²⁺ pump:** in plasma membrane (extrudes Ca²⁺) and ER membrane (sequesters Ca²⁺ in cisternae). Maintains intracellular [Ca²⁺] ~1,000–10,000× lower than extracellular. Opening Ca²⁺ channels then produces rapid signaling rises in cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ (for neurotransmitter release, muscle contraction, Ch 7, 12).
  - **H⁺ pump:** parietal-cell gastric H⁺/K⁺ ATPase; produces gastric acid (Ch 18).
  - **Na⁺/K⁺ pump** (Fig 6.19): the master ATPase that pumps **3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in** per ATP hydrolyzed. ~200 pumps per RBC, ~35,000 per WBC, several million per kidney tubule cell. The 3:2 stoichiometry is **electrogenic** — contributes ~3 mV to the negative interior charge.
- **Three roles of the Na⁺/K⁺ pump gradient:**
  1. Energy source for **coupled transport** (secondary active transport).
  2. Substrate for **action potentials** in nerve, muscle, and heart (Ch 7, 12, 13).
  3. Osmotic stabilization — without the pump, intracellular Na⁺ would rise, drawing water in and lysing cells.
- **Secondary active transport (coupled transport):** the *downhill* movement of Na⁺ down its electrochemical gradient supplies energy to move a second substrate *uphill*. No ATP is hydrolyzed directly by the secondary carrier — but ATP is still required *indirectly* to maintain the Na⁺ gradient via the Na⁺/K⁺ pump.
  - **Cotransport / symport:** both substances move into the cell (e.g., **SGLT1** in small intestine, 2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose; **SGLT2** in kidney proximal tubule, 1 Na⁺ : 1 glucose).
  - **Countertransport / antiport:** Na⁺ in, second species out (e.g., Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger; Na⁺/H⁺ pump).
- **Epithelial transport polarity** (Fig 6.21): glucose absorption across the intestinal or renal tubular epithelium requires:
  1. **Basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ pumps** maintain low intracellular Na⁺.
  2. **Apical Na⁺-glucose cotransport (SGLT)** uses the Na⁺ gradient to pull glucose into the cell against its own gradient.
  3. **Basolateral GLUT facilitated diffusion** lets glucose exit into blood down its gradient.
- **Oral rehydration therapy (Clinical Application):** because SGLT1 absorbs 2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose, oral solutions containing both salt and glucose (with osmolality ~245 mOsm) drive intestinal Na⁺ and water absorption — even when intravenous fluids are unavailable. ORT saves more than a million children per year from cholera and other diarrheal disease.
- **Paracellular transport** is limited by **junctional complexes** (Fig 6.22) consisting of:
  - **Tight junctions (zonula occludens)** — strands of integral proteins occluding the intercellular space; selectively leaky in some epithelia.
  - **Belt desmosomes (zonula adherens)** — circumferential adhesion belts joined to the cytoskeleton.
  - **Desmosomes (macula adherens)** — spot-weld adhesions joined to the cytoskeleton.
- **Bulk transport** = endocytosis and exocytosis (Fig 6.23):
  - **Endocytosis** — invagination → pouch → vesicle pinching off, importing many molecules at once. **Receptor-mediated endocytosis** (e.g., LDL cholesterol uptake by liver and arterial cells via the LDL receptor) is selective.
  - **Exocytosis** — fusion of intracellular vesicles with the plasma membrane, releasing their contents (neurotransmitters, hormones, digestive enzymes).
  - Brain capillaries are joined by tight junctions, so substances must traverse the endothelium **transcellularly** via channels, carriers, or vesicles — the cellular basis of the **blood–brain barrier** (Ch 7, 8).

### Equations / quantitative

**Na⁺/K⁺ pump stoichiometry (per ATP):**
$$3\,\mathrm{Na^+_{in}} + 2\,\mathrm{K^+_{out}} + \mathrm{ATP} \to 3\,\mathrm{Na^+_{out}} + 2\,\mathrm{K^+_{in}} + \mathrm{ADP} + \mathrm{P_i}$$

$$2\,\mathrm{Na^+_{out}} + 1\,\mathrm{glucose_{out}} \to 2\,\mathrm{Na^+_{in}} + 1\,\mathrm{glucose_{in}}$$

**SGLT2 (kidney) cotransport stoichiometry:**
$$1\,\mathrm{Na^+_{out}} + 1\,\mathrm{glucose_{out}} \to 1\,\mathrm{Na^+_{in}} + 1\,\mathrm{glucose_{in}}$$

## Section 6.4 — The Membrane Potential (pp. 147–151)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Equilibrium Potentials** (pp. 148–149)
  - **Nernst Equation** (pp. 149–150)
- **Resting Membrane Potential** (pp. 150–151)
  - **Role of the Na⁺/K⁺ Pumps** (p. 151)

### Core claims
- The cell behaves as a tiny battery: interior negative, exterior positive, with magnitude (in mV) determined by the unequal distribution of charges.
- **Origin of the unequal distribution:**
  1. **Fixed intracellular anions** (proteins, phosphate groups of ATP and other organic ions) cannot cross the membrane → attract permeant cations inward.
  2. The membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ (~20× at rest) → K⁺ accumulates inside in response to the fixed anions, but its outward concentration gradient eventually counterbalances the inward electrical pull.
  3. The **Na⁺/K⁺ pump** (3:2) builds and maintains the Na⁺ and K⁺ concentration gradients, and adds ~3 mV of electrogenic interior negativity.
- **Equilibrium potentials** (the voltage at which net flow of a single permeant ion is zero):
  - **EK ≈ −90 mV.** At this voltage, the inward electrical force on K⁺ exactly balances the outward chemical gradient (K⁺ ≈ 150 mM in, 5 mM out → 30× ratio).
  - **ENa ≈ +66 mV.** Na⁺ is 145 mM out, 12 mM in. A positive interior voltage would be needed to oppose inward Na⁺ diffusion.
- **Nernst equation** (at body temperature):
$$E_X = \frac{61\,\mathrm{mV}}{z} \log\frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i}$$
- **Resting membrane potential (RMP)** of real cells lies between EK and ENa, much closer to EK because membrane permeability to K⁺ ≫ Na⁺. Typical RMP: −65 to −85 mV; **neurons average −70 mV**; cardiac muscle ≈ −85 mV.
- **Why RMP is not exactly EK:** small inward Na⁺ leak makes the cell slightly less negative than EK; this also drives small outward K⁺ leak; both leaks are continuously countered by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump.
- **Two implications:**
  1. Changes in extracellular ion concentration shift RMP — most strongly for K⁺, because permeability to K⁺ is highest.
  2. Changes in membrane permeability to any ion shift RMP — basis of action potentials (Ch 7).
- **Hyperkalemia / hypokalemia (Clinical Application):** because cardiac RMP depends on the K⁺ gradient (the [K⁺]o/[K⁺]i term in the Nernst equation), plasma K⁺ is tightly defended (3.5–5.0 mEq/L):
  - **Hyperkalemia** (↑ plasma K⁺) → less-negative RMP → ECG abnormalities; > 8 mEq/L stops the heart (the principle of lethal-injection **KCl**).
  - **Hypokalemia** (↓ plasma K⁺) from diuretic use also produces ECG abnormalities.

### Equations / quantitative

## Section 6.5 — Cell Signaling (pp. 151–155)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Second Messengers** (pp. 152–153)
- **G-Proteins** (pp. 153–155)

### Core claims
- Cells communicate either by direct cytoplasmic coupling (**gap junctions** — Ch 7) or by chemical signaling into the extracellular environment.
- **Three categories of extracellular chemical signaling** (Fig 6.29):
  1. **Paracrine** — local; regulator diffuses through ECM to nearby target cells in the same organ. (**Autocrine** is the special case where the secreting cell is also the target.)
  2. **Synaptic** — neurotransmitter released by an axon terminal into a tiny synaptic cleft, acting on a postsynaptic cell.
  3. **Endocrine** — hormones secreted into blood, acting on distant target cells that express the relevant receptor.
- **Receptor proteins** are highly specific. Total receptor diversity in humans is estimated at ~30,000 different types across ~200 cell types, generated by ~400–500 G-protein-coupled receptor genes (plus several hundred more in olfaction and taste), alternative splicing, and post-translational modification.
- **Receptor location** is determined by ligand polarity:
  - **Nonpolar / lipid-soluble** ligands (steroid hormones, thyroid hormones, NO gas) cross the membrane → receptors are **intracellular** (cytoplasm or nucleus).
  - **Polar / large** ligands (epinephrine, ACh, polypeptide hormones, insulin) cannot cross → receptors are **on the outer surface of the plasma membrane**.
- **Second messengers** bridge the extracellular signal to intracellular machinery for polar ligands:
  - **Ca²⁺** entering from outside or released from ER stores.
  - **Cyclic AMP (cAMP)** produced from ATP by **adenylate cyclase**.
  - Others (cGMP, IP₃, DAG) appear in Ch 11.
- **General cAMP pathway:** (1) polar ligand binds receptor → (2) receptor (via G-proteins) activates adenylate cyclase → (3) cAMP rises in cytoplasm → (4) cAMP activates **protein kinase**, which phosphorylates downstream targets to produce the cellular response.
- **G-proteins** (1994 Nobel) are heterotrimeric (α, β, γ subunits) and shuttle between receptors and effector proteins (enzymes or ion channels):
  1. At rest: αβγ aggregated at the unbound receptor; α binds **GDP**.
  2. Ligand binds receptor → α exchanges GDP for **GTP** → α dissociates from βγ.
  3. α (or βγ) diffuses through membrane to the effector → activates or inactivates it.
  4. α-bound GTP is hydrolyzed to GDP + Pᵢ → α and βγ reaggregate at the receptor → cycle resets.
- G-protein-coupled receptors are the largest receptor superfamily and the targets of a large fraction of clinically used drugs. Their downstream effects vary (cAMP, ion channels, second messengers) and are detailed in later chapters.

- > "Scientists have estimated that the 200 different cell types found in the human body may have as many as 30,000 different types of receptor proteins for different regulatory molecules." (p. 152)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Extracellular matrix (ECM)** — collagen, elastin, proteoglycans, glycoproteins; structural scaffold of connective tissues.
- **Basement membrane** — collagen-IV-rich layer under epithelia; passport for epithelial polarity.
- **Integrin** — transmembrane glycoprotein linking cytoskeleton to ECM; signaling and adhesion.
- **Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) / TIMP** — Zn²⁺-dependent ECM-digesting enzyme / its endogenous inhibitor.
- **Selectively permeable membrane** — allows some species through and excludes others.
- **Simple diffusion** — net random movement of solute down its concentration gradient; no carrier.
- **Aquaporin** — water channel; constitutive in some cells, ADH-regulated in others (kidney).
- **Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR)** — apical Cl⁻ channel; mutated in cystic fibrosis.
- **Osmosis** — net diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane.
- **Osmotic pressure** — pressure required to stop osmosis; proportional to total solute concentration.
- **Molarity (M)** / **molality (m)** — moles solute per L of solution / per kg of water.
- **Osmolality (Osm)** — sum of molalities of all dissolved particles, after ionization.
- **Tonicity** — effect of a solution on cell volume given membrane permeability; isotonic, hypotonic, hypertonic.
- **Hemolysis / crenation** — cell bursting in hypotonic / shrinking in hypertonic solutions.
- **Osmoreceptor** — hypothalamic neuron sensing plasma osmolality; drives ADH and thirst.
- **ADH (antidiuretic hormone, vasopressin)** — posterior pituitary peptide; promotes renal water retention.
- **Carrier protein** — membrane protein that ferries a substrate by conformational change.
- **Specificity / competition / saturation / Tm** — defining features of carrier-mediated transport.
- **Facilitated diffusion** — passive, carrier-mediated transport down a gradient.
- **GLUT1/GLUT2/GLUT3/GLUT4** — facilitated-diffusion glucose transporters; GLUT4 is insulin/exercise-regulated.
- **Active transport / pump** — uses ATP to move substrate against its gradient.
- **Ca²⁺ pump** — primary active pump extruding Ca²⁺ or sequestering it in ER.
- **Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase)** — primary active pump; 3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in per ATP; electrogenic.
- **Secondary active transport** — coupled transport driven by a primary ion gradient; ATP-dependent indirectly.
- **Cotransport / countertransport** — symport (same direction) / antiport (opposite directions).
- **SGLT1 / SGLT2** — Na⁺-glucose cotransporters in intestine (2:1) and kidney (1:1).
- **Absorption / reabsorption** — net transport into blood from intestinal lumen / from urinary filtrate.
- **Transcellular / paracellular transport** — through cells / between cells.
- **Junctional complex** — tight junction (zonula occludens), belt desmosome (zonula adherens), desmosome (macula adherens).
- **Endocytosis / exocytosis** — bulk import by vesicle formation / bulk export by vesicle fusion.
- **Receptor-mediated endocytosis** — selective import via receptor binding (e.g., LDL receptor).
- **Fixed anion** — non-permeant intracellular organic anion (protein, ATP phosphate).
- **Equilibrium potential (EX)** — voltage at which net flow of ion X is zero (Nernst).
- **Nernst equation** — EX = (61/z) log [X]o/[X]i at 37 °C.
- **EK / ENa** — ~−90 mV / ~+66 mV.
- **Resting membrane potential (RMP)** — neuron −70 mV; cardiac −85 mV.
- **Electrogenic pump** — Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase's net charge-separation contribution (~3 mV).
- **Hyperkalemia / hypokalemia** — high / low plasma K⁺; ECG-relevant.
- **Paracrine / synaptic / endocrine signaling** — local diffusion / cleft transmission / bloodborne hormone.
- **Second messenger** — intracellular signal (cAMP, Ca²⁺) generated by receptor activation.
- **G-protein** — heterotrimeric (α, β, γ) GTPase coupling receptor to effector.
- **G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR)** — seven-transmembrane receptor family; largest receptor superfamily.
