# Chapter 6 — Interactions Between Cells and the Extracellular Environment

> pp. 128–159 · Authors: Fox & Rompolski

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **6.1 Extracellular Environment** (pp. 129–130) — intracellular vs. extracellular fluid compartments; plasma vs. interstitial fluid; the extracellular matrix (collagen, elastin, glycoproteins, proteoglycans); integrins; matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs); categories of transport across the plasma membrane (carrier-mediated vs. non-carrier; passive vs. active).
2. **6.2 Diffusion and Osmosis** (pp. 131–139) — net diffusion down a concentration gradient; the four determinants of diffusion rate; diffusion through phospholipid bilayer (nonpolar) vs. ion channels vs. aquaporins; osmosis as net diffusion of water; molarity vs. molality vs. osmolality; freezing-point depression measurement of osmolality; tonicity (isotonic, hypotonic, hypertonic); 5% dextrose and normal saline; osmoreceptor / ADH / thirst negative feedback regulation of plasma osmolality.

4. **6.4 The Membrane Potential** (pp. 147–151) — fixed intracellular anions; equilibrium potentials EK and ENa; the Nernst equation (simplified, 37 °C); resting membrane potential (RMP) between EK and ENa, dominated by higher K⁺ permeability; electrogenic contribution of the Na⁺/K⁺ pump.
5. **6.5 Cell Signaling** (pp. 151–155) — paracrine, autocrine, synaptic, and endocrine signaling; receptor-protein location (membrane for polar regulators; intracellular for steroid, thyroid hormones, and NO); second messengers (Ca²⁺, cAMP); G-protein cycle; ~400–500 G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs).

## Section 6.1 — Extracellular Environment (pp. 129–130)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Body Fluids** (p. 129)
- **Extracellular Matrix** (p. 129)
- **Categories of Transport Across the Plasma Membrane** (pp. 130–131)

### Core claims
- **Fluid compartments:** ~67% of total body water is **intracellular**; ~33% is **extracellular**. Of the extracellular pool, ~20% is **blood plasma** (intravascular) and the remaining ~80% is **interstitial fluid** in the connective-tissue spaces.
- All molecular traffic between blood and cells must pass *first* through the interstitial fluid and *then* the plasma membrane. The interstitial fluid is continuously formed from plasma at the capillaries and continuously returned to plasma (mechanism deferred to Ch 14).
- **Extracellular matrix (ECM)** consists of:
  - **Protein fibers** — *collagen* (strength; ~15 types; collagen IV in basement membranes) and *elastin* (recoil).
  - **Ground substance** — a hydrated gel of **glycoproteins** and **proteoglycans** (formerly "mucopolysaccharides"; polysaccharide-rich, water-bound).
  - **Basement membrane** — collagen IV + glycoproteins; "weds" epithelium to underlying connective tissue.
- **Integrins** are glycoproteins that span the plasma membrane from the cytoskeleton into the ECM. They act as cell-ECM adhesion proteins, impart cell polarity, control motility and proliferation, and relay signals bidirectionally between the intra- and extracellular compartments (the name fuses *integrate* + *protein*).
- **Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)** are zinc-dependent peptidases that remodel ECM proteins. Essential in development, wound healing, and leukocyte migration. Pathological MMP activity contributes to **tumor metastasis** (cancer cells digest the basement membrane to enter blood and lymph), arthritis, and cardiovascular/neural disease. **TIMPs** inhibit MMPs; MMP/TIMP imbalance underlies several pathologies.
- The **plasma membrane** is **selectively permeable.** It is freely impermeable to proteins and nucleic acids, but permits the two-way traffic of small nutrients, wastes, and certain ions.
- **Transport categories** (two orthogonal classifications):
  - Carrier-mediated (facilitated diffusion, active transport) **vs.** non-carrier-mediated (simple diffusion through the bilayer; ion-channel diffusion; aquaporin-mediated diffusion of water).
  - **Passive transport** = down the concentration gradient, no metabolic energy (includes all simple diffusion plus facilitated diffusion). **Active transport** = against the gradient; ATP-dependent; carriers are called **pumps**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Approximately 67% of the total body water is contained within cells, in the intracellular compartment. The remaining 33% of the total body water is found in the extracellular compartment." (p. 129)
- > "About 20% of this extracellular fluid is contained within the vessels of the cardiovascular system … the remaining 80% … is located outside of the vascular system, and makes up the extracellular fluid within the tissues, called the **interstitial fluid**." (p. 129)
- > "The cells that compose the organs of our body are embedded within the extracellular material of connective tissues. This material is called the **extracellular matrix**, and it consists of the protein fibers collagen and elastin … as well as gel-like ground substance." (p. 129)
- > "**Integrins** are a class of glycoproteins that extend from the cytoskeleton within a cell, through its plasma membrane, and into the extracellular matrix." (p. 130)
- > "The plasma membrane is generally not permeable to proteins, nucleic acids, and other molecules needed for the structure and function of the cell." (p. 130)
- > "**Passive transport** is the net movement of molecules and ions across a membrane from higher to lower concentration … it does not require metabolic energy. **Active transport** is the net movement of molecules and ions across a membrane from the region of lower to the region of higher concentration." (pp. 130–131)

### Core claims
- **Diffusion** is the net movement of solute from higher to lower concentration driven by random thermal motion; in thermodynamic terms it dissipates the concentration gradient (a low-entropy state) toward uniform distribution (maximum entropy).
- **Mean diffusion times** scale with the square of distance:
  - **~10⁻⁷ s** to cross a plasma membrane (~10 nm).
  - **~1.6 × 10⁻⁶ s** to cross a synapse (~40 nm).
  - **~1–2 × 10⁻³ s** to cross the two squamous cells separating air from blood in the lungs (1–2 μm).
  Beyond ~100 μm, simple diffusion is too slow; this is why cells in most organs sit within 100 μm of a capillary, and why neurons use axonal transport rather than diffusion to move material along axons up to a meter long.
- **What crosses the plasma membrane and how:**
  - **Nonpolar / lipid-soluble** (O₂, CO₂, steroid hormones, ethanol, urea) — through the phospholipid bilayer by simple diffusion.
  - **Water** — limited bilayer passage plus regulated diffusion through **aquaporin** channels (kidney, lung, eye, salivary gland, brain).
  - **Inorganic ions** — through selective **channel proteins**; some always open, many **gated** (open in response to voltage or ligand). The resting neuron is ~20× more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺.
  - **Larger polar molecules** (glucose, amino acids) — require carrier proteins; covered in §6.3.
- **Four determinants of diffusion rate** across a membrane:
  1. Magnitude of the concentration gradient.
  2. Permeability of the membrane.
  3. Temperature.
  4. Membrane surface area (amplified by **microvilli** in small intestine and kidney tubule epithelium).
- **Osmosis** = net diffusion of water (the *solvent*) across a selectively permeable membrane. Two requirements:
  1. A concentration difference of a solute on the two sides.
  2. The membrane must be relatively impermeable to that solute (the solute is then **osmotically active**).
- **Osmotic pressure** is the pressure that must be applied to *stop* osmosis. Greater solute concentration → greater osmotic pressure. Pure water has zero osmotic pressure. A 360 g/L glucose solution has twice the osmotic pressure of a 180 g/L glucose solution.
- **Molarity vs. molality:**
  - **1 M** = 1 mole of solute per liter of *solution*. Differs in effective water content depending on solute size.
  - **1 m** (molal) = 1 mole of solute per **kilogram of water** (≈ 1 L of water at 4 °C). Used for osmosis because the solute-to-water ratio is fixed.
- **Osmolality (Osm)** = total molality of *all* dissolved particles. **Electrolytes ionize**: 1 m NaCl = 2 Osm (1 m Na⁺ + 1 m Cl⁻). Plasma osmolality ≈ **300 mOsm**; about 88% of it comes from Na⁺ and its anions.
- **Freezing-point measurement** of osmolality: 1 mole/kg solute depresses freezing point by **−1.86 °C**. Plasma freezes at ≈ −0.56 °C, so plasma osmolality ≈ 0.56 / 1.86 ≈ 0.3 Osm = **300 mOsm**.
- **Tonicity** — describes whether a solution actually drives net water movement into or out of a cell:
  - **Isosmotic** but **isotonic** only if the solute cannot cross the membrane. A 0.3 m urea solution is isosmotic but **not isotonic** — urea crosses the membrane and RBCs in urea swell and burst.
  - **Hypotonic** → RBCs swell, hemolyze.
  - **Hypertonic** (e.g., seawater) → RBCs shrink and become **crenated** (notched).
  - **Clinical IV fluids:** **5% dextrose** (= 0.3 m glucose), **normal saline** (0.9% NaCl = 0.15 m, ionizes to 0.3 Osm), and **Ringer's lactate** (with Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺, Ca²⁺, lactate) are all isotonic. **Mannitol** is given when an *osmotically active* IV is desired (e.g., to draw water from edematous brain in cerebral edema).
- **Plasma proteins** stay in capillaries (they cannot pass the pores) and so create a colloid osmotic pressure that draws water back into the blood. **Hypoalbuminemia** (e.g., in cirrhosis) → **edema** (full mechanism in Ch 14).
- **Homeostasis of plasma osmolality (Fig 6.14):** maintained within 1–3%.
  - **Dehydration → ↑ plasma osmolality → osmoreceptors in hypothalamus shrink → thirst + ADH (vasopressin) release from posterior pituitary → drinking + kidney water retention → blood volume ↑, osmolality ↓ → negative feedback closes.**

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Net diffusion of a molecule or an ion through a membrane always occurs in the direction of its lower concentration." (p. 131)
- > "The mean diffusion time increases very rapidly with the square of the distance that the diffusing molecules or ions must travel." (p. 132)
- > "**Osmosis** is the net diffusion of water (the solvent) across the membrane. For osmosis to occur, the membrane must be selectively permeable; that is, it must be more permeable to water molecules than to at least one species of solute." (p. 134)
- > "The pressure needed to just stop osmosis is the **osmotic pressure** of the solution." (p. 135)
- > "A 1.0 m NaCl solution has a total concentration of 2.0 Osm." (p. 137)
- > "Because plasma freezes at about −0.56° C, its osmolality is equal to 0.56 ÷ 1.86 = 0.3 Osm, which is more commonly indicated as 300 milliosmolal (or 300 mOsm)." (p. 137)
- > "A 0.3 m urea solution, for example, is isosmotic but not isotonic because the plasma membrane is permeable to urea." (p. 138)
- > "Red blood cells placed in hypotonic solutions gain water and may burst — a process called **hemolysis**." (p. 138)
- > "ADH acts on the kidneys to promote water retention, so that a lower volume of more concentrated urine is excreted." (p. 140)

### Equations / quantitative

**Osmolality of a solution** = sum of molalities of all dissolved particles:
$$\mathrm{Osm} = \sum_{i} \nu_i \cdot m_i$$
where $\nu_i$ is the number of ions produced per formula unit (1 for glucose; 2 for NaCl; 3 for CaCl₂).

**Freezing-point depression as osmolality measurement:**
$$\Delta T_f = -1.86\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \cdot \mathrm{Osm} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathrm{Osm} = \frac{|\Delta T_f|}{1.86}$$

**Plasma osmolality from −0.56 °C freezing point:**
$$\mathrm{Osm_{plasma}} = \frac{0.56}{1.86} \approx 0.30\,\mathrm{Osm} = 300\,\mathrm{mOsm}$$

## Section 6.3 — Carrier-Mediated Transport (pp. 140–146)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Facilitated Diffusion** (pp. 141–142)
- **Active Transport** (pp. 142–146)
  - **The Ca²⁺ Pump** (p. 143)
  - **The Sodium-Potassium Pump** (pp. 143–144)
  - **Secondary Active Transport (Coupled Transport)** (pp. 144–145)
  - **Transport Across Epithelial Membranes** (pp. 145–146)
- **Bulk Transport** (pp. 146–147)

### Core claims
- **Carrier proteins** share three enzyme-like properties:
  1. **Specificity** — each carrier accepts only a narrow set of substrates.
  2. **Competition** — substrates that bind the same carrier slow each other's transport.
  3. **Saturation** — transport rate plateaus at a **transport maximum (Tm)**; further substrate cannot raise the rate.
- **Facilitated diffusion** moves a polar solute *down* its gradient via a carrier; **no ATP required**.
- **GLUT family (glucose facilitated-diffusion carriers):**
  - **GLUT1** — broad distribution including the brain and during pathological upregulation.
  - **GLUT2** — pancreatic β-cells (insulin-secreting) and hepatocytes; high-capacity for high-glucose environments.
  - **GLUT3** — major glucose transporter in neurons.
  - **GLUT4** — adipose and skeletal muscle; **insertion** of GLUT4 into the plasma membrane is **acutely regulated** by **insulin** and by **exercise** (vesicle fusion mechanism, akin to exocytosis without cargo release). Central to muscle physiology (Ch 12) and to glucose homeostasis in diabetes (Ch 19).
- Fatty acids also have a carrier-mediated facilitated diffusion system analogous to GLUT — uptake into muscle is increased by exercise and insulin.
- **Hypoglycemia & insulin shock:** because tissue glucose uptake by GLUT carriers depends on the plasma → cell concentration gradient, a *low* plasma glucose starves cells (especially neurons) of glucose. Severe hypoglycemia from insulin overdose can cause loss of consciousness or death; exercise increases muscle GLUT4 and may worsen hypoglycemia.
- **Active transport** = uphill carrier transport, requires ATP, blocked by cyanide (via inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation, Ch 5).
- **Primary active transport**: ATP hydrolysis directly drives the pump. The carriers are themselves **ATPases**.
  - **Ca²⁺ pump** (plasma membrane; sarco/endoplasmic reticulum membrane of striated muscle) — maintains cytosolic Ca²⁺ 10³–10⁴ times lower than extracellular; opening of Ca²⁺ channels then produces a sudden cytosolic Ca²⁺ rise that serves as a signal (neurotransmitter release, muscle contraction).
  - **H⁺ pump** — generates stomach acid (Ch 18).
  - **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump** — **3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in per ATP**. Found in all cells. Quantitative density: ~200 per RBC, ~35,000 per WBC, several million per kidney tubule cell. The Na⁺/K⁺ gradient it maintains underlies (i) energy for cotransport, (ii) electrochemical excitability of nerve, muscle, and heart, and (iii) cell-volume homeostasis (preventing osmotic swelling).
- **Secondary active transport (coupled transport):** ATP not used by the cotransporter directly; energy comes from the **Na⁺ gradient** set up by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. If the pump is inhibited (e.g., by cyanide), the Na⁺ gradient dissipates and coupled transport fails.
  - **Cotransport / symport:** both solutes move the same direction. **SGLT** (Na⁺/glucose cotransporter): **SGLT2** in kidney (1 Na⁺ : 1 glucose); **SGLT1** in small intestine (2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose).
  - **Countertransport / antiport:** solutes move in opposite directions. **Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger** extrudes Ca²⁺ using inward Na⁺ flow.
  - **Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger** uses inward Na⁺ to extrude H⁺; this H⁺ gradient in turn powers proton-coupled amino acid carriers.
- **Transcellular vs. paracellular transport across epithelia (Fig 6.21):**
  - **Transcellular** routes glucose from intestinal lumen → into epithelium (apical SGLT, secondary active) → across the cytosol → out the basolateral side (GLUT, facilitated) → into blood. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase on the basolateral membrane keeps intracellular Na⁺ low.
  - **Paracellular** moves limited amounts of water and solute *between* cells through gaps regulated by **junctional complexes**.
- **Junctional complexes (Fig 6.22) — three components:**
  - **Tight junctions (zonula occludens)** — circumferential rings of trans-membrane protein strands that occlude (or selectively permit) paracellular flow. Brain capillaries' tight junctions form the **blood-brain barrier**; "leaky" tight junctions in other epithelia permit ion flow.
  - **Belt desmosomes (zonula adherens)** — circumferential rings glued via cytoskeleton-linked adhesion proteins.
  - **Desmosomes (macula adherens)** — focal "buttons" cross-linking cytoskeletons of adjacent cells.
- **Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)** exploits intestinal Na⁺/glucose cotransport (SGLT1, 2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose ratio). WHO/UNICEF ORT solution has osmolality **245 mOsm** and equimolar Na⁺ and glucose. Saves >1 million children/year from cholera-type gastroenteritis dehydration. Sodas and juices have too much glucose and too little Na⁺ to work.
- **Bulk transport (endocytosis & exocytosis):**
  - **Exocytosis** — secretion of polypeptide hormones, neurotransmitters, surfactant; vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, contents released.
  - **Endocytosis** — invagination of the plasma membrane forms a vesicle; **receptor-mediated endocytosis** captures specific cargoes such as LDL-bound cholesterol via membrane receptors.
- **Polarity of epithelial cells:** apical (top, lumen-facing) and basolateral (bottom, blood-facing) surfaces carry different transport proteins; exocytotic vesicles from the Golgi fuse apically, while nucleus and ER sit more basally.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The common characteristics of enzymes and carrier proteins are (1) specificity, (2) competition, and (3) saturation." (p. 140)
- > "As the concentration of a transported molecule is increased, its rate of transport will also be increased — but only up to a transport maximum (Tm)." (p. 140)
- > "In the hyperglycemia of diabetes, however, the carriers become saturated; their transport maximum is exceeded. When this occurs, the untransported glucose continues through the renal tubules and appears in the urine, a condition called **glycosuria**." (p. 141)
- > "**Active transport** is the movement of molecules and ions against their concentration gradients, from lower to higher concentrations. This transport requires the expenditure of cellular energy obtained from ATP." (p. 142)

- > "The Na⁺/K⁺ pumps transport three Na⁺ out of the cell cytoplasm for every two K⁺ that they transport into the cytoplasm." (p. 144)

### Equations / quantitative

**Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase stoichiometry (per ATP hydrolyzed):**
$$3\,\mathrm{Na^+_{in}} + 2\,\mathrm{K^+_{out}} + \mathrm{ATP} \to 3\,\mathrm{Na^+_{out}} + 2\,\mathrm{K^+_{in}} + \mathrm{ADP} + \mathrm{P_i}$$

**Net electrogenic charge per pump cycle:** +1 charge moved out → contributes ≈ **3 mV** to the resting membrane potential.

**SGLT2 cotransport (kidney, 1:1):**
$$\mathrm{Na^+_{lumen}} + \mathrm{Glucose_{lumen}} \to \mathrm{Na^+_{cyto}} + \mathrm{Glucose_{cyto}}$$

**SGLT1 cotransport (small intestine, 2:1):**
$$2\,\mathrm{Na^+_{lumen}} + \mathrm{Glucose_{lumen}} \to 2\,\mathrm{Na^+_{cyto}} + \mathrm{Glucose_{cyto}}$$

## Section 6.4 — The Membrane Potential (pp. 147–151)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Equilibrium Potentials** (pp. 148–150)
  - **Nernst Equation** (pp. 149–150)
- **Resting Membrane Potential** (pp. 150–151)
  - **Role of the Na⁺/K⁺ Pumps** (p. 151)

### Core claims
- **Origin of the membrane potential:**
  1. Cellular proteins, ATP, and other phosphate-rich molecules are large negatively charged anions trapped inside the cell ("**fixed anions**"); they cannot cross the membrane.
  2. These fixed anions attract diffusible cations from outside.
  3. The plasma membrane is most permeable to **K⁺**, so K⁺ is the cation that accumulates most inside.
  4. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase amplifies the gradient (3 Na⁺ out : 2 K⁺ in per ATP).
  5. The net result is an unequal distribution of charge: **inside negative, outside positive**.
- **Equilibrium potential ($E_{ion}$)** = the membrane voltage at which the electrical attraction of an ion balances its diffusion gradient; no net flow.
  - At normal K⁺ gradient (150 in / 5 out), **$E_K = -90$ mV**.
  - At normal Na⁺ gradient (12 in / 145 out), **$E_{Na} = +66$ mV**.
- **Nernst equation (simplified, 37 °C body temperature):**
  $$E_x = \frac{61}{z} \log\!\left( \frac{[X_o]}{[X_i]} \right) \;\mathrm{mV}$$
  where $z$ is the valence and $[X_o]/[X_i]$ is the ratio of extracellular to intracellular concentration. Units: mV.
- **Resting membrane potential (RMP)** is the membrane voltage of a cell *not producing impulses*. It lies between $E_K$ and $E_{Na}$, closer to $E_K$ because membrane permeability to K⁺ ≫ permeability to Na⁺. Typical neurons: **−70 mV**; range across body cells: −65 to −85 mV.
  - Because RMP is less negative than $E_K$, K⁺ leaks slightly *out*.
  - Because RMP is less positive than $E_{Na}$, Na⁺ leaks slightly *in*.
  - The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase counters both leaks and contributes ~3 mV of electrogenic negativity.
- **Two regulatory levers** for changing the RMP:
  1. Change *concentration ratio* of an ion across the membrane (e.g., **hyperkalemia** raises extracellular K⁺ and depolarizes resting cells — dangerous to the heart; concentrations >8 mEq/L stop the heart, the basis for **lethal-injection KCl**).
  2. Change *membrane permeability* to an ion via gated channels — this is how nerve and muscle impulses are generated (Ch 7, 12).
- **Plasma K⁺** is kept in a narrow 3.5–5.0 mEq/L window, primarily by the kidneys. Both **hyperkalemia** and **hypokalemia** (often from diuretics) cause ECG abnormalities.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Cellular proteins and the phosphate groups of ATP and other organic molecules are negatively charged at the pH of the cell cytoplasm. These negative ions (anions) are 'fixed' within the cell because they cannot penetrate the plasma membrane." (pp. 147–148)
- > "Because the plasma membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to any other cation, K⁺ accumulates within the cell more than the others." (p. 148)
- > "The intracellular K⁺ concentration is 150 mEq/L in the human body compared to an extracellular concentration of 5 mEq/L." (p. 148)
- > "A real resting cell is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺, but it is not completely impermeable to Na⁺. As a result, its resting membrane potential is close to the E_K but somewhat less negative due to the slight inward diffusion of Na⁺." (p. 150)
- > "The resting membrane potential of most cells in the body ranges from −65 mV to −85 mV (in neurons it averages −70 mV)." (p. 151)
- > "Because [the Na⁺/K⁺ pump] transports 3 Na⁺ out of the cell for every 2 K⁺ that it moves in, it has the net effect of contributing to the negative intracellular charge. This electrogenic effect of the pumps adds approximately 3 mV to the membrane potential." (p. 151)

### Equations / quantitative

**Nernst equation (simplified, 37 °C):**
$$E_x = \frac{61\,\mathrm{mV}}{z} \log\!\left(\frac{[X_o]}{[X_i]}\right)$$

**Application to K⁺ ($z = +1$, [K]o = 5, [K]i = 150 mEq/L):**
$$E_K = 61\,\mathrm{mV} \cdot \log\!\left(\frac{5}{150}\right) = 61 \cdot (-1.477) \approx -90\,\mathrm{mV}$$

**Application to Na⁺ ($z = +1$, [Na]o = 145, [Na]i = 12 mEq/L):**
$$E_{Na} = 61\,\mathrm{mV} \cdot \log\!\left(\frac{145}{12}\right) = 61 \cdot (+1.082) \approx +66\,\mathrm{mV}$$

## Section 6.5 — Cell Signaling (pp. 151–155)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Second Messengers** (pp. 152–153)
- **G-Proteins** (pp. 153–155)

### Core claims
- **Three modes of chemical signaling between cells** (plus a fourth, autocrine):
  - **Paracrine signaling** — a regulator diffuses through the ECM to *nearby* target cells in the *same organ*; local action.
  - **Autocrine signaling** — the secreting cell is itself the target.
  - **Synaptic signaling** — a neuron releases a **neurotransmitter** at a **synapse** onto its target cell.
  - **Endocrine signaling** — an endocrine gland secretes a **hormone** into the blood; only cells with receptors for that hormone respond.
- Gap-junction signaling is a special case in which ions and small regulators move directly between coupled cells (Ch 7).
- **Receptor proteins** make signaling specific. Estimates: ~30,000 distinct receptor types across the ~200 cell types of the human body; ~10,000–100,000 receptors of a given type per cell.
- **Location of receptors depends on signal lipophilicity:**
  - **Lipophilic / nonpolar regulators** (steroid hormones, thyroid hormones, nitric oxide gas) cross the plasma membrane and bind **intracellular** (cytoplasmic or nuclear) receptors; they typically act by altering gene expression.
  - **Hydrophilic / polar regulators** (epinephrine, acetylcholine, insulin) cannot cross the membrane; they bind receptors on the **outer surface of the plasma membrane**.
- **Second messengers** transduce extracellular polar signals into intracellular biochemical effects without the signal itself entering the cell:
  - **Ca²⁺** entering from the extracellular fluid or from the ER lumen.
  - **Cyclic AMP (cAMP)** produced from ATP by membrane-bound adenylate cyclase after receptor activation.
  - Generic cAMP sequence: (1) polar ligand binds membrane receptor; (2) the receptor activates a membrane enzyme that produces cAMP from ATP; (3) cAMP rises and activates cytoplasmic enzymes (protein kinase A); (4) the activated enzymes change cell behavior. The action of the ligand is thus carried out by the second messenger.
- **G-proteins** (Nobel Prize 1994, Gilman & Rodbell) shuttle the signal between the receptor and the effector enzyme/channel in the membrane:
  - Three subunits (α, β, γ); the heterotrimer is membrane-anchored.
  - **Inactive state:** the α subunit holds **GDP** and is bound to the βγ pair and the receptor.
  - **Ligand binding** → α releases GDP and binds **GTP**; α dissociates from βγ.
  - **Active state:** α (or βγ in some pathways) diffuses laterally through the membrane to an **effector protein** (an enzyme such as adenylate cyclase, or an ion channel).
  - **Termination:** α hydrolyzes its bound GTP to GDP + Pᵢ; α and βγ reassociate and the heterotrimer returns to the inactive receptor.
- Estimated **400–500 G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)** for neurotransmitters, hormones, and paracrine signals, *plus* several hundred more for olfaction and taste — by far the largest receptor superfamily.
- Specific GPCR/cAMP and GPCR/ion-channel mechanisms are taken up with concrete neurotransmitters and hormones in Ch 7 (autonomic / synaptic), Ch 10 (sensory), and Ch 11 (endocrine).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In **paracrine signaling**, cells within an organ secrete regulatory molecules that diffuse through the extracellular matrix to nearby target cells." (p. 152)
- > "In **endocrine signaling**, the cells of endocrine glands secrete chemical regulators called hormones into the extracellular fluid." (p. 152)
- > "If the regulatory molecule is nonpolar, it can diffuse through the cell membrane and enter the target cell. Such nonpolar regulatory molecules include steroid hormones, thyroid hormones, and nitric oxide gas." (p. 152)
- > "This feat is accomplished by means of intermediaries known as **second messengers**, sent into the cytoplasm from the receptor proteins in the plasma membrane." (p. 152)
- > "In 1994, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded for the discovery of the **G-proteins**: three protein subunits that shuttle between receptors and different membrane effector proteins, including specific enzymes and ion channels." (p. 153)
- > "Because there are an estimated 400 to 500 different G-protein-coupled receptors for neurotransmitters, hormones, and paracrine regulators … there is great diversity in their effects." (p. 153)

## Clinical apparatus

- **Clinical Investigation: Jessica** — physiology student, hypertension treated with a diuretic, constant thirst, glycosuria, hyperglycemia, abnormal ECG with mild hypokalemia. Threads the chapter: glucose Tm (saturated renal carriers → glycosuria), osmotic diuresis (glucose in urine drags water), osmoreceptor → thirst, hypokalemia (diuretic) → RMP and ECG.
- **Clinical Application — Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in tumor metastasis** (§ 6.1): MMPs digest ECM and basement membrane; cancer cells exploit them to invade vessels; MMP/TIMP imbalance also implicated in arthritis, cardiovascular and neural disease.
- **Clinical Application — Dialysis** (§ 6.2): renal failure → wastes built up in blood. **Hemodialysis** uses an external semipermeable circuit; **peritoneal dialysis** uses the peritoneum as the membrane. Both rely on net diffusion of wastes into a dialysate.
- **Clinical Application — Cystic fibrosis** (§ 6.2): the CFTR Cl⁻ channel is defective; abnormal NaCl and water movement across sweat-gland, pancreatic, and small-airway epithelia; salty sweat, dense viscous mucus, pancreatic and pulmonary disease. ~2,000 known CFTR mutations; new modulator drugs target some defects.
- **Clinical Application — Plasma proteins, edema, and cirrhosis** (§ 6.2): plasma proteins (especially albumin) are osmotically active because they cannot leave the capillaries; low albumin (cirrhosis) → reduced oncotic pull → fluid accumulation in tissues (edema).
- **Clinical Application — Isotonic IV fluids** (§ 6.2): normal saline (0.9% NaCl), 5% dextrose, Ringer's lactate are all isotonic. Mannitol is used precisely *because* it's osmotically active (draws fluid from edematous brain in cerebral edema or trauma).
- **Clinical Application — Diabetes mellitus, renal Tm, glycosuria** (§ 6.3): in hyperglycemia, plasma glucose exceeds the renal carrier Tm; excess glucose remains in the filtrate and appears in urine.
- **Clinical Application — Hypoglycemia and insulin shock** (§ 6.3): below-normal plasma glucose collapses the GLUT concentration gradient; neurons starve; severe insulin overdose may cause coma or death.
- **Clinical Application — Acute gastroenteritis & oral rehydration therapy** (§ 6.3): cholera-type diarrhea kills 1.5–2.5 million children annually. WHO/UNICEF ORT (osmolality 245 mOsm; equimolar Na⁺ and glucose) exploits intestinal SGLT1 to absorb Na⁺ and water; saves >1 million children/year. Sodas and juices fail (too much sugar, too little salt).
- **Clinical Application — Hyperkalemia / hypokalemia & lethal injection** (§ 6.4): plasma K⁺ must stay 3.5–5.0 mEq/L. Hyperkalemia depolarizes resting cells → ECG abnormalities; >8 mEq/L stops the heart (basis for KCl in execution protocols). Hypokalemia from diuretics also causes ECG abnormalities (Jessica's case).

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Intracellular vs. extracellular fluid** — 67% vs. 33% of total body water; extracellular = 20% plasma + 80% interstitial.
- **Extracellular matrix (ECM)** — collagen + elastin + glycoprotein/proteoglycan ground substance; gives form to connective tissue.
- **Basement membrane** — collagen-IV-anchored ECM layer beneath epithelial sheets.
- **Integrin** — transmembrane glycoprotein linking the cytoskeleton to ECM proteins; mediates cell-matrix adhesion and bidirectional signaling.
- **Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) / TIMPs** — Zn²⁺-dependent ECM-degrading peptidases / their inhibitors.
- **Selectively permeable membrane** — passes some solutes, blocks others; foundational to diffusion and osmosis.
- **Simple diffusion / facilitated diffusion / active transport** — passive non-carrier / passive carrier / ATP-requiring uphill.
- **Aquaporin** — water channel; major regulator of kidney water balance.
- **Concentration gradient** — driving force for diffusion; rate scales with steepness, permeability, temperature, and surface area.
- **Microvilli** — apical-membrane folds in small intestine and kidney tubules increasing absorptive surface.
- **Osmosis** — net diffusion of water through a selectively permeable membrane.
- **Osmotic pressure** — force required to halt osmosis; greater with higher solute concentration.
- **Molarity (M) vs. molality (m)** — moles solute per liter solution vs. per kilogram water.
- **Osmolality (Osm)** — total particle molality; plasma ≈ 300 mOsm; NaCl ionizes so 1 m NaCl = 2 Osm.
- **Freezing-point depression** — measurement basis for osmolality (−1.86 °C per Osm).
- **Tonicity (iso-, hypo-, hypertonic)** — operational classification by net water movement into a cell.
- **Hemolysis / crenation** — RBC rupture in hypotonic / shrinkage in hypertonic solution.
- **5% dextrose / normal saline / Ringer's lactate / mannitol** — common IV fluids; first three isotonic, mannitol osmotically active.
- **ADH (vasopressin) / osmoreceptors** — pituitary hormone / hypothalamic neurons coordinating plasma-osmolality homeostasis.
- **Carrier protein** — membrane protein with specificity, competition, saturation.
- **Transport maximum (Tm)** — saturation rate of a carrier; renal Tm for glucose underlies glycosuria of diabetes.
- **GLUT1–4** — facilitative glucose-transporter isoforms (1 broad/elevated; 2 liver/β-cell; 3 neuron; 4 muscle/adipose, insulin-regulated).
- **Primary active transport** — ATP-driven pump; Ca²⁺ pump, H⁺ pump, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.
- **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** — 3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in per ATP; electrogenic ~3 mV; cyanide-sensitive via ATP supply.
- **Secondary active transport / cotransport / symport / antiport** — coupled to the Na⁺ (or H⁺) gradient; SGLT1/SGLT2; Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger; Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger.
- **Junctional complex** — tight junction (zonula occludens), belt desmosome (zonula adherens), desmosome (macula adherens).
- **Transcellular vs. paracellular transport** — through vs. between epithelial cells.
- **Endocytosis / receptor-mediated endocytosis / exocytosis** — bulk transport in / specific receptor-coupled / bulk transport out.
- **Apical vs. basolateral membrane** — top (lumen-facing) vs. bottom (blood-facing) surface of an epithelial cell.
- **Fixed anions** — proteins and phosphates trapped inside the cell; create the negative-charge sink.
- **Equilibrium potential (E_x)** — membrane voltage at which diffusion and electrical forces on ion x balance.
- **Nernst equation** — $E_x = (61/z)\log([X_o]/[X_i])$ mV at 37 °C.
- **EK ≈ −90 mV, ENa ≈ +66 mV** — at typical mammalian concentrations.
- **Resting membrane potential (RMP)** — −65 to −85 mV (typical neuron −70 mV); closer to EK because the resting membrane is more permeable to K⁺.
- **Hyperkalemia / hypokalemia** — high / low plasma K⁺; both cause ECG abnormalities; >8 mEq/L K⁺ stops the heart.
- **Paracrine / autocrine / synaptic / endocrine signaling** — local-to-near / self / neuron-target / blood-borne hormone.
- **Receptor protein** — high-specificity binding protein; membrane-bound for polar regulators, intracellular for lipophilic regulators.
- **Second messenger** — intracellular relay (cAMP, Ca²⁺) for membrane-receptor signaling.
- **G-protein / GPCR** — heterotrimeric (α β γ) signal shuttle; 400–500+ receptor types in humans; Nobel Prize 1994.
