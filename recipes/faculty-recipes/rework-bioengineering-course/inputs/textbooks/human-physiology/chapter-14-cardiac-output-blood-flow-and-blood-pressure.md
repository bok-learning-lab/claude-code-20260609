### Core claims
- **Cardiac output (CO):** volume of blood pumped per minute by each ventricle.
  $$\mathrm{CO}\ (\mathrm{mL/min}) = \mathrm{HR}\ (\mathrm{beats/min}) \times \mathrm{SV}\ (\mathrm{mL/beat})$$
  At rest: ~70 bpm × ~70–80 mL/beat ≈ **5.5 L/min**. Because total blood volume ≈ 5.5 L, the entire blood volume circulates each minute.
- **Pulmonary vs. systemic mean arterial pressure:** pulmonary 10–20 mmHg; systemic 70–105 mmHg. Pulmonary circulation must be low-resistance, low-pressure, high-flow to match systemic output.
- **Chronotropic (heart rate) regulation:**
  - **Sympathetic (NE, adrenal Epi → β1 → cAMP → HCN/Ca²⁺ channels → faster diastolic depolarization)** — positive chronotrope.
  - **Parasympathetic (vagus → ACh → muscarinic → K⁺ channels opened → hyperpolarization)** — negative chronotrope. Vagal tone keeps resting HR below the intrinsic SA node rate (~90–100 bpm).
  - Athletes have **resting bradycardia** from high vagal tone.
  - During exercise: HR rises first by **vagal withdrawal**, then by **sympathetic activation**.
  - The **cardiac control center** in the medulla coordinates input from baroreceptors (Ch 13 §13.5; Ch 14 §14.6).
- **Stroke volume (SV)** is regulated by three variables:
  1. **End-diastolic volume (EDV) = preload.**
  2. **Total peripheral resistance (TPR) = afterload.**
  3. **Contractility** (strength of contraction at a given fiber length).
- **Ejection fraction (EF) = SV/EDV ≈ 60% at rest** (~70–80 mL ejected from ~110–130 mL EDV).
- **Frank-Starling law (Fig 14.3):** an intrinsic, neuroendocrine-independent property of cardiac muscle. As EDV rises, sarcomere length increases from ~1.5 μm (resting) toward 2.0–2.25 μm (optimum), recruiting more cross bridges and increasing Ca²⁺ sensitivity of the SR release channels. The increased force makes SV proportional to EDV across the physiological range.
- **Anrep effect:** a delayed (10–15 min) additional rise in contractility after stretch, due to increased Ca²⁺ entry via the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger.
- **Cardiac vs. skeletal length-tension contrast (Fig 14.4):** cardiac muscle's resting sarcomere length is below optimum (so stretch boosts force), while skeletal muscle's resting length is near optimum (so significant stretch lowers force). Cardiac muscle is also more Ca²⁺-sensitive than skeletal muscle.
- **The Frank-Starling mechanism matches RV and LV output beat-to-beat,** preventing pulmonary edema or systemic underperfusion when one ventricle's output transiently changes.
- **Extrinsic control of contractility (positive inotropy):** sympathetic NE / adrenal Epi → β1 → ↑intracellular Ca²⁺ → stronger contraction. Demonstrated by steeper Starling curves with sympathetic stimulation (Fig 14.2).
- **Venous return** determines EDV → SV → CO. Two-thirds of total blood volume is in **veins** (capacitance vessels). Mean venous pressure ~2 mmHg vs. mean arterial pressure ~90–100 mmHg. Venous return is driven by:
  - The pressure gradient from venules (10 mmHg) to right atrium (0 mmHg).
  - **Sympathetic venoconstriction** of capacitance vessels (reduces compliance, mobilizes the venous pool).
  - **Skeletal muscle pump** with one-way valves.
  - **Respiratory pump** (inspiration → ↓thoracic + ↑abdominal pressure → drives venous flow).

### Equations / quantitative

**Cardiac output:**
$$\mathrm{CO} = \mathrm{HR} \times \mathrm{SV}$$

**Resting baseline (typical adult):**
$$\mathrm{CO} = 70\ \mathrm{bpm} \times 75\ \mathrm{mL/beat} \approx 5{,}250\ \mathrm{mL/min} \approx 5.5\ \mathrm{L/min}$$

**Stroke volume from chamber volumes:**
$$\mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{EDV} - \mathrm{ESV}$$

**Ejection fraction:**
$$\mathrm{EF} = \frac{\mathrm{SV}}{\mathrm{EDV}} \approx 0.60$$

**Pulmonary vs. systemic mean arterial pressure:**
$$\mathrm{MAP_{pulm}} \approx 10{-}20\ \mathrm{mmHg};\quad \mathrm{MAP_{sys}} \approx 70{-}105\ \mathrm{mmHg}$$

### Core claims
- **Body water distribution (Fig 14.8):**
  - Total body water ~42 L in a 70 kg adult; ⅔ intracellular (27–30 L), ⅓ extracellular (14–16.5 L).
  - **Extracellular compartment**: ~80% interstitial fluid (11–13 L); ~20% plasma (3.0–3.5 L); ~1.5–2.5 L H₂O in/out per day.
- **Capillary fluid exchange (Starling forces, Fig 14.9):**
  - **Filtration out** is driven by:
    - $P_c$ — capillary hydrostatic pressure (37 mmHg arteriolar end → 17 mmHg venular end).
    - $\pi_i$ — interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure (≈0 mmHg, normally negligible).
  - **Absorption back** is driven by:
    - $P_i$ — interstitial fluid hydrostatic pressure (~1 mmHg).
    - $\pi_p$ — plasma colloid osmotic pressure (~25 mmHg, from albumin = **oncotic pressure**).
  - $$\mathrm{Net\ filtration} = (P_c + \pi_i) - (P_i + \pi_p)$$
  - At the **arteriolar end:** (37 + 0) − (1 + 25) = **+11 mmHg** → filtration out.
  - At the **venular end:** (17 + 0) − (1 + 25) = **−9 mmHg** → reabsorption back in.
  - ~85–90% of filtrate returns to the venular end of capillaries; the remaining 10–15% (≈1–2 L/day, 20–30 g protein/L) returns via lymphatics.
- **Six causes of edema (Table 14.2):**
  1. ↑ arterial blood pressure (↑Pc).
  2. **Venous obstruction** (phlebitis, pregnancy) — ↑Pc.
  3. **Leaky capillaries** with protein loss → ↑πi locally (inflammation, allergy).
  4. **Myxedema** — hypothyroidism increases interstitial mucin / glycoprotein.
  5. **Hypoproteinemia** — liver disease, kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome), Crohn's protein-losing enteropathy → ↓πp.
  6. **Lymphatic obstruction** — filariasis (elephantiasis), post-mastectomy with axillary node dissection, radiation.
- **Kidney regulation of blood volume:**
  - Kidneys filter ~180 L/day, reabsorbing 98–99%. Reducing reabsorption by just 1% would double urine output. Three hormones tune reabsorption:
  - **ADH (antidiuretic hormone / arginine vasopressin)** from posterior pituitary; secreted in response to hypothalamic osmoreceptors detecting ↑plasma osmolality OR in response to ↓atrial / aortic / carotid sinus stretch. Promotes water reabsorption in collecting ducts. Net effect: small volume of concentrated urine; dilutes plasma; raises BV.
  - **Aldosterone** (mineralocorticoid steroid from adrenal cortex zona glomerulosa) — stimulates Na⁺ + water reabsorption proportionally (does NOT dilute the blood). Secreted under angiotensin II stimulation during salt deprivation/low BP.
  - **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS, Fig 14.12):**
    1. ↓BP / ↓renal blood flow → **juxtaglomerular apparatus** secretes **renin**.
    2. Renin cleaves **angiotensinogen** (a liver-derived plasma protein) → **angiotensin I** (10 AAs).
    3. **Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)** in the pulmonary capillaries removes 2 AAs → **angiotensin II** (8 AAs).
    4. Angiotensin II effects: (a) potent **vasoconstriction** of arterioles → ↑TPR; (b) stimulates **hypothalamic thirst** → ↑drinking; (c) stimulates **adrenal cortex aldosterone secretion** → ↑Na⁺/water retention.
  - **ACE inhibitors** (captopril, enalapril, lisinopril) block step 3 → ↓vasoconstriction → ↓BP.
  - **Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs)** (losartan, valsartan, telmisartan) block AT₁ receptors on vascular smooth muscle.
  - **ACE2** (different from ACE) inactivates angiotensin II; it is also the cell-entry receptor for SARS-CoV-2's spike protein.
  - **Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)** is secreted by atrial myocytes in response to atrial stretch (volume overload). It promotes **natriuresis** (Na⁺ excretion) and diuresis → ↓BV → ↓BP. It is the body's counterregulator to RAAS.

#### Figure 14.12 — The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system *(viewed)*

A negative feedback loop diagram. **Stimulus** (top): ↓blood pressure → ↓blood flow to kidneys. **Sensor / integrating center / effector**: the **juxtaglomerular apparatus** in the kidneys, which secretes **renin** (enzyme arrow leaving the apparatus). The renin acts on circulating **angiotensinogen** (a liver-derived plasma α2-globulin), cleaving it to **angiotensin I**. Angiotensin I is acted on by **ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme)** — primarily in pulmonary capillaries — producing **angiotensin II** (the 8-amino-acid active form). Angiotensin II branches into two effects: (1) stimulates the **adrenal cortex** to secrete **aldosterone**, which acts on the kidneys to promote **salt and water retention**; (2) directly causes **vasoconstriction of arterioles**. Both effects (retention + vasoconstriction) close the **negative feedback** loop by raising blood pressure.

> Vision note: Canonical RAAS diagram. Anchor for any question about blood pressure regulation, hypertension pharmacology (ACE inhibitors / ARBs / direct renin inhibitors), heart failure treatment, and kidney-cardiovascular cross-talk. Note that ACE is distinct from ACE2 — the former generates angiotensin II, the latter destroys it (and is the SARS-CoV-2 entry receptor).

### Core claims
- **Poiseuille's law** describes laminar flow through a rigid tube. Blood flow $F$ is:
  $$F = \frac{\Delta P \cdot \pi r^4}{8 \eta L}$$
  - **Directly proportional** to pressure difference $\Delta P$ and to **the fourth power of the vessel radius** $r$.
  - **Inversely proportional** to blood viscosity $\eta$ and to vessel length $L$.
- **Radius dominance (Fig 14.15):** doubling the radius gives a **16-fold increase** in flow (and 1/16th the resistance); halving the radius gives 1/16th the flow (16× resistance). Vasomotor control is the most powerful regulator of regional perfusion.
- **Resistance:**
  $$R \propto \frac{\eta L}{r^4}$$
  $$F = \frac{\Delta P}{R}$$
- **Total peripheral resistance (TPR):** the sum of resistances of all parallel vascular beds in the systemic circulation. Because organ vascular beds are in parallel (Fig 14.17), a change in one organ's resistance does not directly affect another organ's blood supply.
- **Arteriole-dominated resistance:** the greatest pressure drop occurs across the arterioles (Fig 14.16), because they have small radii and contractile smooth-muscle walls. Arteriolar tone is the master adjustable knob of regional perfusion and arterial blood pressure.
- **Extrinsic regulation (Table 14.4):**
  - **Sympathetic adrenergic** fibers release **norepinephrine** → α1 receptors → **vasoconstriction** in viscera and skin (dominant systemic effect).
  - **Sympathetic adrenergic** + epinephrine on **β2 receptors** in skeletal muscle and coronary vessels → **vasodilation** (masked at most sites by α effects).
  - **Sympathetic cholinergic** fibers (release ACh) → vasodilation in skeletal-muscle arterioles during the **fight-or-flight pre-exercise** state.
  - **Parasympathetic** vasodilation is limited to the digestive tract, external genitalia, and salivary glands — minimal effect on TPR.
  - **Angiotensin II** — potent vasoconstrictor (via AT₁ receptors on vascular smooth muscle).
  - **ADH (vasopressin)** — vasoconstrictor at high concentrations.
  - **Histamine** — local vasodilator (inflammation, allergy).
  - **Bradykinin** — local vasodilator (sweat glands, endothelium).
  - **Prostaglandins** — PGI₂ (prostacyclin) vasodilates; TxA₂ vasoconstricts.
- **Paracrine endothelium-derived regulators:**
  - **Nitric oxide (NO)** is produced by **endothelial NO synthase (eNOS)** from L-arginine. NO diffuses into vascular smooth muscle, activates **guanylate cyclase → cGMP → ↓cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ → vasodilation**. NO can bind hemoglobin cysteine sulfurs as **S-nitrosohemoglobin (SNO)** and be carried as a downstream vasodilator (released at low O₂).
  - **Prostacyclin (PGI₂)** — vasodilator, antiaggregant.
  - **Endothelin-1** — vasoconstrictor; targeted by endothelin receptor antagonists for pulmonary hypertension.
- **Nitroglycerin** (an NO donor) treats angina pectoris by vasodilating coronaries and reducing afterload.
- **Intrinsic regulation (autoregulation):**
  - **Myogenic:** vascular smooth muscle directly responds to stretch — ↑pressure stretches the vessel → myogenic contraction → vasoconstriction → maintains constant flow despite pressure changes. Especially active in brain and kidneys.
  - **Metabolic:** ↓O₂, ↑CO₂, ↓pH (lactate, CO₂), ↑K⁺, ↑adenosine, paracrine factors (NO, prostaglandins) cause vasodilation → matches blood flow to tissue metabolic demand.
  - **Reactive hyperemia:** post-occlusion ↑flow from accumulated metabolites.
  - **Active hyperemia:** ↑flow during exercise as metabolic byproducts accumulate.

## Section 14.4 — Blood Flow to the Heart and Skeletal Muscles (pp. 467–472)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Aerobic Requirements of the Heart** (pp. 467–468)
- **Regulation of Coronary Blood Flow** (pp. 468–469)
- **Regulation of Blood Flow Through Skeletal Muscles** (p. 469)
- **Circulatory Changes During Exercise** (pp. 469–472)

### Core claims
- **Myocardial capillary density** 2,500–4,000/mm³ (vs. 300–400/mm³ in fast skeletal muscle) → each myocyte is within ~10 μm of a capillary. **Myocardial myoglobin** stores O₂ during diastole and releases it during systole, when contractile compression of coronary vessels reduces coronary inflow.
- The heart is **obligate aerobic** — almost all ATP comes from oxidative phosphorylation. Myocardial ATP turns over completely every ~10 seconds. **50–70% of resting myocardial ATP** comes from β-oxidation of fatty acids; the rest from glucose + lactate.
- **Coronary blood flow** rises from ~80 mL/min/100g at rest to ~400 mL/min/100g at heavy exercise. This is driven primarily by **metabolic vasodilation** (↓O₂, ↑CO₂, ↑K⁺, ↑adenosine, NO, prostaglandins) plus sympathetic β-adrenergic effects.
- **Coronary artery disease management:**
  - **Coronary angiogram:** catheter-delivered iodine contrast under fluoroscopy.
  - **Coronary angioplasty:** balloon catheter widens an occlusion. Restenosis often follows.
  - **Stent:** metal mesh keeps the angioplastied vessel open.
  - **CABG (Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting):** saphenous vein or internal mammary artery graft circumvents the occluded segment.
- **Skeletal muscle blood flow at rest:** ~1,000 mL/min total (~20–25% of resting CO). **At heavy exercise:** up to ~20,000 mL/min — a 20-fold increase. Mechanisms:
  - **Pre-exercise:** sympathetic **cholinergic** vasodilation + circulating epinephrine on β-receptors → blood flow rises before exercise begins.
  - **During dynamic exercise:** **intrinsic metabolic vasodilation** (↑CO₂, ↓pH, ↓O₂, ↑K⁺, adenosine, NO) dominates.
- **Static (isometric) exercise** sustains arteriolar compression and impairs blood flow → pain and fatigue come faster. Dynamic exercise allows rhythmic perfusion.
- **Cardiovascular changes during exercise (Table 14.7):**
  - **Cardiac output ↑ (up to 5× in untrained, 6–7× in elite athletes).**
  - **Cardiac rate ↑** (sympathetic up, vagal withdrawal).
  - **Stroke volume ↑** (sympathoadrenal positive inotropy + improved venous return; EDV unchanged because of compensations).
  - **Ejection fraction rises from ~60% (rest) to ~90% (heavy exercise).**
  - **TPR ↓** (vasodilation in exercising muscles outweighs visceral vasoconstriction).
  - **Arterial BP ↑** (systolic rises sharply; diastolic rises little).
  - **Blood flow to heart and skeletal muscles ↑**; **blood flow to visceral organs ↓**; **blood flow to skin ↑** (thermoregulation); **blood flow to brain ~unchanged** (autoregulation).
- **Distribution at rest vs. heavy exercise (Fig 14.20):**
  - Rest (CO 5 L/min): liver/GI 20–25%, kidneys 20%, brain 15%, skeletal muscle 15–20%, skin 4–5%, coronary 4–5%, other 3–5%.
  - Heavy exercise (CO 25 L/min): **skeletal muscle 80–85%**, coronary 3–5%, brain ~3–4% (in absolute terms, still ~750 mL/min — unchanged), other organs sharply reduced.
- **Maximum cardiac rate** ≈ 220 − age (older formula) or 208 − 0.7 × age (newer, more accurate). Endurance training can raise V̇O₂ max by ~20%; elite athletes reach SVs ~double that of untrained adults.

## Section 14.5 — Blood Flow to the Brain and Skin (pp. 472–474)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Cerebral Circulation** (pp. 472–473)
  - **Myogenic Regulation** (p. 473)
  - **Metabolic Regulation** (p. 473)
- **Cutaneous Blood Flow** (pp. 473–474)

### Core claims
- **Cerebral blood flow ~750 mL/min (~15% of resting CO)** is held nearly constant by intrinsic mechanisms (autoregulation). Only at mean arterial pressure > ~200 mmHg do sympathetic nerves constrict cerebral arterioles enough to matter (protecting thin-walled vessels from rupture / stroke).
- **Myogenic cerebral autoregulation:** ↑BP → cerebral vessels constrict (protect downstream vessels); ↓BP → cerebral vessels dilate (maintain flow). Operates in the MAP range ~60–160 mmHg.
- **CO₂ sensitivity:** ↑arterial CO₂ (hypoventilation) → ↓CSF pH → cerebral arteriolar **dilation** → ↑flow. **Hyperventilation** lowers CO₂ → ↑CSF pH → **vasoconstriction** → ↓flow → dizziness.
- **Functional hyperemia (neurovascular coupling):** the most active brain regions get more blood than they strictly need. Mechanisms include release of K⁺, adenosine, NO, prostaglandins, and astrocyte-released vasodilators (prostaglandin E₂, CO) from astrocytes activated by neuronal glutamate spillover.
- **fMRI BOLD** signal exploits the local change in deoxy-/oxy-hemoglobin ratio caused by functional hyperemia to map active brain regions.
- **Cutaneous blood flow** can vary 200× — from <20 mL/min (maximal vasoconstriction in cold) to 3–4 L/min (maximal vasodilation in heat). Skin tolerates extreme variation because of low metabolic demand; severe cold restriction → **frostbite**.
- **Arteriovenous anastomoses** in fingertips, palms, toes, soles, ears, nose, and lips can shunt blood directly from arterioles to deep venules, bypassing superficial capillary loops.
- **Thermoregulation reflex (Fig 14.23):**
  - **Cold:** sympathetic α-adrenergic vasoconstriction; AV anastomoses constrict; superficial capillary loops fill (rosy face) but total cutaneous flow is reduced — minimizes heat loss.
  - **Warming:** ↓α-adrenergic tone → arteriolar dilation; with further warming, **sympathetic cholinergic** axons release ACh → **active vasodilation**; sweat glands secrete **bradykinin** → further vasodilation; eccrine sweating cools by evaporation.
- **Heat illness:** if cutaneous vasodilation persists after exercise but CO falls back, MAP can collapse → syncope or death.
- **Emotional vasomotor responses:** fear / cold sweat → vasoconstriction + sweat gland activation; embarrassment / heat → blush.
- The cutaneous vasoconstrictor reflex weakens in older adults → higher risk of hypothermia.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Cerebral blood flow is held remarkably constant at about 750 ml per minute. This amounts to about 15% of the total cardiac output at rest." (p. 472)
- > "In the normal range of arterial pressures, cerebral blood flow is regulated almost exclusively by local intrinsic mechanisms — a process called autoregulation." (p. 472)
- > "When the carbon dioxide concentration rises… the cerebral arterioles dilate. This is believed to be due to decreases in the pH of cerebrospinal fluid… Conversely, when the arterial CO₂ falls below normal during hyperventilation, the cerebral vessels constrict." (p. 473)
- > "Active brain regions are hyperemic — their blood flow actually exceeds the aerobic requirements of the active neurons." (p. 473)
- > "In this way, neurons, astrocytes, and arterioles function together — a process termed neurovascular coupling." (p. 473)
- > "Blood flow to the skin can vary from less than 20 ml per minute at maximal vasoconstriction to as much as 3 to 4 L per minute at maximal vasodilation." (p. 474)

## Section 14.6 — Blood Pressure (pp. 474–481)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Baroreceptor Reflex** (pp. 476–478)
- **Atrial Stretch Reflexes** (p. 478)
- **Measurement of Blood Pressure** (pp. 478–480)
- **Pulse Pressure and Mean Arterial Pressure** (pp. 480–481)

### Core claims
- **Arterial blood pressure ∝ CO × TPR** (combining all four determinants):
  $$\mathrm{BP_{arterial}} \propto \mathrm{CO} \times \mathrm{TPR} = (\mathrm{HR} \times \mathrm{SV}) \times \mathrm{TPR}$$
- Pressure falls steeply across the arterioles (Fig 14.16): from ~120/80 mmHg in large arteries to ~30 mmHg at capillaries to ~0 mmHg at the right atrium.
- **Baroreceptors (Fig 14.27):** stretch receptors in the **aortic arch** and **carotid sinus**. Tonically active; firing frequency rises with stretch (and pressure). Afferents travel via **CN X (vagus)** from the aortic arch and **CN IX (glossopharyngeal)** from the carotid sinus to the medulla oblongata.
- **Baroreceptor reflex pathway (Fig 14.28):**
  1. **Sensor:** aortic + carotid baroreceptors detect arterial pressure.
  2. **Integrating center:** cardiac control center + vasomotor center in the medulla oblongata.
  3. **Effectors:** sympathetic (↑HR, ↑SV, vasoconstriction → ↑TPR) and parasympathetic (↓HR).
- **Standing from supine:** 500–700 mL of blood pools in lower-limb veins → ↓venous return → ↓SV → ↓CO → ↓BP → baroreceptor firing decreases → sympathetic activation, parasympathetic withdrawal → reflex tachycardia + vasoconstriction → BP restored.
- **Orthostatic hypotension** occurs when this reflex fails (dehydration, β-blockers, autonomic dysfunction, postprandial hypotension in the elderly).
- **Carotid sinus massage** triggers the reflex and can slow tachycardia; bilateral massage can cause syncope or cardiac arrest in susceptible people.
- **Atrial stretch reflexes:** atrial volume-receptors activated by ↑venous return → reflex tachycardia (sympathetic), inhibition of ADH, secretion of **atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)**.
- **Valsalva maneuver:** forced expiration against a closed glottis (e.g., weightlifting, straining at stool). Raises intrathoracic pressure → compresses thoracic veins → ↓venous return → ↓CO → ↓aortic pressure → baroreceptor reflex ↑HR; on release, venous return surges → BP overshoots → reflex ↓HR. Dangerous in coronary disease.
- **Pulse pressure (PP) = SBP − DBP** ≈ 40 mmHg in a 120/80 mmHg adult; reflects stroke volume.
- **Mean arterial pressure (MAP):** time-averaged pressure during the cardiac cycle (diastole longer than systole, so not a simple average):
  $$\mathrm{MAP} \approx \mathrm{DBP} + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \mathrm{PP} = 80 + \tfrac{1}{3}(40) \approx 93\ \mathrm{mmHg}$$
- **Auscultatory method (Fig 14.30):**
  - Cuff inflated above SBP → artery occluded → no sound.
  - Cuff lowered to SBP → first **Korotkoff sound** (turbulent flow); cuff pressure = SBP.
  - Continued descent → Korotkoff sounds persist as cuff pressure stays above DBP.
  - At DBP → cuff = DBP → artery now patent throughout cycle → laminar flow → silence resumes (last Korotkoff sound).
- **Five phases of Korotkoff sounds (Fig 14.32):** (1) snapping; (2) murmurs; (3) thumping; (4) muffling (sometimes used as DBP); (5) silence (true DBP).
- **Pulmonary vs. systemic pressures:** pulmonary 22/8 mmHg; systemic 120/80 mmHg. RV pumps against much lower resistance and has thinner walls.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The most important variables affecting blood pressure are thus the cardiac rate, stroke volume… and total peripheral resistance." (p. 475)
- > "The baroreceptor reflex is the most important regulator of arterial blood pressure on a beat-to-beat basis (longer-term regulation of blood pressure is achieved by the kidneys through regulation of blood volume)." (p. 477)
- > "When the cuff pressure is equal to the systolic pressure, the first Korotkoff sound is heard as blood passes in a turbulent flow through the constricted opening of the artery." (p. 479)
- > "The last Korotkoff sound thus occurs when the cuff pressure is equal to the diastolic pressure." (p. 479)
- > "Pulse pressure = systolic pressure – diastolic pressure." (p. 480)
- > "Mean arterial pressure = diastolic pressure + 1/3 pulse pressure." (p. 481)
- > "The average pulmonary arterial blood pressure is only 22/8 mmHg." (p. 480)

### Equations / quantitative

**Arterial blood pressure determinants:**
$$\mathrm{BP_{arterial}} \propto \mathrm{CO} \times \mathrm{TPR}$$

**Pulse pressure:**
$$\mathrm{PP} = \mathrm{SBP} - \mathrm{DBP}$$

**Mean arterial pressure (rule of thumb):**
$$\mathrm{MAP} \approx \mathrm{DBP} + \tfrac{1}{3}\mathrm{PP}$$

Example: 120/80 mmHg → MAP ≈ 80 + 13.3 ≈ 93 mmHg.

#### Figure 14.30 — Blood flow and Korotkoff sounds during a blood pressure measurement *(viewed)*

## Section 14.7 — Hypertension, Shock, and Congestive Heart Failure (pp. 481–486)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

  - **Dangers of Hypertension** (p. 483)
  - **Treatment of Hypertension** (p. 483)
- **Circulatory Shock** (pp. 483–485)
  - **Hypovolemic Shock** (pp. 484–485)
  - **Septic Shock** (p. 485)
  - **Other Causes of Circulatory Shock** (p. 485)
- **Congestive Heart Failure** (pp. 485–486)

### Core claims
- **Hypertension classification (ACC/AHA 2017, Table 14.8):**
  - **Normal:** SBP <120 AND DBP <80 mmHg.
  - **Elevated:** SBP 120–129 AND DBP <80.
  - **Stage 1 hypertension:** SBP 130–139 OR DBP 80–89.
  - **Stage 2 hypertension:** SBP ≥140 OR DBP ≥90.
- **Primary (essential) hypertension** = 95% of cases; multifactorial (salt sensitivity, RAAS dysregulation, endothelin/NO imbalance, increased SNS activity, leptin in obesity, age-related decline in glomerular filtration and Na⁺ excretion).
- **Secondary hypertension** (~5%) has identifiable causes (Table 14.9):
  - **Kidney disease** (↓urine formation; ↑renin).
  - **Endocrine:** pheochromocytoma (adrenal medulla catecholamine tumor); Conn's syndrome (adrenal cortex aldosterone-secreting tumor).
  - **Nervous:** ↑intracranial pressure; vasomotor center damage.
  - **Cardiovascular:** patent ductus arteriosus, aortic coarctation.
- **Dietary salt and BP:** high-Na⁺ diet raises BP in salt-sensitive individuals (esp. older adults, hypertensives). High-K⁺ diet (fruits, vegetables) lowers BP (potassium-rich diet acts as a natural diuretic).
- **Dangers of HTN:** "silent killer" — asymptomatic until vascular damage. Effects include:
  - **↑afterload** → LV hypertrophy → CHF / arrhythmia risk.
  - **Cerebrovascular accident (stroke).**
  - **Atherosclerosis** acceleration.
- **Preeclampsia:** new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of pregnancy with end-organ damage (proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, edema). Risk factor: obesity. Cure: delivery.
- **Antihypertensive drug classes (Table 14.10):**
  - **Diuretics** (thiazide, furosemide): ↓BV.
  - **Sympathoadrenal inhibitors:** central α2 agonists (clonidine, α-methyldopa), NE depleters (reserpine, guanethidine), β-blockers (atenolol), α-blockers (phentolamine).
  - **Direct vasodilators** (hydralazine, minoxidil, nitroprusside).
  - **Calcium-channel blockers** (verapamil, diltiazem, dihydropyridines).
  - **ACE inhibitors** (captopril, enalapril, lisinopril).
  - **ARBs** (losartan, valsartan, telmisartan).
  - **Direct renin inhibitors** (newer).
- **Circulatory shock = inadequate tissue perfusion / oxygen utilization.**
  - **Signs (Table 14.11):** early — decreased pulse pressure, increased diastolic, ↑urine osmolality + ↓urinary Na⁺, alkalosis from hyperventilation, restlessness. Late — ↓SBP, ↓urine volume, metabolic acidosis, cold clammy skin, "cloudy" senses.
  - **Compensatory mechanisms (Table 14.12):** sympathoadrenal-driven tachycardia + positive inotropy; vasoconstriction of skin/GI/kidneys (diverting flow to brain + heart); ↓urine output via aldosterone + ADH.
- **Hypovolemic shock:** from hemorrhage, dehydration, burns. Activates baroreflex + RAAS + ADH. Coronary + cerebral resistance unchanged, so blood is preferentially shunted to heart and brain (similar to the dive reflex of marine mammals).
- **Septic shock:** sepsis with persistent hypotension. Bacterial endotoxin (LPS) activates macrophage **iNOS** → massive **NO release** → systemic vasodilation → hypotension. Mortality 50–70%. Inhibition of NO production has been used therapeutically.
- **Anaphylactic shock:** systemic histamine from severe allergic reaction (bee stings, penicillin) → widespread vasodilation → ↓TPR → ↓BP.
- **Neurogenic shock:** loss of sympathetic tone (upper spinal cord injury, spinal anesthesia).
- **Cardiogenic shock:** primary pump failure (MI, severe arrhythmia, valve disease).
- **Congestive heart failure (CHF):** CO inadequate to meet tissue demand. Causes:
  - **Left ventricular failure:** MI, aortic stenosis, mitral or aortic regurgitation → ↑LA pressure → pulmonary congestion + edema → dyspnea, fatigue, potentially fatal.
  - **Right ventricular failure:** usually secondary to LV failure; → ↑RA pressure → systemic venous congestion + dependent edema (legs, ascites).
- **Electrolyte effects on cardiac arrest:** ↑K⁺ + ↓Ca²⁺ → arrest in diastole; ↓K⁺ + ↑Ca²⁺ → arrest in systole.
- **CHF compensations mirror hypovolemic shock:** sympathoadrenal activation, RAAS, ↓urine output, blood volume rises → ↑EDV → enlarged ventricles → maladaptive remodeling and pulmonary congestion.
- **CHF treatment (in order of importance):**
  - **ACE inhibitors / ARBs** (first-line) — reduce afterload, prevent remodeling.
  - **β-blockers** (e.g., metoprolol, carvedilol) — reduce sympathetic-driven myocardial damage.
  - **Diuretics** (furosemide, spironolactone) — relieve congestion.
  - **Vasodilators** (nitroglycerin).
  - **Digitalis / digoxin** — positive inotrope; helps in select cases.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Hypertension that is the result of complex and poorly understood processes is called primary, or essential, hypertension." (p. 481)
- > "Because most patients are asymptomatic (without symptoms) until substantial vascular damage has occurred, hypertension is often referred to as a silent killer." (p. 483)
- > "Circulatory shock occurs when there is inadequate blood flow and/or oxygen utilization by the tissues." (p. 483)
- > "Septic shock has recently been treated effectively with drugs that inhibit the production of nitric oxide." (p. 485)
- > "Endotoxin activates the enzyme nitric oxide synthase within macrophages… Nitric oxide synthase produces nitric oxide, which promotes vasodilation and, as a result, a fall in blood pressure." (p. 485)

## Clinical apparatus

- **Clinical Investigation: Mark** —
  - **Crohn's disease** → protein-losing enteropathy → **hypoproteinemia** → ↓πp → ↑net filtration → leg edema and abdominal swelling. Treatment: address underlying inflammation; once plasma proteins normalize, edema resolves via lymphatic drainage.
  - **Marathon training on a hot day** → sweat-induced hypovolemia + Na⁺/K⁺/Cl⁻ losses → **orthostatic hypotension**. Advice: pre-hydrate with sports drinks (Na⁺/K⁺/Cl⁻ + sugar) for prolonged exercise (>60 min).
  - **Essential hypertension** → prescribed **ACE inhibitor** (blocks angiotensin I → angiotensin II → ↓vasoconstriction → ↓TPR → ↓BP).
  - Advice **not to perform Valsalva maneuver** during heavy lifting → because HTN is a CVD risk factor and Valsalva-induced pressure swings worsen the strain.
- **Clinical Application — Filariasis** (p. 458): nematode worms block lymphatic drainage → elephantiasis.
- **Clinical Application — ACE inhibitors / ARBs / ACE2 / SARS-CoV-2** (p. 461): inhibition of the RAAS lowers BP and protects post-MI cardiac function; ACE2 (separate enzyme that destroys angiotensin II) is the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein receptor.
- **Clinical Application — Orthostatic hypotension** (p. 477): pooling of ~700 mL in lower limb veins → ↓CO → must be compensated by baroreflex.
- **Clinical Application — Carotid sinus massage** (p. 478): can terminate tachycardia but bilateral massage can cause syncope or cardiac arrest.
- **Clinical Application — Coronary angiogram / angioplasty / stent / CABG** (p. 468).
- **Clinical Application — Preeclampsia** (p. 483): new-onset HTN + proteinuria + edema during pregnancy → can progress to seizures and stroke; only cure is delivery.
- **Lifestyle Applications** — Hydration during exercise; salt in human history; exercise effects on cardiac rate and SV; exercise + diet for cardiovascular health.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Cardiac output (CO)** — HR × SV; ~5.5 L/min at rest, up to 25 L/min during exercise.
- **Stroke volume (SV) / end-diastolic volume (EDV) / end-systolic volume (ESV) / ejection fraction (EF)** — volume ejected per beat / chamber volume before contraction / chamber volume after / SV÷EDV.
- **Preload / afterload** — EDV / arterial pressure the ventricle must overcome.
- **Frank-Starling law** — SV ∝ EDV via length-tension and Ca²⁺ sensitization.
- **Anrep effect** — delayed (10–15 min) inotropic adaptation to stretch via Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger.
- **Contractility / inotropy** — strength of contraction at a given fiber length.
- **Chronotropy** — rate effect (positive = ↑HR; negative = ↓HR).
- **HCN channels / funny current** — pacemaker Na⁺ channels opened by hyperpolarization; cAMP-sensitive.
- **Capacitance / resistance vessels** — veins (compliant) / arterioles (high-resistance).
- **Venous return / skeletal muscle pump / respiratory pump** — return of blood to right atrium / muscular compression of veins / inspiratory thoracic vacuum.
- **Total peripheral resistance (TPR)** — sum of vascular resistances in systemic circuit.
- **Poiseuille's law** — F ∝ ΔP·r⁴ / ηL.
- **Starling forces (Pc, Pi, πp, πi)** — capillary hydrostatic, interstitial hydrostatic, plasma colloid osmotic, interstitial colloid osmotic.
- **Oncotic pressure** — plasma colloid osmotic pressure (~25 mmHg) due primarily to albumin.
- **Edema** — interstitial fluid accumulation; six causes (↑BP, venous obstruction, capillary leak, myxedema, hypoproteinemia, lymphatic obstruction).
- **Lymphatic return** — ~10–15% of filtrate (~1–2 L/day) plus interstitial protein.
- **ADH / vasopressin** — posterior pituitary hormone; ↑water reabsorption.
- **Aldosterone** — adrenal cortex mineralocorticoid; ↑Na⁺ and water reabsorption.
- **Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)** — atrial hormone; ↑Na⁺ and water excretion.
- **Renin / angiotensinogen / angiotensin I / ACE / angiotensin II / ACE2** — kidney enzyme / liver substrate / 10-AA intermediate / pulmonary endothelial cleavage enzyme / 8-AA active vasoconstrictor / its inactivator (also SARS-CoV-2 receptor).
- **Juxtaglomerular apparatus** — renal sensor for BP and tubular Na⁺; secretes renin.
- **ACE inhibitors / ARBs / direct renin inhibitors** — antihypertensive drug classes acting on RAAS.
- **Endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) / NO / cGMP** — vasodilator paracrine cascade.
- **Endothelin-1** — endothelial vasoconstrictor; target for pulmonary hypertension drugs.
- **Prostacyclin (PGI₂)** — endothelial vasodilator and antiaggregant.
- **Bradykinin** — local vasodilator from sweat glands and endothelium.
- **Autoregulation** — intrinsic maintenance of constant flow despite pressure changes; myogenic + metabolic mechanisms.
- **Reactive hyperemia / active hyperemia** — post-occlusion overshoot / exercise-driven flow increase.
- **Arteriovenous anastomosis** — direct arteriole-to-venule shunt; abundant in skin.
- **Coronary blood flow / coronary autoregulation / myoglobin** — myocardial perfusion mechanism (greatest in diastole).
- **Angiogram / angioplasty / stent / CABG** — coronary diagnostic and therapeutic procedures.
- **Functional hyperemia / neurovascular coupling / BOLD fMRI** — activity-driven cerebral blood flow.
- **Carotid sinus / aortic arch baroreceptors** — arterial pressure stretch receptors.
- **Baroreceptor reflex** — afferent CN IX & X → medullary cardiac and vasomotor centers → autonomic effectors.
- **Cardiac control center / vasomotor center** — medullary integrators.
- **Atrial stretch reflex** — volume-receptor reflex; ↑HR, ↓ADH, ↑ANP.
- **Valsalva maneuver** — forced expiration against closed glottis → biphasic CV response.
- **Korotkoff sounds** — turbulent-flow sounds during sphygmomanometry; first = SBP, last = DBP.
- **Pulse pressure / mean arterial pressure** — SBP–DBP / DBP + ⅓ PP.
- **Sphygmomanometer / auscultatory method** — pressure cuff + stethoscope; indirect BP measurement.
- **Primary (essential) vs. secondary hypertension** — idiopathic multifactorial / caused by identifiable disease.
- **Preeclampsia** — new-onset gestational hypertension with end-organ damage.
- **Circulatory shock subtypes** — hypovolemic / septic / anaphylactic / neurogenic / cardiogenic.
- **Endotoxin / iNOS / NO in septic shock** — bacterial LPS → macrophage NO → vasodilation.
- **Congestive heart failure (CHF) — left / right** — pulmonary edema / systemic edema.
- **β-blocker / ACE inhibitor / ARB / diuretic / digoxin / nitroglycerin** — heart failure drug classes.
