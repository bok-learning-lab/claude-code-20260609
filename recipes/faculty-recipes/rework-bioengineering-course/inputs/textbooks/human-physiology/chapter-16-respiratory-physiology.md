## Section 16.1 — The Respiratory System (pp. 532–535)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Structure of the Respiratory System** (pp. 532–535)
- **Thoracic Cavity** (p. 535)

### Core claims
- **Respiration** comprises three processes: (1) **ventilation** (breathing), (2) **gas exchange** between air and blood (and between blood and tissues), and (3) **cell respiration** (O₂ utilization for ATP production). Lung gas exchange = **external respiration**; tissue gas exchange + cellular utilization = **internal respiration**.
- **Alveoli:** ~300 million per lung; ~100 μm diameter; total surface area 60–80 m² (~760 ft²). The **air-blood barrier** is only 0.3 μm thick (type I alveolar cell + fused basement membrane + capillary endothelium).
- **Two alveolar cell types** (Fig 16.1): **Type I** (95–97% of alveolar surface area; site of gas exchange; extremely thin); **Type II** (secrete pulmonary surfactant; reabsorb Na⁺ and H₂O to prevent alveolar flooding). **Alveolar macrophages** clear inhaled particulates.
- **Conducting zone** (Fig 16.4): nose → pharynx → larynx (vocal folds) → trachea → primary, secondary, tertiary bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles. Function: warming, humidification, filtration, conduction. No gas exchange. ~23 generations of branching to alveoli.
- **Respiratory zone:** respiratory bronchioles + alveolar ducts + alveolar sacs.
- **Mucociliary escalator:** 300 cilia per epithelial cell beat coordinately to move mucus toward pharynx (1–2 cm/min). Cigarette smoke and **cystic fibrosis** (CFTR chloride channel mutation → thick, dehydrated mucus) impair clearance.
- **Pleural membranes:** **parietal pleura** lines thoracic wall; **visceral pleura** covers lung surface. Normally apposed; only a thin fluid film (the **intrapleural space** is a "potential space" until lung collapses).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Gas exchange in each lung occurs across an estimated 300 million tiny (about 100 μm in diameter) air sacs known as pulmonary alveoli. Their enormous number provides a large surface area (60 to 80 square meters, or about 760 square feet) for diffusion of gases." (p. 532)
- > "There are two types of alveolar cells … The type I alveolar cells comprise 95% to 97% of the total surface area of the lung; gas exchange with the blood thus occurs primarily through type I alveolar cells … The type II alveolar cells are the cells that secrete pulmonary surfactant." (p. 532)
- > "There are about 300 cilia per cell that beat in a coordinated fashion as a mucociliary escalator to propel the mucus toward the pharynx." (p. 535)

## Section 16.2 — Physical Aspects of Ventilation (pp. 535–539)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Intrapulmonary and Intrapleural Pressures** (pp. 536–537)
  - **Boyle's Law** (p. 537)
- **Physical Properties of the Lungs** (pp. 537–539)
  - **Compliance** (p. 537)
  - **Elasticity** (p. 537)
  - **Surface Tension** (p. 538)
- **Surfactant and Respiratory Distress Syndrome** (p. 539)

### Core claims
- **Pressures during ventilation** (Table 16.1; Fig 16.14): atmospheric is reference (0 cmH₂O). Intrapulmonary pressure goes to **−1 cmH₂O** (inspiration) and **+1 cmH₂O** (expiration). Intrapleural pressure is always subatmospheric: **−8 cmH₂O** (inspiration) and **−5 cmH₂O** (expiration). The **transpulmonary pressure** (intrapulmonary − intrapleural) is always positive (+6 to +7 cmH₂O) — this is the pressure that keeps lung against chest wall.
- **Boyle's Law:** at constant temperature, $PV = k$, i.e., pressure inversely proportional to volume. Diaphragm contracts → thoracic volume ↑ → intrapulmonary pressure ↓ → air enters.
- **Compliance:** distensibility = $\Delta V/\Delta P$. Lungs are ~100× more compliant than a toy balloon. Reduced by pulmonary fibrosis (infiltration with stiff collagen).
- **Elasticity:** elastin proteins provide tension → recoil → expiration. Lungs always under elastic tension stretched between chest wall (recoils outward) and lung tissue (recoils inward).
- **Surface tension** of alveolar fluid film promotes alveolar collapse. **Law of Laplace** for a sphere: $P = 2T/r$ — smaller alveoli would have higher pressure and would empty into larger ones without surfactant.
- **Pulmonary surfactant** (Fig 16.11): secreted by type II alveolar cells; primarily phosphatidylcholine + phosphatidylglycerol + hydrophobic surfactant proteins. Inserts between water molecules at the air-fluid interface, breaks H-bonds, reduces surface tension. Effect strengthens as alveoli shrink (surfactant molecules concentrate). Prevents alveolar collapse and reduces the work of subsequent inspirations.
- **Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS):** premature neonates lack mature surfactant production → alveolar collapse → severe hypoxemia. Treatment: exogenous surfactant via endotracheal tube + mechanical ventilation. Incidence: 60% of babies <28 weeks; <5% after 34 weeks.
- **Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS):** sepsis or pneumonia-induced inflammation → increased capillary permeability → protein-rich alveolar fluid → reduced compliance and surfactant.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Air enters the lungs during inspiration because the atmospheric pressure is greater than the intrapulmonary, or intra-alveolar, pressure." (p. 537)
- > "Because of the elastic tension of the lungs … and the thoracic wall on each other, the lungs pull in one direction (they 'try' to collapse) while the thoracic wall pulls in the opposite direction (it 'tries' to expand). The opposing elastic recoil of the lungs and the chest wall produces a subatmospheric pressure in the intrapleural space." (p. 537)
- > "As described by the law of Laplace, the pressure thus created is directly proportional to the surface tension and inversely proportional to the radius of the alveolus." (p. 538)
- > "Surfactant becomes interspersed between water molecules at the water–air interface, which reduces the hydrogen bonds between water molecules at the surface and thereby reduces the surface tension. As a result of this surfactant effect, the surface tension of the alveoli is negligible." (p. 539)

### Equations / quantitative

**Boyle's law (isothermal):**
$$P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 \quad (\text{at constant } T)$$

**Transpulmonary pressure:**
$$P_{\text{transpulmonary}} = P_{\text{intrapulmonary}} - P_{\text{intrapleural}}$$

**Compliance:**
$$C_L = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta P_{\text{transpulmonary}}}$$

**Law of Laplace (alveolus):**
$$P = \frac{2T}{r}$$

### Core claims
- **Quiet inspiration:** active contraction of **diaphragm** (phrenic nerve, C3–C5) + **external intercostals** and **parasternal intercostals** → thoracic volume ↑.
- **Forced inspiration:** also recruits **scalenes**, **pectoralis minor**, **sternocleidomastoid**.
- **Quiet expiration:** passive elastic recoil of stretched lungs and thorax.
- **Forced expiration:** active contraction of **internal intercostals** (depress ribs) + **abdominal muscles** (push diaphragm up) → intrapulmonary pressure to +40 cmH₂O or more.
- **Lung volumes and capacities** (Table 16.3; Fig 16.15):
  - **Tidal volume (TV)** ≈ 500 ml per breath at rest.
  - **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** — extra forced inhalation above TV.
  - **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** — extra forced exhalation below TV.
  - **Residual volume (RV)** ≈ 1200 ml — cannot be expired; cannot be spirometrically measured.
  - **Vital capacity (VC)** = IRV + TV + ERV ≈ 4800 ml.
  - **Total lung capacity (TLC)** = VC + RV ≈ 6000 ml.
  - **Functional residual capacity (FRC)** = ERV + RV — volume at end of normal expiration.
  - **Inspiratory capacity (IC)** = TV + IRV.
- **Total minute volume** = tidal volume × breaths/min ≈ 6 L/min at rest; 100–200 L/min during exercise.
- **Anatomical dead space** = volume of conducting zone (~150 ml); fresh air mixes with dead-space air; effective alveolar ventilation = (TV − dead space) × respiratory rate.
- **Restrictive vs. obstructive disorders:**
  - **Restrictive** (pulmonary fibrosis): VC decreased, FEV1/FVC normal. Lung tissue stiffens.
  - **Obstructive** (asthma, emphysema, COPD): VC may be normal but **FEV1** (forced expiratory volume in 1 sec) reduced (<80% of VC), because of bronchoconstriction or alveolar destruction increasing airway resistance.
- **Asthma:** chronic inflammatory disorder with bronchial hyperresponsiveness. Triggered by allergens (IgE, mast cells; Ch 15), exercise (airway dehydration), cold dry air, aspirin. TH2 lymphokines → IgE → mast-cell histamine + leukotrienes + prostaglandins → bronchoconstriction + mucus + eosinophil infiltration. Treatment: **β2-adrenergic agonists** (albuterol short-acting; salmeterol long-acting) for bronchodilation; **inhaled corticosteroids** for inflammation; **leukotriene receptor antagonists** (montelukast).
- **Emphysema:** alveolar wall destruction → fewer, larger alveoli → decreased gas-exchange surface area + loss of lateral tension that holds bronchioles open → bronchiolar collapse during expiration → **air trapping**. ~90% caused by smoking; proteinases (matrix metalloproteinases, elastase) from inflammatory cells degrade extracellular matrix, especially in α1-antitrypsin deficiency.
- **COPD:** = chronic obstructive bronchiolitis + emphysema. Smoking-induced inflammation and remodeling; accelerated FEV1 decline with age. Third leading cause of death worldwide. Pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale (right ventricular hypertrophy and failure).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "An unforced, or quiet, inspiration results primarily from contraction of the dome-shaped diaphragm, which lowers and flattens when it contracts." (p. 540)
- > "An FEV1 that is significantly less than 80% suggests the presence of obstructive pulmonary disease." (p. 543)
- > "The dyspnea, wheezing, and other symptoms of asthma are produced by an obstruction of air flow through the bronchioles that occurs in episodes, or 'attacks.' This obstruction is caused by inflammation, mucous secretion, and bronchoconstriction." (p. 544)
- > "About 90% of people with COPD are (or have been) smokers." (p. 545)
- > "COPD is currently the third leading cause of death worldwide." (p. 545)

## Section 16.4 — Gas Exchange in the Lungs (pp. 546–552)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Calculation of P_O2** (pp. 546–547)
- **Partial Pressures of Gases in Blood** (pp. 547–549)
  - **Blood Gas Measurements** (pp. 547–549)

- **Pulmonary Circulation and Ventilation/Perfusion Ratios** (pp. 549–551)
- **Disorders Caused by High Partial Pressures of Gases** (pp. 551–552)
  - **Oxygen Toxicity** (p. 551)
  - **Nitrogen Narcosis** (p. 551)
  - **Decompression Sickness** (p. 552)

### Core claims
- **Dalton's law:** total pressure of a gas mixture = sum of partial pressures. Each gas's partial pressure = total × fraction.
  - Sea-level atmospheric pressure ≈ 760 mmHg (= 760 torr = 1 atm).
  - Dry air: ~21% O₂, ~78% N₂, ~0.04% CO₂.
  - **Dry-atmosphere P_O2 = 0.21 × 760 = 159 mmHg.**
- **Water vapor (47 mmHg)** at body temperature dilutes alveolar gas partial pressures:
  - $P_{O_2, \text{inspired (saturated, sea level)}} = 0.21 \times (760 - 47) = 150 \text{ mmHg}$
  - **Alveolar P_O2 ≈ 105 mmHg** (further lowered by CO₂ release into alveoli).
  - **Arterial P_O2 ≈ 100 mmHg** (5 mmHg lower than alveolar due to V/Q mismatch).
- **Henry's law:** dissolved gas concentration in a fluid ∝ partial pressure of that gas above the fluid. Plasma at P_O₂ = 100 mmHg contains 0.3 ml O₂/100 ml plasma — only the dissolved O₂ contributes to P_O₂; hemoglobin-bound O₂ does not.
- **Blood gas values** (Fig 16.22):
  - Pulmonary artery (venous-side): **P_O₂ = 40, P_CO₂ = 46 mmHg**
  - Alveolar air: **P_O₂ = 105, P_CO₂ = 40 mmHg**
  - Pulmonary vein / systemic artery: **P_O₂ = 100, P_CO₂ = 40 mmHg**
  - Tissue cells / systemic veins: **P_O₂ = 40, P_CO₂ = 46 mmHg**
- **Altitude (Table 16.5):** P_atm decreases with altitude → P_O₂ decreases. At Denver (5,000 ft) P_atm = 619, alveolar P_O₂ = 79. At Mt. Everest summit (29,000 ft) P_atm = 226, alveolar P_O₂ ≈ 21 mmHg.
- **Pulse oximetry (Clinical Application):** non-invasive measurement of % oxyhemoglobin saturation using red (600–750 nm) and infrared (850–1,000 nm) LEDs and a photodetector. Oxy-Hb absorbs more IR; deoxy-Hb absorbs more red. Two-wavelength ratio yields O₂ saturation.
- **Pulmonary circulation:** low-resistance, low-pressure (pulmonary artery mean 15 mmHg vs. systemic 100 mmHg). Driving pressure 15 − 5 = 10 mmHg; same cardiac output (~5.5 L/min) → pulmonary vascular resistance is ~10% of systemic. Low capillary pressure protects against pulmonary edema.
- **Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction:** pulmonary arterioles **constrict** when alveolar P_O₂ is low (opposite of systemic arterioles). Mitochondrial sensing → membrane depolarization → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open → smooth-muscle contraction. Diverts blood away from poorly ventilated alveoli. Chronic global hypoxia (high altitude, COPD) → pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale.
- **V/Q matching (Fig 16.23):** apex of lung is over-ventilated (V/Q ≈ 3.4); base is over-perfused (V/Q ≈ 0.6). Mismatch produces the 5 mmHg arterial-alveolar P_O₂ gap.
- **Disorders at high P_gas:**
  - **Oxygen toxicity** at P_O₂ > 2.5 atm (deep diving with pure O₂): oxidation of enzymes → CNS damage.
  - **Nitrogen narcosis** at depth: dissolved N₂ → "rapture of the deep" (alcohol-like intoxication).
  - **Decompression sickness ("the bends"):** rapid ascent → N₂ bubbles in tissues and blood → joint pain, neurological injury.
  - **Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT):** 100% O₂ at 2–3 atm → up to 6 ml O₂/100 ml plasma (vs. 0.3 ml at sea level air); treats decompression sickness, CO poisoning, gas gangrene, crush injuries, diabetic wounds.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "According to Dalton's law, the total pressure of a gas mixture (such as air) is equal to the sum of the pressures that each gas in the mixture would exert independently." (p. 546)
- > "When the effect of water vapor pressure is considered, the partial pressure of oxygen in the inspired air is decreased at sea level to P_O2 (sea level) = 0.21 (760 − 47) = 150 mmHg." (p. 546)
- > "Because the driving pressure in the pulmonary circulation is only one-tenth that of the systemic circulation and yet the flow rates are equal, it follows that the pulmonary vascular resistance must be one-tenth that of the systemic vascular resistance." (p. 550)
- > "Pulmonary arterioles constrict when the alveolar P_O2 is low and dilate as the alveolar P_O2 is raised. This response is opposite to that of systemic arterioles." (p. 550)
- > "Functionally, the alveoli at the apex of the lungs are overventilated (or underperfused) and they are larger than alveoli at the base." (p. 551)

### Equations / quantitative

**Dalton's law:**
$$P_{\text{total}} = \sum_i P_i \quad \text{and} \quad P_i = P_{\text{total}} \cdot f_i$$

**P_O₂ in dry atmospheric air at sea level:**
$$P_{O_2} = 0.21 \times 760 = 159 \text{ mmHg}$$

**P_O₂ in saturated inspired air (with water vapor 47 mmHg):**
$$P_{O_2,\text{inspired}} = 0.21 \times (760 - 47) = 150 \text{ mmHg}$$

**Wet-atmosphere gas decomposition:**
$$P_{\text{atm}} = P_{N_2} + P_{O_2} + P_{CO_2} + P_{H_2O}$$

**Henry's law (dissolved gas in fluid):**
$$[X]_{\text{dissolved}} \propto P_X$$

**Alveolar ventilation:**
$$\dot{V}_A = (V_T - V_D) \cdot f$$

where $V_T$ = tidal volume, $V_D$ = anatomical dead space, $f$ = breaths/min.

**Ventilation/perfusion ratio:**
$$V/Q = \frac{\text{alveolar ventilation}}{\text{pulmonary capillary blood flow}}$$

## Section 16.5 — Regulation of Breathing (pp. 552–558)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Brain Stem Respiratory Centers** (pp. 552–554)

  - **Peripheral Chemoreceptors** (pp. 555–556)
- **Effects of Blood P_O2 on Ventilation** (pp. 556–557)

### Core claims
- **Rhythmogenic center:** **pre-Bötzinger complex** in the ventrolateral medulla generates the inspiratory rhythm via automatic bursting neurons. A second expiratory rhythmicity center (active during exercise, silent at rest) lies superior to it.
- **Pontine centers:** **apneustic center** stimulates inspiration; **pneumotaxic center** inhibits inspiration. Roles in normal human breathing remain unclear but the pons contributes to the hypoxic ventilatory response.
- **Phrenic nerve** (C3–C5) innervates diaphragm; cervical spinal cord lesions above C4 → loss of spontaneous breathing.
- **Central chemoreceptors:** in the **retrotrapezoid nucleus** on the ventrolateral medulla; respond to pH of brain interstitial fluid and CSF. Blood-brain barrier blocks H⁺ but allows CO₂. CO₂ → carbonic acid → H⁺ in CSF → stimulates breathing. Account for 70–80% of the sustained ventilatory response to elevated arterial CO₂ (takes minutes).
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors:** **carotid bodies** (carotid bifurcation; signal via glossopharyngeal nerve IX) and **aortic bodies** (aortic arch; signal via vagus nerve X). Respond to: rise in arterial CO₂ (indirectly via H⁺), fall in pH (directly), and fall in P_O₂ below ~70 mmHg (direct effect). Provide immediate ventilatory response. **2019 Nobel Prize** to Kaelin, Semenza, Ratcliffe for elucidating cellular oxygen sensing.
- **Sensitivity ranking (Table 16.6):**
  - Normal range: P_CO₂ is the primary regulator; even small CO₂ rise → immediate ventilation increase.
  - P_O₂ stimulates breathing only when <70 mmHg ("hypoxic drive"; carotid body glomus cells).
  - Hypoxia augments chemoreceptor sensitivity to CO₂.
- **Emphysema with chronically elevated CO₂:** central chemoreceptor response blunted (CSF HCO₃⁻ buffers the pH change); patient becomes O₂-driven. Giving high O₂ can suppress breathing.
- **SIDS:** sudden infant death syndrome; possibly chemoreceptor failure to detect CO₂ rise. Incidence reduced by back-sleeping position.
- **Obstructive sleep apnea:** upper-airway collapse during sleep; apnea → hypoxemia, hypercapnia → arousal with gasp. Risk factor: obesity, male sex. Treatment: CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure).
- **Hering-Breuer reflex:** pulmonary stretch receptors inhibit further inspiration at tidal volumes >1 L; vagal afferents. More important in newborns and during deep exercise inspirations.
- **Irritant / rapidly adapting receptors and C-fiber afferents** trigger cough in response to pulmonary interstitial fluid, smoke, capsaicin.

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

  - **Anemia** (p. 562)

- **Muscle Myoglobin** (pp. 563–564)

### Core claims
- **Hemoglobin structure (Fig 16.32):** 4 polypeptide chains (2α of 141 aa + 2β of 146 aa) each carrying 1 heme group with a central Fe²⁺. **Each Hb binds 4 O₂.** ~280 million Hb per RBC → >10⁹ O₂ molecules per RBC.
- **Hemoglobin states:**
  - **Deoxyhemoglobin** — Fe²⁺, no O₂.
  - **Oxyhemoglobin** — Fe²⁺ with bound O₂ (NB: iron does *not* become oxidized; it shares electrons but remains Fe²⁺).
  - **Methemoglobin** — Fe³⁺; cannot bind O₂ (oxidized hemoglobin).
  - **Carboxyhemoglobin** — CO bound to Fe²⁺; CO affinity is **210× stronger** than O₂; basis of CO poisoning.
- **O₂ content of arterial blood:** ~20 ml O₂/100 ml blood (0.3 ml dissolved + 19.7 ml on Hb). **1.34 ml O₂/g Hb maximum.**
- **% oxyhemoglobin saturation** of arterial blood at sea level = 97% at P_O₂ = 100 mmHg.
- **Oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve (Fig 16.33):** S-shaped (cooperative O₂ binding). Flat at high P_O₂ (loading region; even 10,000 ft altitude lowers saturation only to ~93%). Steep at low P_O₂ (unloading region; venous P_O₂ 40 mmHg = 75% saturation → 22% unloading at rest).
- **Bohr effect (Fig 16.34; Table 16.8):** affinity of Hb for O₂ decreases as pH falls. Acidic blood (lower pH = higher H⁺ from CO₂ or lactate) shifts curve right → more unloading. Alkaline blood shifts curve left → less unloading. Mechanism: H⁺ binds deoxyhemoglobin more tightly than oxyhemoglobin, favoring O₂ release.
- **Temperature shift:** higher temperature → rightward shift (more unloading). Useful in exercising muscle.
- **2,3-DPG (Fig 16.35):** byproduct of RBC anaerobic glycolysis; binds deoxyhemoglobin, stabilizes it, lowers Hb-O₂ affinity → more unloading. 2,3-DPG production is inhibited by oxyhemoglobin → negative feedback; rises during anemia and high altitude.
- **Fetal hemoglobin (HbF):** 2α + 2γ chains; gamma chains do not bind 2,3-DPG → higher O₂ affinity → maternal-to-fetal O₂ transfer in placenta.
- **Sickle-cell anemia:** β-chain Glu → Val substitution (HbS); deoxygenated HbS polymerizes → sickled RBCs → vaso-occlusion + hemolysis. Treated with hydroxyurea (induces HbF), hematopoietic stem cell transplant, and emerging gene therapy.
- **Thalassemia:** α or β chain underproduction; β-thalassemia clinically more severe; transfusion-dependent.
- **Myoglobin (Fig 16.37):** single-chain, single-heme; in slow-twitch + cardiac muscle. Higher O₂ affinity than Hb (curve far to the left); rectangular shape → only releases O₂ at very low P_O₂ in mitochondria. Storage and intracellular shuttle of O₂.

- > "The bond strength between hemoglobin and oxygen is normally strong enough so that 97% of the hemoglobin leaving the lungs is in the form of oxyhemoglobin, yet the bond is sufficiently weak so that adequate amounts of oxygen are unloaded to sustain aerobic cell respiration in the tissues." ## Section 16.7 — Carbon Dioxide Transport (pp. 564–566)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The Chloride Shift** (pp. 564–565)
- **The Reverse Chloride Shift** (pp. 565–566)

### Core claims
- **Three forms of CO₂ in blood (Fig 16.38):**
  - **Dissolved CO₂** (~10%; CO₂ is ~21× more soluble than O₂).
  - **Carbaminohemoglobin** (~20%; CO₂ bonded to amine groups of hemoglobin — distinct from carboxyhemoglobin which is CO on iron).
  - **Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)** (~70%; the majority).
- **Carbonic anhydrase** catalyzes $\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3}$ inside RBCs (where the enzyme is concentrated). Then $\mathrm{H_2CO_3 \rightleftharpoons H^+ + HCO_3^-}$.
- **Chloride shift (tissues, Fig 16.38):** HCO₃⁻ generated in RBC diffuses out into plasma; to preserve electrical neutrality, Cl⁻ moves into RBC. Meanwhile, H⁺ is buffered by binding to deoxyhemoglobin (which has higher H⁺ affinity than oxyhemoglobin — this is the Bohr effect mechanism). The unloading of O₂ improves CO₂ transport, and vice versa.
- **Reverse chloride shift (lungs, Fig 16.39):** at the low P_CO₂ of pulmonary capillaries, the equilibria reverse — HCO₃⁻ re-enters RBC, combines with H⁺ to form H₂CO₃, which is dehydrated by carbonic anhydrase back to CO₂ and H₂O, exhaled.
- **Buffering function (Fig 16.40):** the bicarbonate generated by CO₂ transport also serves as the principal extracellular buffer for nonvolatile metabolic acids (lactate, ketoacids, fatty acids): $\mathrm{HCO_3^- + H^+ \to H_2CO_3 \to CO_2 + H_2O}$. The lungs blow off the resulting CO₂.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Carbon dioxide is carried by the blood in three forms: (1) as dissolved CO₂ in the plasma … (2) as carbaminohemoglobin … and (3) as bicarbonate ion, which accounts for most of the CO₂ carried by the blood." (p. 564)
- > "Because this enzyme is confined to the red blood cells, most of the carbonic acid is produced there rather than in the plasma." (p. 564)
- > "This exchange of anions as blood travels through the tissue capillaries is called the chloride shift." (p. 565)
- > "The unloading of oxygen is increased by the bonding of the H⁺ released from carbonic acid to oxyhemoglobin. This is the Bohr effect." (p. 565)

### Equations / quantitative

**Carbonic acid equilibria (catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase in RBCs):**
$$\mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{low } P_{CO_2}]{\text{high } P_{CO_2}} \mathrm{H_2CO_3} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{HCO_3^-}$$

**Bohr effect coupling (tissues):**
$$\mathrm{HbO_2} + \mathrm{H^+} \to \mathrm{Hb}\!\cdot\!\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{O_2}$$

## Section 16.8 — Acid-Base Balance of the Blood (pp. 566–569)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Principles of Acid-Base Balance** (pp. 567–568)
- **Ventilation and Acid-Base Balance** (p. 568)

### Core claims
- **Normal arterial pH:** 7.35–7.45 (mean 7.40). H⁺ concentration ≈ 10⁻⁷·⁴ M.
- **Volatile acid:** carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), regulated by ventilation (blown off as CO₂). All other acids — **nonvolatile** (lactic acid, fatty acids, ketone bodies) — are buffered by bicarbonate and the kidneys handle long-term excretion.
- **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:**
$$\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log \frac{[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{0.03 \cdot P_{CO_2}}$$
A normal pH of 7.40 corresponds to a 20:1 bicarbonate-to-CO₂ ratio.
- **Acid-base disturbances (Tables 16.10, 16.11, 16.12):**
  - **Respiratory acidosis:** hypoventilation → ↑P_CO₂ → ↓pH. Causes: COPD, oversedation, CNS depression.
  - **Respiratory alkalosis:** hyperventilation → ↓P_CO₂ → ↑pH. Causes: anxiety, fever, salicylate toxicity, high altitude.
  - **Metabolic acidosis:** ↓[HCO₃⁻]. Causes: ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, diarrhea (HCO₃⁻ loss), renal failure.
  - **Metabolic alkalosis:** ↑[HCO₃⁻]. Causes: vomiting (loss of gastric HCl), excessive bicarbonate intake.
- **Compensation:** primary respiratory disturbances → renal compensation (HCO₃⁻ excretion or generation) over **days**. Primary metabolic disturbances → respiratory compensation (hyper/hypoventilation) over **hours**.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis:** insulin deficiency → β-oxidation acetyl-CoA exceeds Krebs capacity → ketone bodies → H⁺ → ↓pH → compensatory hyperventilation (Kussmaul breathing) → ↓P_CO₂ → partial respiratory compensation. (See Ch 5, Ch 19.)

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Because CO₂ is a volatile gas released in the expired breath, carbonic acid is referred to as a volatile acid. This is significant because its blood concentration is uniquely regulated by breathing. All other acids in the blood—including lactic acid, fatty acids, ketone bodies, and so on—are nonvolatile acids that cannot be eliminated through ventilation." (p. 567)
- > "A fall in blood pH below 7.35 is called acidosis because the pH has shifted to the acid side of normal. Acidosis does not mean acidic (pH less than 7); a blood pH of 7.2, for example, represents serious acidosis." (p. 567)
- > "An acid-base disturbance caused primarily by abnormal changes in ventilation, producing abnormal arterial CO₂ levels, is described as respiratory acidosis or alkalosis." (p. 567)

## Section 16.9 — Effect of Exercise and High Altitude on Respiratory Function (pp. 569–574)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Ventilation During Exercise** (pp. 569–570)
  - **Lactate Threshold and Endurance Training** (p. 570)
- **Acclimatization to High Altitude** (pp. 570–573)
  - **Changes in Ventilation** (p. 571)
  - **The Affinity of Hemoglobin for Oxygen** (pp. 571–572)
  - **Increased Hemoglobin and Red Blood Cell Production** (pp. 572–573)

### Core claims
- **Exercise ventilation (hyperpnea):** total minute volume rises in proportion to CO₂ production / O₂ consumption. Arterial P_O₂, P_CO₂, and pH remain near resting values during moderate exercise. Both **neurogenic** (proprioceptor + cortical input) and **humoral** (cyclic local chemoreceptor stimulation) mechanisms drive the response.
- **Lactate threshold (≈ 50–70% VO₂max):** rate of muscle oxygen consumption beyond which anaerobic glycolysis dominates and lactate accumulates. Endurance training raises the lactate threshold by increasing muscle mitochondrial content, Krebs-cycle enzymes, and cardiac output.
- **Altitude (Table 16.14, Table 16.15):**
  - At 7,500 ft, arterial P_O₂ ≈ 69–74 mmHg; oxyhemoglobin saturation ≈ 92–93% (vs. 97% at sea level).
  - At Everest summit (29,000 ft), arterial P_O₂ ≈ 26–33 mmHg.
- **Acclimatization (Fig 16.42)** = a sequence of compensations:
  1. **Immediate (hours):** carotid bodies sense ↓P_O₂ → **hyperventilation** (hypoxic ventilatory response) → ↓P_CO₂ → respiratory alkalosis (which initially blunts further hyperventilation).
  2. **Days:** kidneys **excrete HCO₃⁻** to compensate the respiratory alkalosis → pH normalizes → hypoxic drive can persist; **2,3-DPG** rises in RBCs → right-shifts oxyhemoglobin curve → more unloading.
  3. **Days–weeks:** **erythropoietin** from kidneys → bone marrow produces more RBCs → **polycythemia** + increased hemoglobin (e.g., from 15 to 19.8 g/dL in Peruvian Andes residents) → total O₂ content recovers.
- **Trade-offs of polycythemia:** increased blood viscosity → pulmonary hypertension → right ventricular hypertrophy → cor pulmonale (chronic mountain sickness). Tibetans have a genetic adaptation: lower hemoglobin, more nitric oxide production, lower pulmonary artery pressure than acclimatized lowlanders.
- **Acute mountain sickness (AMS):** headache, nausea, dizziness; cerebral vasodilation from low arterial P_O₂. Treat with NSAIDs, descent, or **acetazolamide** (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor; produces compensatory metabolic acidosis that counters respiratory alkalosis from hyperventilation).
- **High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE):** above ~9,000 ft over days; vasoconstriction-induced damage to pulmonary capillaries.
- **High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE):** above ~13,000 ft; cerebral capillary leak; potentially fatal.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "As soon as a person begins to exercise, breathing becomes deeper and more rapid to produce a total minute volume that is many times the resting value. This increased ventilation, particularly in well-trained athletes, is exquisitely matched to the simultaneous increase in oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production." (p. 569)
- > "These adjustments to high altitude—including increased ventilation, increased hemoglobin affinity for oxygen, increased total hemoglobin concentration, and decreased blood volume—are referred to as acclimatization." (p. 570)
- > "The 'ideal' hemoglobin concentration is probably close to 18 g/dl of blood. When values reach 21 to 23 g/dl, the circulation becomes abnormal and the person displays symptoms of chronic mountain sickness." (p. 573)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Ventilation / gas exchange / cellular respiration** — three components of "respiration."
- **External vs. internal respiration** — lung gas exchange vs. tissue gas exchange.
- **Alveolus** — gas-exchange air sac; ~100 μm; ~300 million per lung.
- **Type I / Type II alveolar cell** — thin gas-exchange cell / surfactant-secreting cell.
- **Conducting zone** — nose → terminal bronchioles; no gas exchange.
- **Respiratory zone** — respiratory bronchioles + alveoli; site of gas exchange.
- **Mucociliary escalator** — ciliated columnar epithelium moves mucus toward pharynx.
- **Pleural membranes / intrapleural space** — parietal and visceral; potential space with thin lubricating fluid.
- **Intrapulmonary pressure** — pressure inside alveoli; −1 / +1 cmH₂O.
- **Intrapleural pressure** — pressure between pleurae; always subatmospheric.
- **Transpulmonary pressure** — intrapulmonary minus intrapleural; keeps lung apposed to chest wall.
- **Boyle's law** — at constant T, $PV = k$.
- **Compliance** — $\Delta V/\Delta P$ of lung; decreased in fibrosis.
- **Elasticity** — recoil tendency; from elastin fibers.
- **Surface tension** — alveolar fluid film tendency to collapse alveolus; reduced by surfactant.
- **Surfactant** — type II cell phospholipid/protein mixture; reduces alveolar surface tension.
- **Law of Laplace** — $P = 2T/r$ for a sphere.
- **Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS)** — neonatal surfactant deficiency.
- **Tidal volume / vital capacity / FRC / residual volume** — standard spirometric quantities.
- **FEV₁** — forced expiratory volume in 1 second; ↓ in obstructive disease.
- **Restrictive vs. obstructive disorder** — ↓VC, normal FEV1/FVC vs. normal VC, ↓FEV1/FVC.
- **Asthma** — chronic inflammatory airway disease; IgE-mediated; reversible bronchoconstriction.
- **Emphysema** — alveolar destruction; air trapping; smoking-induced.
- **COPD** — emphysema + chronic obstructive bronchiolitis.
- **Dalton's law** — total pressure = sum of partial pressures.
- **Henry's law** — dissolved gas ∝ partial pressure.
- **Anatomical / physiological dead space** — conducting-zone volume / dead space + poorly perfused alveoli.
- **Pulse oximetry** — non-invasive % oxyhemoglobin saturation measurement.
- **V/Q (ventilation/perfusion) ratio** — match between alveolar ventilation and capillary blood flow.
- **Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** — pulmonary arterioles constrict in low alveolar O₂ (opposite of systemic).
- **Cor pulmonale** — right heart failure from pulmonary hypertension.
- **Hyperbaric oxygen therapy** — 100% O₂ at >1 atm; treats CO poisoning, decompression sickness, gas gangrene.
- **Pre-Bötzinger complex** — medullary inspiratory rhythm generator.
- **Central chemoreceptors** — retrotrapezoid nucleus; CSF pH sensing.
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors** — aortic and carotid bodies; ↓P_O₂, ↓pH, ↑P_CO₂ sensing.
- **Hering-Breuer reflex** — pulmonary stretch receptors inhibit further inspiration at large V_T.
- **Hemoglobin** — 2α + 2β + 4 heme; binds 4 O₂.
- **Oxyhemoglobin / deoxyhemoglobin / methemoglobin / carboxyhemoglobin** — Hb states.
- **% oxyhemoglobin saturation** — fraction of Hb bound to O₂; 97% arterial, 75% venous at rest.
- **Bohr effect** — ↓pH or ↑temperature → ↓Hb-O₂ affinity (rightward shift).
- **2,3-DPG** — RBC glycolytic byproduct; binds deoxyhemoglobin; rightward shift.
- **Fetal hemoglobin (HbF)** — 2α + 2γ; cannot bind 2,3-DPG; higher O₂ affinity than HbA.
- **Sickle-cell anemia** — β-globin Glu→Val; HbS polymerizes when deoxygenated.
- **Thalassemia** — α or β chain underproduction.
- **Myoglobin** — single-chain, single-heme; muscle O₂ storage; high affinity, rectangular curve.
- **Carbonic anhydrase** — RBC enzyme catalyzing $\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3}$.
- **Chloride shift / reverse chloride shift** — Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchange in tissues vs. lungs.
- **Carbaminohemoglobin** — CO₂ bound to Hb amines (~20% of CO₂ transport).
- **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation** — $\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log\frac{[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}]}{0.03 P_{CO_2}}$.
- **Volatile (carbonic) vs. nonvolatile (metabolic) acid** — eliminable via ventilation vs. requiring renal/buffer handling.
- **Respiratory vs. metabolic acidosis/alkalosis** — primary CO₂-driven vs. HCO₃⁻-driven disturbances.
- **Acclimatization** — multistep adaptation to high altitude (hyperventilation, 2,3-DPG, erythropoietin, polycythemia).
- **Erythropoietin** — kidney-derived hormone; stimulates bone marrow RBC production.
- **Acute mountain sickness (AMS)** — headache, nausea from low P_O₂; cerebral vasodilation.
- **Acetazolamide** — carbonic anhydrase inhibitor; metabolic acidosis used to treat AMS.
