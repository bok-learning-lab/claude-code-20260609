## Section 13.1 — Functions and Components of the Circulatory System (pp. 404–405)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Functions of the Circulatory System** (pp. 404–405)
- **Major Components of the Circulatory System** (p. 404)

### Core claims
- ~60,000 miles of vessels deliver materials to ~37 trillion cells. The blood transports O₂ (lungs → tissues), CO₂ (tissues → lungs), nutrients (GI → tissues, with liver as intermediate), wastes (tissues → kidneys), and hormones (endocrine glands → target organs). Blood also distributes heat: superficial vasodilation cools the body; superficial vasoconstriction conserves heat.
- Two protective functions: **clotting** (hemostasis when vessels are damaged) and **immune defense** (leukocytes and lymph nodes).
- The **circulatory system = cardiovascular + lymphatic.** The heart is a four-chambered double pump. Right side → **pulmonary circulation** (lungs); left side → **systemic circulation** (all other tissues). The two circuits are in series.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "An estimated 60,000 miles of vessels throughout the body of an adult ensure that continued sustenance reaches each of the trillions of living cells." (p. 404)
- > "The circulatory system consists of two subdivisions: the cardiovascular system and the lymphatic system." (p. 404)

### Core claims
- **Blood volume ~5 L** in an average adult. Centrifuged whole blood separates into ~55% **plasma** (top), buffy coat of leukocytes and platelets, and ~45% packed erythrocytes (the **hematocrit**).
- **Plasma:**
  - 92% water; ~7% **plasma proteins** (albumin ~60%, globulins, fibrinogen); ~1% solutes.
  - **Albumin** is the main contributor to plasma colloid osmotic pressure (water retention in capillaries — Ch 14).
  - **Globulins** include alpha, beta (transferrin, lipoproteins), and gamma (immunoglobulins/antibodies).
  - **Fibrinogen** is the soluble precursor of fibrin; **serum** = plasma minus fibrinogen.
- **Erythrocytes (RBCs)** are biconcave, non-nucleated discs ~4–6 million/mm³ that carry ~280 million **hemoglobin** molecules each. Lifespan ~120 days; destroyed in spleen and liver macrophages; iron recycled via **transferrin**. RBCs have no mitochondria → respire anaerobically (Ch 5).
- **Leukocytes (WBCs)** ~5,000–10,000/mm³ have nuclei and mitochondria. They emigrate from capillaries by **diapedesis (extravasation)**. Granulocytes:
  - **Neutrophils** (54–62%): phagocytic; nuclei with 2–5 lobes; immature "band cells" with sausage-shaped nuclei.
  - **Eosinophils** (1–3%): red-staining granules; antiparasitic; help dissolve clots.
  - **Basophils** (<1%): blue-staining granules; release **heparin** (anticoagulant) and **histamine** (inflammation).
- Agranulocytes:
  - **Lymphocytes** (25–33%): small cells with round nuclei; specific immunity (B → antibodies, T → cell-mediated; Ch 15).
  - **Monocytes** (3–9%): largest leukocyte; differentiate into **macrophages** in tissues.
- **Platelets (thrombocytes)** 130,000–400,000/mm³ are cell fragments from **megakaryocytes** in bone marrow; lifespan 5–9 days; release **serotonin** (vasoconstrictor) and growth factors.
- **Hematopoiesis:** **hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)** in **myeloid (red bone marrow)** tissue (long bones, ribs, sternum, pelvis, vertebrae, skull) are self-renewing multipotent stem cells producing ~500 billion blood cells/day. **Lymphoid tissue** (lymph nodes, tonsils, spleen, thymus) produces lymphocytes.
- **Erythropoiesis (Fig 13.4):** hemocytoblast → proerythroblast → erythroblast → normoblast (expels nucleus) → reticulocyte → mature erythrocyte. **Erythropoietin (EPO)** from kidneys is the main regulator, secreted in response to tissue hypoxia (low altitude, anemia, lung disease). Recombinant EPO is used clinically and (illegally) for performance doping.
- **Thrombopoietin** from the liver stimulates megakaryocyte differentiation; thrombopoietin levels are regulated by a sponge mechanism (platelet receptors mop it up as platelet counts rise).
- **Iron homeostasis:** dietary iron is absorbed in the duodenum; **ferroportin** is the enterocyte iron exporter; **transferrin** carries iron in blood. **Hepcidin** (liver hormone) inhibits ferroportin → lowers plasma iron. Daily requirement: ~10 mg (men), ~15 mg (menstruating women), ~30 mg (pregnant women). The body has no excretory pathway for iron — losses occur via intestinal cell shedding and menstruation.
- **Anemias:** iron-deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency (pernicious anemia → intrinsic factor failure or Crohn's disease), aplastic anemia (bone marrow damage, e.g., chemotherapy). Sickle-cell anemia (Ch 16) is a hemoglobin variant.
- **ABO blood typing:**
  - Type A (genotype IᴬIᴬ or IᴬI⁰): A antigen on RBC; anti-B antibodies.
  - Type B (Iᴮ Iᴮ or Iᴮ I⁰): B antigen; anti-A.
  - Type AB (Iᴬ Iᴮ): both antigens; no anti-A or anti-B (universal recipient).
  - Type O (I⁰ I⁰): no antigens; both anti-A and anti-B (universal donor of RBCs).
- **Crossmatch:** mix recipient serum + donor RBCs; agglutination indicates mismatch. Transfusion errors → hemolysis → kidney damage.
- **Rh factor (D antigen):** ~85% of Caucasians are Rh⁺. Risk in pregnancy: Rh⁻ mother carrying Rh⁺ fetus is sensitized at birth; subsequent pregnancies → maternal anti-Rh IgG crosses placenta → fetal hemolysis (**erythroblastosis fetalis / hemolytic disease of the newborn**). Prevented by **RhoGAM** (anti-Rh antibody) injection within 72 h of each Rh⁺ delivery.
- **Hemostasis (three overlapping mechanisms):**
  1. **Vasoconstriction** of the injured vessel.
  2. **Platelet plug** formation: damaged endothelium exposes subendothelial collagen; platelets bind via glycoprotein receptors to **von Willebrand's factor (VWF)** which bridges them to collagen. Platelet release reaction: ADP + serotonin + **thromboxane A₂ (TxA₂)** → recruit more platelets.
  3. **Fibrin web** stabilizes the plug.
- **Intact-endothelium platelet protection:** endothelial **prostacyclin (PGI₂)** and **nitric oxide (NO)** act as vasodilators and platelet-aggregation inhibitors; endothelial **CD39** breaks down circulating ADP.
- **Clotting cascade (Fig 13.9):**
  - **Extrinsic pathway** (in vivo initiator): vessel injury exposes **tissue factor (factor III)** → activates factor VII → factor VIIa-TF complex activates factor X (and factor IX). Tissue factor amplifies factor VIIa's ability to activate X by **a factor of 2 million**.
  - **Intrinsic pathway** (amplification): contact with collagen, glass, polyphosphates, or NETs activates factor XII → XI → IX. The intrinsic VIII complex (factor VIII + IXa + Ca²⁺ + phospholipids) activates factor X.
  - **Common pathway**: factor X activated → V complex (Xa + Va + Ca²⁺ + phospholipids) → **prothrombin (II) → thrombin (IIa)** → **fibrinogen (I) → fibrin monomers** → factor XIII cross-links → **fibrin polymer**.
- **Required cofactors:** Ca²⁺ (factor IV) and phospholipids (from activated platelet phosphatidylserine surfaces). **Vitamin K** is required for hepatic γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX, X (and proteins C and S).
- **Inherited bleeding disorders (Table 13.5):**
  - **Hemophilia A** (factor VIII defect): X-linked recessive; "royal disease."
  - **Hemophilia B / Christmas disease** (factor IX defect): X-linked recessive.
  - **Von Willebrand's disease**: autosomal recessive defect in VWF; most common bleeding disorder.
- **Clot dissolution:** active factor XII → kallikrein → **plasminogen → plasmin** → digests fibrin into split products. **Thrombolytic drugs** (tissue plasminogen activator/t-PA/alteplase; r-PA/reteplase; streptokinase; urokinase) accelerate this pathway for stroke, MI, DVT, and pulmonary embolism.
- **Anticoagulants:**
  - **Aspirin** (irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase → blocks TxA₂).
  - **Clopidogrel (Plavix)** (ADP receptor antagonist).
  - **Heparin** (activates antithrombin III).
  - **Warfarin (Coumadin)** (inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase).
  - **Rivaroxaban (Xarelto)** (direct factor Xa inhibitor — given to atrial fibrillation patients).
  - **Citrate / EDTA** (chelate Ca²⁺ — in vitro only).
  - **GP IIb/IIIa monoclonals** (block platelet-collagen/VWF binding).

### Figures

#### Figure 13.9 — The clotting pathways *(viewed)*

A three-region Venn diagram in two large lobes (blue = extrinsic pathway on the left; yellow = intrinsic pathway on the right) joined by a central green region (common pathway). **Extrinsic pathway (left, blue lobe):** activator **tissue factor** activates **VII → VII activated**, which (with tissue factor, calcium, and phospholipids forming the VII complex) feeds into the common pathway by activating factor X. **Intrinsic pathway (right, yellow lobe):** activators (collagen, glass, polyphosphates, NETs) trigger **XII → XII activated**, which activates **XI → XI activated**, which activates **IX → IX activated**. Factor IXa with factor VIII, calcium, and phospholipids forms the **VIII complex**, which feeds into the common pathway by activating factor X. **Common pathway (central green region):** Factor X is activated, and X activated with V activated, calcium, and phospholipids forms the **V complex**, which converts **prothrombin → thrombin**. Thrombin converts **fibrinogen → fibrin**, which is cross-linked by **factor XIII** into a **fibrin polymer** (rightward arrow leaving the diagram).

> Vision note: Canonical clotting cascade architecture. Anchor for any clotting-factor or anticoagulant question. The convergence at factor X explains why rivaroxaban (a factor Xa inhibitor) and warfarin (which depletes vitamin-K-dependent factors II, VII, IX, X) both work despite acting on different parts of the cascade. The diagram makes clear that tissue factor (extrinsic) initiates clotting in vivo while the intrinsic pathway provides amplification through the VIII complex.

## Section 13.3 — Structure of the Heart (pp. 417–421)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Pulmonary and Systemic Circulations** (pp. 417–418)
- **Atrioventricular and Semilunar Valves** (pp. 418–419)
- **Heart Sounds** (pp. 419–421)

### Core claims
- **Four chambers:** right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle. Atria receive blood from veins; ventricles pump blood into arteries.
- **Pulmonary circulation:** right atrium ← superior/inferior vena cava ← systemic veins; **tricuspid (right AV) valve** → right ventricle → **pulmonary semilunar valve** → pulmonary trunk → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium.
- **Systemic circulation:** left atrium → **bicuspid (mitral / left AV) valve** → left ventricle → **aortic semilunar valve** → aorta → tissues → vena cava → right atrium.
- The **fibrous skeleton** of the heart electrically and mechanically separates atrial and ventricular myocardia.
- **AV valves** (tricuspid; mitral/bicuspid) are anchored by **chordae tendineae** to **papillary muscles** that prevent valve inversion during ventricular systole.
- **Semilunar valves** (pulmonary, aortic) are cup-shaped, pressed open by ejection and snapped shut by back-pressure during diastole.
- **Heart sounds (S1 "lub" and S2 "dub"):** S1 = AV-valve closure at the start of systole; S2 = semilunar-valve closure at the start of diastole.
- **Stethoscope sites** (Fig 13.13): aortic area (right 2nd intercostal space), pulmonic area (left 2nd ICS), tricuspid (left 5th ICS at sternum), mitral (left 5th ICS, midclavicular line — apex).
- **Murmurs** arise from valvular **regurgitation** (incompetent valve) or **stenosis** (narrowed valve), most often from rheumatic fever damage. **Mitral valve prolapse** is a common cause of systolic regurgitation murmur.
- **Septal defects** (atrial septal defect / ventricular septal defect / patent foramen ovale) usually shunt blood from left to right (higher-pressure side) → potential pulmonary hypertension.
- **Fetal shunts:** the **foramen ovale** (between atria) and **ductus arteriosus** (between pulmonary trunk and aorta) bypass the non-functional fetal lungs. After birth, lung inflation lowers pulmonary resistance → foramen ovale closes; rising arterial O₂ closes the ductus arteriosus.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The right side of the heart pumps the blood through the lungs in the pulmonary circulation. The left side… pumps the blood to the rest of the body in the systemic circulation." (p. 418)
- > "Both AV valves are similarly anchored to the walls of their ventricles by strong tendinous cords, the chordae tendineae, which arise from finger-like papillary muscles." (p. 418)
- > "The first heart sound (S1, or lub) is produced immediately after the QRS wave." (p. 429)
- > "Many people have small defects that produce detectable murmurs but do not seriously compromise the pumping ability of the heart." (p. 420)

## Section 13.4 — Cardiac Cycle (pp. 421–423)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Pressure Changes During the Cardiac Cycle** (pp. 422–423)

### Core claims
- **Systole** = ventricular contraction; **diastole** = ventricular relaxation. At a resting cardiac rate of 75 bpm, one cycle is 0.8 s (diastole 0.5 s; systole 0.3 s).
- The ventricles fill to about 80% of their end-diastolic volume during early diastole (passive filling). **Atrial systole** adds the final 20% just before ventricular systole.
- **End-diastolic volume (EDV)** ≈ 130 mL.
- **Stroke volume (SV)** ≈ 80 mL = volume ejected per beat.
- **End-systolic volume (ESV)** ≈ 50 mL.
- **Ejection fraction (EF) = SV / EDV ≈ 2/3 (~60–65%)**.
- **Five phases (Fig 13.17):**
  1. **Isovolumetric contraction:** ventricles begin to contract; intraventricular pressure rises; AV valves snap shut (S1); semilunar valves still closed; no volume change.
  2. **Ejection:** ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure → semilunar valves open; blood ejected; LV peaks at ~120 mmHg.
  3. **Isovolumetric relaxation:** ventricles begin to relax; back-pressure snaps semilunar valves shut (S2 — dicrotic notch on aortic trace); AV valves still closed; no volume change. Aortic pressure falls to ~80 mmHg; LV pressure falls to ~0 mmHg.
  4. **Rapid filling:** when LV pressure drops below LA pressure, AV valves open; ventricles fill passively.
  5. **Atrial contraction:** atrial systole delivers the final 20% of EDV.
- **Right side pressures** are much lower: RV ~25 mmHg systolic / ~8 mmHg diastolic; pulmonary artery ~25/15 mmHg.
- **Aortic dicrotic notch** is a brief inflection in the aortic pressure trace produced by closure of the aortic valve at S2.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The ventricles are about 80% filled with blood even before the atria contract. Contraction of the atria adds the final 20% to the end-diastolic volume." (p. 422)
- > "Contraction of the ventricles in systole ejects about two-thirds (the ejection fraction) of the blood they contain." (p. 422)
- > "When the pressure in the left ventricle becomes greater than the pressure in the aorta, the phase of ejection begins as the semilunar valves open. The pressure in the left ventricle and aorta rises to about 120 mmHg." (p. 422)
- > "Similar events occur in the right ventricle and pulmonary circulation, but the pressures are lower. The maximum pressure produced at systole in the right ventricle is 25 mmHg, which falls to a low of 8 mmHg at diastole." (p. 423)
- > "Closing of these [semilunar] valves produces the second heart sound and the dicrotic notch during the phase of isovolumetric relaxation." (p. 423)

### Equations / quantitative

**Cardiac cycle key values:**
$$\mathrm{EDV} \approx 130\ \mathrm{mL};\quad \mathrm{ESV} \approx 50\ \mathrm{mL};\quad \mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{EDV} - \mathrm{ESV} \approx 80\ \mathrm{mL}$$
$$\mathrm{EF} = \frac{\mathrm{SV}}{\mathrm{EDV}} \approx \frac{2}{3}\quad (\approx 60{-}65\%)$$
$$\mathrm{LV\ pressure:\ ~120/0\ mmHg};\quad \mathrm{Aortic\ pressure:\ ~120/80\ mmHg};\quad \mathrm{RV:\ ~25/8\ mmHg}$$

## Section 13.5 — Electrical Activity of the Heart and the Electrocardiogram (pp. 424–430)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Electrical Activity of the Heart** (pp. 424–427)
  - **Pacemaker Potential** (pp. 424–425)
  - **Myocardial Action Potential** (pp. 425–426)
  - **Conducting Tissues of the Heart** (p. 426)
  - **Conduction of the Impulse** (p. 426)
  - **Excitation-Contraction Coupling in Heart Muscle** (pp. 426–427)
- **The Electrocardiogram** (pp. 427–430)
  - **Correlation of the ECG with Heart Sounds** (p. 429)

### Core claims
- The heart's **automaticity** is intrinsic: an isolated heart continues to beat. Three regions can act as pacemakers — the **SA node** (normal), **AV node**, and **Purkinje fibers** (latent secondaries; their slower rates are normally suppressed by the SA node's faster rhythm).
- **SA node pacemaker potential (Fig 13.18):** instead of a flat resting potential, SA cells show a slow diastolic depolarization driven by:
  - **HCN channels** ("funny current," cyclic-nucleotide-gated; open in response to hyperpolarization, conduct Na⁺ inward; cAMP-sensitive — sympathetic stimulation accelerates them).
  - **Clocklike Ca²⁺ leak** into the cytoplasm.
  - At threshold (~−40 mV), **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels** open → upstroke (this is NOT a Na⁺ upstroke as in nerve/skeletal muscle).
  - **Voltage-gated K⁺ channels** mediate repolarization.
- **Autonomic modulation:**
  - **Sympathetic (norepinephrine, epinephrine) → β-adrenergic → ↑cAMP → opens HCN channels → faster pacemaker** (chronotropy ↑). Also opens L-type Ca²⁺ channels for stronger contraction (inotropy ↑).
  - **Parasympathetic (vagus → ACh) → muscarinic receptors → opens K⁺ channels → hyperpolarizes pacemaker → slower rate.**
- **Myocardial (working) cell action potential (Fig 13.19):**
  - Resting Vm ~−85 mV.
  - **Phase 0 — Rapid upstroke:** voltage-gated **fast Na⁺** channels open; Vm reverses to ~+15 mV.
  - **Phase 1 — Initial rapid repolarization** (brief).
  - **Phase 2 — Plateau (~200–300 ms):** slow inward **Ca²⁺** balances outward **K⁺**. This long plateau is the cardiac signature.
  - **Phase 3 — Repolarization:** voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; rapid outward K⁺.
  - **Phase 4 — Resting** (steady at −85 mV for non-pacemaker cells).
- The long action potential (~300 ms ≈ contraction duration) produces a **long refractory period** that prevents tetanus — critical for the pumping function.
- **Conduction pathway (Fig 13.20):**
  - SA node (right atrium, near SVC opening) → atrial myocardium via gap junctions (0.8–1.0 m/s).
  - **AV node** (inferior interatrial septum) — slow conduction (0.03–0.05 m/s) creates the **AV delay** that allows atrial systole to complete before ventricular systole begins.
  - **Bundle of His (AV bundle)** — pierces fibrous skeleton.
  - **Right and left bundle branches** along the interventricular septum.
  - **Purkinje fibers** through the ventricular myocardium (5 m/s) — spread depolarization from endocardium to epicardium.
- **Cardiac E-C coupling:** Ca²⁺ enters T-tubule via voltage-gated channels → triggers SR Ca²⁺ release via ryanodine receptors (**CICR**, Ch 12) → Ca²⁺ binds troponin → contraction. Relaxation: **SERCA** pumps Ca²⁺ back into SR; **Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX)** and plasma membrane **Ca²⁺-ATPase** extrude Ca²⁺. ~20,000 "signaling complexes" per myocardial cell synchronize the contraction.
- **Digitalis (digoxin)** inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase → ↑intracellular Na⁺ → ↓NCX outward Na⁺/Ca²⁺ flux → ↑intracellular Ca²⁺ → ↑contractility (positive inotrope). It also slows AV node conduction, useful in atrial fibrillation.
- **ECG (Figs 13.22, 13.23):**
  - **P wave** = atrial depolarization (atria contract shortly after).
  - **PR interval** = onset of atrial depolarization to onset of ventricular depolarization (normally 0.12–0.20 s). Includes AV node delay.
  - **QRS complex** = ventricular depolarization (atrial repolarization is masked underneath).
  - **ST segment** = corresponds to the plateau phase of the ventricular AP; flat at baseline.
  - **T wave** = ventricular repolarization. (Same direction as QRS because repolarization spreads in the reverse direction — epicardium → endocardium.)
- The ECG measures voltage differences between body surface electrodes; it is NOT a direct recording of action potentials.
- **12-lead ECG (Table 13.7):**
  - **Bipolar limb leads I, II, III** (Einthoven's triangle): RA–LA, RA–LL, LA–LL.
  - **Augmented unipolar limb leads aVR, aVL, aVF** at right arm, left arm, left leg.
  - **Precordial (chest) leads V1–V6** across the anterior and lateral chest.
- **ECG correlation with cycle (Fig 13.25):** QRS → ventricular depolarization → onset of systole → AV valves close → S1. T → ventricular repolarization → onset of diastole → semilunar valves close → S2.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "During the period of diastole, the SA node exhibits a slow spontaneous depolarization called the **pacemaker potential**." (p. 424)
- > "It is the influx of Ca²⁺ at this time — rather than the more usual inflow of Na⁺ — that produces the upward phase of the action potential in the pacemaker cells." (p. 424)
- > "This plateau phase results from a slow inward diffusion of Ca²⁺ through slow Ca²⁺ channels, which balances a slow outward diffusion of K⁺." (p. 425)
- > "The cardiac action potential, as a result, is about 100 times longer in duration than the spikelike action potential in an axon." (p. 425)
- > "Slow conduction of impulses (0.03 to 0.05 m/sec) through the AV node accounts for over half of the time delay between excitation of the atria and ventricles." (p. 426)
- > "Summation and tetanus, as can occur in skeletal muscles, is prevented from occurring in the myocardium by this long refractory period." (p. 426)
- > "The spread of atrial depolarization thereby creates the P wave." (p. 427)
- > "The plateau phase of the cardiac action potential is correlated with the S-T segment of the ECG." (p. 427)
- > "The repolarization of the ventricles, as indicated by the T wave, occurs at the same time that the ventricles relax at the beginning of diastole." (p. 429)

### Equations / quantitative
**Conduction velocities:**
$$\text{Atrial myocardium: } 0.8{-}1.0\ \mathrm{m/s};\quad \text{AV node: } 0.03{-}0.05\ \mathrm{m/s};\quad \text{Purkinje: } \approx 5\ \mathrm{m/s}$$

**Cardiac action potential duration vs. skeletal AP:**
$$\tau_{cardiac\ AP} \approx 200{-}300\ \mathrm{ms};\quad \tau_{skeletal\ AP} \approx 2{-}5\ \mathrm{ms}\quad (\approx 100{\times}\ \text{longer})$$

**Normal PR interval:** 0.12–0.20 s.

## Section 13.6 — Blood Vessels (pp. 430–434)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Arteries** (pp. 430–432)
- **Capillaries** (pp. 432–434)
  - **Types of Capillaries** (pp. 432–434)
- **Veins** (pp. 434–435)

### Core claims
- **Three tunics:**
  - **Tunica externa** (outer connective tissue, vasa vasorum).
  - **Tunica media** (smooth muscle and elastic fibers).
  - **Tunica interna** (endothelium + basement membrane + internal elastic lamina).
- **Elastic (conducting) arteries** (aorta, large branches): many elastin layers; stretch during systole and recoil during diastole to maintain pressure between beats.
- **Muscular (distributing) arteries** and **arterioles**: thick smooth-muscle layer; **resistance vessels**. Arterioles 20–30 μm diameter; small muscular arteries 100 μm. Constriction of arterioles is the dominant determinant of TPR (Ch 14).
- **Aneurysm:** balloon-like swelling, often in aorta (thoracic or abdominal); risk factors include hypertension, atherosclerosis, diabetes.
- **Capillaries** 7–10 μm in diameter; >40 billion in body; >1,000 mi² total surface area. No tissue cell is more than 60–80 μm from a capillary. Wall is one endothelial cell layer on a basement membrane.
- **Three capillary types:**
  - **Continuous** (muscle, lung, adipose, CNS): tight junctions; pinocytotic vesicles transport substances; in CNS contribute to the blood-brain barrier.
  - **Fenestrated** (kidney, endocrine glands, intestines): 800–1,000 Å pores covered by mucoprotein; allow rapid bulk exchange.
  - **Discontinuous (sinusoidal)** (liver, spleen, bone marrow): huge intercellular gaps; allow whole cells (erythrocytes) to pass.
- **Pericytes** are contractile non-smooth-muscle cells along CNS capillaries; regulate cerebral blood flow and contribute to the blood-brain barrier.
- **Metarterioles** ± **precapillary sphincters** regulate flow into capillary beds; **arteriovenous anastomoses** shunt blood directly from arterioles to venules, bypassing capillaries (Fig 13.27).
- **Angiogenesis** (new vessels): driven by **vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)** and **fibroblast growth factor (FGF)** via tyrosine kinase receptors. Anti-VEGF antibodies — **bevacizumab (Avastin)** for cancers and **ranibizumab (Lucentis)** for wet macular degeneration — exploit this pathway.
- **Veins** = capacitance vessels. Hold most of total blood volume. Mean venous pressure ~2 mmHg (vs. mean arterial ~100 mmHg).
- **Skeletal muscle pump:** rhythmic compression of veins between contracting muscles; **one-way venous valves** prevent retrograde flow (William Harvey demonstration, 1628).
- **Respiratory pump:** diaphragm contraction → ↑abdominal pressure + ↓thoracic pressure → drives venous return into thoracic vena cava.
- **Varicose veins:** failure of venous valves after chronic distension. **Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)** can dislodge as a venous thromboembolism → pulmonary embolism.

- > "The thick muscle layer of the arteries allows them to transport blood ejected from the heart under high pressure." (p. 430)
- > "Because arterioles and small muscular arteries have narrow lumina, they provide the greatest resistance to blood flow through the arterial system." (p. 431)
- > "The walls of capillaries are composed of just one cell layer — a simple squamous epithelium, or endothelium." (p. 432)
- > "Scarcely any cell in the body is more than 60 to 80 μm away from a blood capillary." (p. 432)
- > "Fenestrated capillaries occur in the kidneys, endocrine glands, and intestines." (p. 433)
- > "Discontinuous capillaries are found in the bone marrow, liver, and spleen." (p. 433)
- > "Veins are able to expand as they accumulate additional amounts of blood. The average pressure in the veins is only 2 mmHg, compared to a much higher average arterial pressure of about 100 mmHg." (p. 434)

## Section 13.7 — Atherosclerosis and Cardiac Arrhythmias (pp. 435–441)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Atherosclerosis** (pp. 435–438)
  - **Cholesterol and Plasma Lipoproteins** (pp. 436–437)
  - **Inflammation and Atherosclerosis** (p. 437)
- **Ischemic Heart Disease** (pp. 438–439)
- **Arrhythmias Detected by the Electrocardiograph** (pp. 439–441)
  - **Flutter and Fibrillation** (p. 440)
  - **AV Node Block** (pp. 440–441)

### Core claims
- **Atherosclerosis** is the most common form of arteriosclerosis ("hardening of arteries") and the principal cause of heart attack and stroke; ~31% of deaths in the US, Europe, Japan.
- **Pathogenesis:**
  1. **Endothelial damage** (smoking, hypertension, high LDL, diabetes).
  2. **Fatty streaks** appear: lipid-laden macrophages (foam cells) + lymphocytes in tunica interna.
  3. **Fibrous plaques:** connective tissue cap over a lipid core with macrophages, foam cells, and smooth muscle cells (which switch from contractile to "synthetic" phenotype).
  4. **Plaque rupture:** thinned collagen cap (weakened by macrophage collagenases) ruptures → exposes thrombogenic surface → **thrombus** forms → vessel occlusion.
- **Protective endothelial functions:** physical barrier; **nitric oxide** (vasodilator, anti-aggregant); **prostacyclin (PGI₂)**. Atherosclerosis-prone factors counter these (e.g., increased **endothelin-1** vasoconstrictor).
- **Cholesterol transport (Fig 13.32):**
  - **LDL** ("bad") delivers cholesterol from liver to peripheral tissues. **Apolipoprotein B** in LDL is internalized via receptor-mediated endocytosis.
  - **HDL** ("good") reverse-transports cholesterol from peripheral tissues (including foam cells in atheromas) back to liver.
  - **Familial hypercholesterolemia:** autosomal dominant; defective LDL receptor → high LDL; homozygotes have MIs in childhood.
  - **Statins** inhibit **HMG-CoA reductase** → ↓cholesterol synthesis → ↑LDL receptors on liver → ↓plasma LDL.
- **Oxidized LDL** is a major driver of the inflammatory cascade; **C-reactive protein** (CRP) is a stronger predictor of cardiovascular events than LDL cholesterol.
- **Ischemic heart disease:** atherosclerosis of coronary arteries → inadequate myocardial O₂. **Angina pectoris** = substernal pain (often referred to left shoulder/arm) from reversible myocardial ischemia. Treated with **nitroglycerin** (NO donor, vasodilator).
- **Myocardial infarction (MI, "heart attack"):** irreversible ischemic necrosis. Fibroblasts replace dead myocytes with collagen scar (non-contractile). **Reperfusion injury** can extend the infarct via apoptosis (Ca²⁺ overload, ROS).
- **ECG signatures:** **ST depression** = subendocardial ischemia; **ST elevation** = transmural ischemia (acute MI). **Cardiac troponin I or T** is the diagnostic blood marker (Ch 12).
- **Cerebrovascular accident (stroke):**
  - **Ischemic stroke** (most common): thrombotic occlusion of a cerebral artery. **Excitotoxicity** (Ch 7) drives delayed neuronal death even after reperfusion. Best treated with thrombolytics/anticoagulants within a few hours.
  - **Hemorrhagic stroke**: bleeding from a cerebral artery, often from an aneurysm.
- **Arrhythmias:**
  - **Bradycardia** (<60 bpm) — normal in trained athletes (high vagal tone); pathological when from AV block or sinus node dysfunction.
  - **Tachycardia** (>100 bpm) — normal during sympathetic activation; pathological when sustained at rest.
  - **Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (SVT):** ectopic atrial pacemaker fires rapidly; treated with IV adenosine.
  - **Ventricular tachycardia:** ectopic ventricular pacemaker; dangerous, can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation.
  - **Atrial flutter** (200–300/min, coordinated) → frequently degenerates to **atrial fibrillation** (~600/min, uncoordinated). AF reduces CO by ~15%; major risk is thromboembolic stroke. Treated with antiarrhythmics, anticoagulants (warfarin or rivaroxaban), or pulmonary vein **catheter ablation**.
  - **Ventricular fibrillation:** lethal within minutes; treated by **electrical defibrillation** (depolarizes all myocardium simultaneously to reset rhythm).
- **Fibrillation mechanisms:** circus rhythms / reentry around scars or refractory islands; focal excitation by ischemic ectopic foci.
- **Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD):** subcutaneous pulse generator with intracardiac coil; detects and shocks ventricular fibrillation.
- **AV node block (Fig 13.35):**
  - **First-degree:** PR interval > 0.20 s; every P followed by a QRS but with delay.
  - **Second-degree:** some Ps fail to conduct (e.g., 2:1 or 3:1).
  - **Third-degree (complete):** atria and ventricles dissociate; an ectopic Purkinje pacemaker drives ventricles at 20–40 bpm. Requires artificial pacemaker.
- **Antiarrhythmic drug classes:**
  - Class I — Na⁺ channel blockers (quinidine, procainamide, lidocaine).
  - Class II — β-blockers (propranolol, atenolol).
  - Class III — K⁺ channel blockers (amiodarone).
  - Class IV — Ca²⁺ channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The first anatomically recognized change is the appearance of fatty streaks." (p. 435)
- > "Cholesterol is carried to the arteries by plasma proteins called low-density lipoproteins (LDLs)." (p. 437)
- > "Statins also have other beneficial effects: they slightly increase the HDL level, and they reduce inflammation, which promotes atherosclerosis." (p. 437)
- > "Atherosclerosis is now believed to be an inflammatory disease to a significant degree. … measurement of blood C-reactive protein… is actually a stronger predictor of atherosclerotic heart disease than the blood LDL cholesterol level." (p. 437)
- > "An obstruction in a coronary artery, for example, may allow sufficient coronary blood flow at rest but not when the heart is stressed by exercise or emotional conditions." (p. 438)
- > "If ischemia and anaerobic metabolism are prolonged, necrosis (cellular death) may occur. … A sudden, irreversible injury of this kind is called a myocardial infarction." (p. 438)
- > "Currently, the diagnosis of an MI is based mainly on rising blood troponin levels, primarily troponin I." (p. 438)
- > "Ventricular tachycardia… is very dangerous because it can quickly degenerate into a lethal condition known as ventricular fibrillation." (p. 440)
- > "It has been estimated that 20% to 25% of all strokes may result from thrombi promoted by atrial fibrillation." (p. 440)

## Section 13.8 — Lymphatic System (pp. 441–443)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
(no further subsections within § 13.8 in the source.)

### Core claims
- The interstitial space contains **interstitial fluid** (filtered from plasma) and the **extracellular matrix** (collagen fibers + glycosaminoglycan gel).
- **Three functions of the lymphatic system:**
  1. Return interstitial fluid (≈ 3 L/day net filtration in excess of capillary reabsorption — Ch 14) to the blood.
  2. Transport absorbed dietary fat (chylomicrons) from intestinal lacteals to blood (Ch 18).
  3. Provide immune surveillance through lymphocytes and lymphoid organs.
- **Lymphatic capillaries** are blind-ended, highly permeable tubes that admit interstitial fluid, proteins, extravasated WBCs, microorganisms, and fat. Once inside, the fluid is called **lymph**.
- Lymphatic vessels have three tunics and one-way valves, and contain smooth muscle with **pacemaker** activity (stretch-activated peristaltic waves drive lymph flow).
- **Drainage:**
  - **Right lymphatic duct** → right subclavian vein (drains right upper quadrant).
  - **Thoracic duct** (originating at the cisterna chyli) → left subclavian vein (drains the rest of the body).
- **Lymph nodes** are bean-shaped filters along lymphatic pathways, packed with phagocytic cells and germinal centers that proliferate lymphocytes.
- **Lymphoid organs:** tonsils, thymus, spleen — all produce lymphocytes (Ch 15).
- **Lymphedema:** failure of lymphatic drainage (e.g., post-mastectomy with axillary node dissection or radiation; **filariasis** elephantiasis) → protein-rich interstitial fluid accumulation → swelling, inflammation, fibrosis.
- **Metastasis:** cancer cells (notably breast, colon, prostate) enter porous lymphatic capillaries → seed regional lymph nodes → spread distally. The "sentinel lymph node" technique exploits this pattern.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The lymphatic system has three basic functions: (1) it transports interstitial (tissue) fluid… back to the blood; (2) it transports absorbed fat from the small intestine to the blood; and (3) its cells — called lymphocytes — help provide immunological defenses against disease-causing agents." (pp. 441–442)
- > "Because the walls of lymphatic capillaries are composed of endothelial cells with porous junctions, interstitial fluid, proteins, extravasated white blood cells, microorganisms, and absorbed fat (in the intestine) can easily enter." (p. 442)
- > "The lymph ducts eventually empty into one of two principal vessels: the thoracic duct or the right lymphatic duct. These ducts drain the lymph into the left and right subclavian veins, respectively." (p. 442)
- > "Metastasis to regional lymph nodes is the first step in the dissemination of tumors for cancers of the breast, colon, prostate, and others." (p. 443)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Plasma / serum** — fluid portion of blood / plasma minus fibrinogen.
- **Hematocrit** — fraction of blood volume occupied by RBCs (~45%).
- **Hemoglobin (Hb)** — O₂-carrying iron-porphyrin protein in RBCs.
- **Albumin / globulin / fibrinogen** — major plasma protein classes; albumin is the main contributor to colloid osmotic pressure.
- **Erythrocyte / leukocyte / platelet** — RBC / WBC / thrombocyte.
- **Diapedesis / extravasation** — WBC migration through capillary wall to tissues.
- **Neutrophil / eosinophil / basophil / lymphocyte / monocyte** — five WBC types; first three are granular, last two agranular.
- **Megakaryocyte** — bone marrow giant cell that produces platelets.
- **Hematopoiesis / erythropoiesis / leukopoiesis** — blood-cell production / RBC production / WBC production.
- **Hematopoietic stem cell (HSC)** — multipotent self-renewing bone marrow stem cell.
- **Erythropoietin (EPO)** — kidney-derived hormone driving RBC production in response to hypoxia.
- **Thrombopoietin** — liver-derived hormone driving platelet production.
- **Hepcidin / ferroportin / transferrin** — iron homeostasis regulator / iron exporter / iron transport protein.
- **Pernicious anemia** — B12 deficiency from loss of intrinsic factor.
- **Aplastic anemia** — bone-marrow failure (chemotherapy, toxins).
- **ABO blood group** — A / B / AB / O antigens on RBC; antibodies determined by tolerance.
- **Universal donor / universal recipient** — O− / AB+ (small-volume only).
- **Rh factor / RhoGAM** — D antigen / passive anti-D prophylaxis for Rh− mothers.
- **Erythroblastosis fetalis (HDFN)** — Rh hemolysis of the newborn.
- **Hemostasis** — arrest of bleeding (vasoconstriction → platelet plug → fibrin clot).
- **Von Willebrand factor (VWF)** — endothelial glycoprotein bridging platelets to subendothelial collagen.
- **Thromboxane A₂ (TxA₂) / serotonin / ADP** — platelet-released aggregation activators / vasoconstrictor / aggregation promoter.
- **Prostacyclin (PGI₂) / nitric oxide (NO) / CD39** — anti-aggregation defenses of intact endothelium.
- **Intrinsic / extrinsic / common clotting pathway** — collagen-triggered XII cascade / tissue-factor-triggered VII cascade / converging factor X → thrombin → fibrin pathway.
- **Tissue factor (factor III)** — extrinsic pathway initiator.
- **Prothrombin (II) / thrombin (IIa)** — common-pathway enzyme.
- **Fibrinogen (I) / fibrin / factor XIII** — clot precursor / clot mesh / cross-linker.
- **Plasmin / plasminogen / tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA)** — fibrinolytic enzyme / its zymogen / its activator (drug: alteplase).
- **Heparin / warfarin / aspirin / rivaroxaban** — antithrombin III activator / vitamin K antagonist / cyclooxygenase inhibitor / direct factor Xa inhibitor.
- **Hemophilia A / B** — factor VIII / factor IX deficiency (X-linked recessive).
- **Atrium / ventricle / interatrial / interventricular septum** — receiving chamber / pumping chamber / dividing walls.
- **Tricuspid (right AV) / bicuspid or mitral (left AV) / pulmonary semilunar / aortic semilunar** — the four heart valves.
- **Chordae tendineae / papillary muscles** — tendon-like cords / pulling muscles for AV valves.
- **S1 (lub) / S2 (dub)** — AV-valve closure / semilunar-valve closure heart sounds.
- **Murmur / regurgitation / stenosis / mitral valve prolapse** — turbulent flow sound / leakage / narrowing / common cause of systolic murmur.
- **Patent foramen ovale / patent ductus arteriosus** — fetal shunts persisting after birth.
- **Systole / diastole** — ventricular contraction / ventricular relaxation.
- **End-diastolic / end-systolic volume / stroke volume / ejection fraction** — EDV ~130 mL / ESV ~50 mL / SV ~80 mL / EF ~2/3.
- **Isovolumetric contraction / ejection / isovolumetric relaxation / rapid filling / atrial contraction** — the five cardiac cycle phases.
- **Dicrotic notch** — aortic-trace inflection from semilunar-valve closure.
- **SA node / AV node / bundle of His / bundle branches / Purkinje fibers** — the cardiac conduction pathway.
- **Pacemaker potential / HCN channels / funny current** — diastolic depolarization of SA node / hyperpolarization-cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels / their inward Na⁺ current.
- **L-type Ca²⁺ channel (DHP)** — voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel in T-tubules.
- **Plateau phase** — phase 2 of the cardiac AP; balanced Ca²⁺ in and K⁺ out; ~300 ms long.
- **Long refractory period** — prevents tetanus in the myocardium.
- **CICR (calcium-induced calcium release)** — extracellular Ca²⁺ entry triggers SR Ca²⁺ release.
- **SERCA / NCX** — SR Ca²⁺-ATPase / Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger; together restore low cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ during relaxation.
- **Digitalis / digoxin** — Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase inhibitor; positive inotrope, slows AV node conduction.
- **P wave / QRS complex / T wave** — atrial depolarization / ventricular depolarization / ventricular repolarization.
- **PR / ST / QT interval** — ECG timing markers.
- **12-lead ECG** — limb leads I/II/III, augmented aVR/aVL/aVF, precordial V1–V6.
- **Ectopic pacemaker** — abnormal pacemaker outside the SA node.
- **Tachycardia / bradycardia** — fast / slow heart rate.
- **Paroxysmal SVT** — supraventricular tachycardia; treated with adenosine.
- **Atrial / ventricular flutter / fibrillation** — fast coordinated / disorganized rhythms.
- **Circus rhythm / reentry** — recycled depolarization waves causing fibrillation.
- **Defibrillation** — synchronous depolarization to reset rhythm.
- **AV block (first / second / third degree)** — PR>0.20 s / dropped beats / complete dissociation.
- **Tunica externa / media / interna** — three vessel layers.
- **Elastic artery / muscular artery / arteriole / capillary / venule / vein** — vessel hierarchy.
- **Continuous / fenestrated / discontinuous capillary** — three capillary subtypes (CNS muscle / kidney glomerulus / liver sinusoid).
- **Pericyte** — contractile peri-capillary cell; modulates CNS blood flow and BBB.
- **Precapillary sphincter / arteriovenous anastomosis / metarteriole** — flow-regulating microvascular elements.
- **VEGF / FGF / bevacizumab / ranibizumab** — angiogenic growth factors / anti-VEGF antibodies.
- **Skeletal muscle pump / venous valves** — venous return mechanism.
- **Atherosclerosis / atheroma / foam cell / fibrous plaque / plaque rupture** — major pathological steps.
- **LDL / HDL / VLDL / apolipoprotein B / oxidized LDL** — lipoprotein classes and key apolipoprotein.
- **HMG-CoA reductase / statins** — cholesterol-synthesis rate-limiting enzyme / inhibitor drug class.
- **C-reactive protein (CRP)** — inflammation marker; strong CVD predictor.
- **Angina pectoris / myocardial infarction / reperfusion injury** — reversible ischemic chest pain / irreversible necrosis / apoptotic cell death on reperfusion.
- **ST depression / ST elevation** — subendocardial / transmural ischemia on ECG.
- **Cardiac troponin I / T** — gold-standard MI biomarker (Ch 12).
- **Stroke (CVA) — ischemic / hemorrhagic** — atherothrombotic / aneurysmal.
- **Excitotoxicity** — ischemia-driven glutamate accumulation killing neurons.
- **Lymph / lymphatic capillary / lymph node / lymphoid organ** — interstitial fluid in vessels / blind-ended drainage / filter / immune tissue.
- **Thoracic duct / right lymphatic duct** — major lymphatic drains → subclavian veins.
- **Lymphedema / metastasis / sentinel lymph node** — failed lymphatic drainage / cancer spread / first-draining node.
