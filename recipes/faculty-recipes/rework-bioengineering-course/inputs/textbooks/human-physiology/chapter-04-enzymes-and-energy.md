## Section 4.1 — Enzymes as Catalysts (pp. 87–90)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Mechanism of Enzyme Action** (pp. 87–88)
- **Naming of Enzymes** (pp. 89–90)

### Core claims
- **Enzymes** are biological catalysts — almost always proteins (some are RNA = **ribozymes**, e.g., the ribosome's peptidyl transferase). They increase reaction rate but are not consumed.
- For a reaction to proceed, reactants need **activation energy** to overcome the transition-state barrier. Enzymes lower the activation energy so a much larger fraction of reactant molecules can react at body temperature.
- **Lock-and-key model:** the enzyme's **active site** has a complementary shape to the substrate, like a lock to a key.
- **Induced-fit model** (refinement): substrate binding induces a small conformational change in the enzyme that strains the substrate's bonds and accelerates the reaction. Modern textbooks favor this view.
- The **enzyme-substrate complex** forms transiently; products leave; the free enzyme is regenerated unchanged.
- **Enzymes are specific** — usually one or a few related substrates.
- **Naming:** most enzymes end in **-ase**. Categories include:
  - **Hydrolases** — hydrolyze bonds (digestive enzymes).
  - **Phosphatases / kinases** — remove / add phosphate.
  - **Dehydrogenases** — remove H (oxidation).
  - **Synthetases / synthases** — build (often coupled to ATP hydrolysis).
  - **Isomerases** — rearrange.
  - **Polymerases** — polymerize nucleotides.
- **Isoenzymes** are structurally distinct enzymes catalyzing the same reaction; tissue-specific isoenzyme profiles support clinical diagnostics — see Table 4.1 (LDH, CK, AST, ALT).
- Clinically, leakage of intracellular enzymes into plasma signals tissue damage: **troponin I/T** for myocardial infarction (Ch 13); CK-MB historically; AST/ALT for hepatitis; amylase/lipase for pancreatitis.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "**Enzymes** are biological catalysts that increase the rate of chemical reactions." (p. 87)
- > "Catalysts make reactions go faster at lower temperatures by lowering the activation energy required." (p. 87)
- > "Each type of enzyme has a characteristic three-dimensional shape, or *conformation*, with ridges, grooves, and pockets lined with specific amino acids." (p. 87)
- > "The particular pockets that are active in catalyzing a reaction are called the **active sites** of the enzyme." (p. 87)
- > "This induced fit … weakens the existing bonds within the substrate molecules and allows them to be more easily broken." (p. 87)
- > "The only known exceptions are the few special cases in which RNA demonstrates enzymatic activity; in these cases they are called **ribozymes**." (p. 87)

## Section 4.2 — Control of Enzyme Activity (pp. 90–95)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Effects of Temperature and pH** (pp. 90–91)
- **Cofactors and Coenzymes** (pp. 91–92)
- **Substrate Concentration and Reversibility of Reactions** (p. 92)
- **Metabolic Pathways** (pp. 93–95)
  - **End-Product Inhibition** (pp. 93–94)
  - **Inborn Errors of Metabolism** (pp. 94–95)

### Core claims
- **Temperature:** reaction rate rises with T up to an optimum (~37–40 °C for human enzymes), then drops abruptly as the enzyme **denatures**. Beyond ~60 °C, most enzymes are irreversibly inactivated.
- **pH:** each enzyme has an optimum pH at which active-site residues are properly protonated.
  - **Pepsin** (stomach) — optimum pH ~2.
  - **Trypsin, chymotrypsin** (small intestine) — optimum pH ~8.
  - Most blood enzymes — optimum near pH 7.4. Hence the body defends blood pH tightly.
- **Cofactors** = nonprotein partners required for activity. Two flavors:
  - **Inorganic cofactors** — metal ions (Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Mn²⁺, Mo) that often coordinate substrate or stabilize charge.
  - **Coenzymes** — small organic molecules; often **vitamin-derived**. Examples: **NAD⁺** from niacin (B₃), **FAD** from riboflavin (B₂), **CoA** from pantothenic acid (B₅), **TPP** from thiamine (B₁), pyridoxal phosphate from B₆.
- **Substrate concentration:** at low [S], rate rises proportionally with [S]; at high [S], enzymes saturate and rate approaches **Vmax**. The hyperbolic shape is the basis of **Michaelis–Menten kinetics** (formally treated in advanced courses).
- **Reversibility:** thermodynamically every reaction is reversible; biologically, mass action drives net direction. Enzymes do *not* change the equilibrium constant — only the rate to reach it. The cell maintains net direction by continuously removing products or supplying substrate.
- **Metabolic pathways** are sequential enzyme-catalyzed reactions: A →ᴱ¹ B →ᴱ² C →ᴱ³ D. May be **linear, branched, or cyclic.**
- **End-product inhibition (allosteric feedback):** the final product of a pathway binds an allosteric site on the first committed enzyme, decreasing its activity (Fig 4.9). This is the prototypical metabolic control. In a branched pathway, end-product inhibition of one branch reroutes flux into the other (Fig 4.9).
- **Allosteric vs. competitive inhibitors:** allosteric inhibitors bind a regulatory site (away from the active site) and change enzyme shape; competitive inhibitors bind in the active site itself, mimicking substrate (their effect can be overcome by raising [S]).
- **Inborn errors of metabolism (Garrod, 1908):** defective enzymes from genetic mutations cause substrate accumulation and/or product deficiency. Examples (Table 4.4 and Fig 4.10–4.11):
  - **PKU (phenylketonuria)** — defective phenylalanine hydroxylase → phenylalanine accumulates and is shunted to phenylpyruvic acid → severe intellectual disability if untreated. Universal newborn screening + low-Phe diet.
  - **Albinism** — defective tyrosinase → no melanin from DOPA.
  - **Alkaptonuria** — homogentisate dioxygenase deficiency (not clinically severe).
  - **Tay-Sachs & GM1 gangliosidosis** — lysosomal hydrolase defects → ganglioside accumulation → neurodegeneration.
  - **Galactosemia** — galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase deficiency.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Each enzyme exhibits maximum activity at a characteristic temperature, called its **optimum temperature**." (p. 90)
- > "Each enzyme has characteristic pH values at which it functions optimally." (p. 91)
- > "Many enzymes require cofactors (ions or small molecules) in order to function." (p. 91)

- > "**Inborn errors of metabolism** are inherited diseases that result from a defective gene that produces a defective enzyme." (p. 94)

## Section 4.3 — Bioenergetics (pp. 95–103)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Endergonic and Exergonic Reactions** (pp. 96–97)
- **Coupled Reactions: ATP** (pp. 97–98)
- **Coupled Reactions: Oxidation-Reduction** (pp. 98–101)

### Core claims
- **Bioenergetics** = the study of energy flow in living systems. Cells obey the first and second laws of thermodynamics:
  - **First law:** energy is conserved.
  - **Second law:** entropy of an isolated system increases; cells maintain low entropy by importing usable energy from the environment.
- **Photosynthesis** (Fig 4.12) converts solar energy into chemical bonds: $6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} + \text{light} \to \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2}$.
- **Cell respiration** (Fig 4.13) reverses this stepwise, releasing free energy in a controlled cascade rather than as a single combustion: $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2} \to 6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O}$. Each step is a small, manageable drop on the "energy staircase."
- **Exergonic vs. endergonic:**
  - **Exergonic** — releases free energy (ΔG < 0); spontaneous (given activation energy).
  - **Endergonic** — requires free-energy input (ΔG > 0); not spontaneous.
- **Coupled reactions** (Fig 4.14): exergonic reactions drive endergonic ones. Energy transfer is not 100% efficient; some energy is always lost as heat (second law).
- **ATP — adenosine triphosphate (Fig 4.15)** — the universal energy currency. Structure: adenine + ribose (= adenosine) + three phosphates linked by two **high-energy phosphoanhydride bonds** (denoted by ~).
  - **ATP ⇌ ADP + Pᵢ** with ΔG° ≈ −7.3 kcal/mol (cell concentrations push it to ~−12 kcal/mol).
  - ATP is constantly recycled: a person at rest hydrolyzes roughly their body weight in ATP per day, but ATP stores remain small (~100 g).
  - Exergonic reactions (catabolism of glucose, fatty acids) regenerate ATP from ADP + Pᵢ; endergonic reactions (anabolism, muscle contraction, active transport, nerve impulse generation) hydrolyze ATP back to ADP.
- **Oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions:**
  - **Oxidation** = loss of electrons (often as H atoms).
  - **Reduction** = gain of electrons.
  - "OIL RIG" / "LEO the lion goes GER."
  - Oxidations are coupled to reductions: one molecule gives, another receives.
- **NAD⁺ / NADH** (from **niacin / B₃**) and **FAD / FADH₂** (from **riboflavin / B₂**) are the cell's two principal electron carriers. They shuttle electrons (as H atoms) from catabolism to the **mitochondrial electron transport chain** (Ch 5), where the energy of those electrons is used to make ATP.
  - **NAD⁺ + 2 H → NADH + H⁺** (Fig 4.17a). NAD takes one H and 2 e⁻; the other H⁺ stays in solution.
  - **FAD + 2 H → FADH₂** (Fig 4.17b). FAD takes both H atoms.
- **Pellagra** (Sandra's Clinical Investigation) is the deficiency disease of niacin: dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia, death — the "four Ds." Treatment: niacin / nicotinic acid supplementation. Riboflavin deficiency causes cheilitis and angular stomatitis.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Energy that is locked up in the chemical bonds of organic molecules can be converted by cell respiration into a usable form of energy in the cell." (p. 95)
- > "Reactions that release energy are called **exergonic** reactions, whereas reactions that require an input of energy are called **endergonic** reactions." (p. 96)
- > "The amount of usable energy obtained from coupled reactions is therefore always less than the energy released by the exergonic reactions, with the rest of the energy lost as heat." (p. 97)
- > "The energy released by most exergonic reactions in the cell is used, either directly or indirectly, to drive one particular endergonic reaction: the formation of **adenosine triphosphate (ATP)**." (p. 97)
- > "ATP is the universal energy carrier of the cell. High-energy bonds are indicated by a squiggle (~)." (p. 97)
- > "When the bond to the last phosphate is broken, energy is released and ATP is converted back into ADP and Pᵢ." (p. 97)
- > "A molecule is said to be **oxidized** when it loses electrons and **reduced** when it gains electrons." (p. 98)
- > "NAD and FAD are coenzymes that transfer pairs of hydrogen atoms from one molecule to another." (p. 99)

### Equations / quantitative
$$\mathrm{ATP} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{ADP} + \mathrm{P_i} \quad \Delta G^{\circ\prime} \approx -7.3\,\mathrm{kcal/mol}$$

$$\mathrm{NAD^+} + 2\,\mathrm{H} \to \mathrm{NADH} + \mathrm{H^+}$$
$$\mathrm{FAD} + 2\,\mathrm{H} \to \mathrm{FADH_2}$$

Photosynthesis / cell respiration overall (reverse of one another):
$$6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{respiration}]{\text{photosynthesis}} \mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2}$$

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Enzyme** — biological catalyst (almost always a protein).
- **Ribozyme** — catalytic RNA (e.g., the ribosome peptidyl transferase).
- **Activation energy** — energy barrier between reactants and products.
- **Active site** — substrate-binding catalytic pocket of an enzyme.
- **Lock-and-key / induced-fit** — original / refined model of substrate binding.
- **Enzyme-substrate complex** — transient enzyme–substrate intermediate.
- **Substrate** — the reactant on which an enzyme acts.
- **Product** — what the enzyme produces from the substrate.
- **Isoenzyme (isozyme)** — alternative form of the same enzyme; tissue-specific patterns useful in diagnosis.
- **Cofactor** — non-protein helper required for enzyme activity (metal ion or organic).
- **Coenzyme** — organic cofactor; often vitamin-derived (NAD, FAD, CoA, TPP, PLP).
- **Vitamin** — micronutrient cofactor precursor; water-soluble (B, C) or fat-soluble (A, D, E, K).
- **Niacin (B₃) / Riboflavin (B₂)** — precursors of NAD/NADP / FAD/FMN.
- **Vmax** — maximum reaction rate at substrate saturation.
- **Michaelis–Menten kinetics** — hyperbolic rate vs. [S] curve; characteristic of carriers and enzymes.
- **Denaturation** — loss of protein structure (heat, pH, organic solvents).
- **Optimum temperature / pH** — value at which enzyme activity peaks.
- **Pepsin / trypsin / amylase** — protease at gastric pH ~2 / protease at intestinal pH ~8 / starch-cleaving enzyme.
- **Metabolic pathway** — sequence of enzyme-catalyzed reactions.
- **End-product inhibition (allosteric feedback)** — final product of a pathway inhibits its own first committed enzyme.
- **Allosteric site / inhibitor / activator** — regulatory site away from active site.
- **Competitive inhibitor** — binds active site; reversed by ↑ substrate.
- **Inborn error of metabolism (IEM)** — genetic enzyme defect.
- **PKU (phenylketonuria) / albinism / alkaptonuria / Tay-Sachs / galactosemia** — representative IEMs.
- **Bioenergetics** — energy flow in living systems.
- **First / second law of thermodynamics** — energy conservation / entropy increase.
- **Exergonic / endergonic** — ΔG < 0 / ΔG > 0.
- **Coupled reaction** — exergonic reaction powers an endergonic reaction.
- **Entropy** — disorder; biological order requires continuous energy input.
- **ATP / ADP / Pᵢ** — adenosine tri- / di-phosphate / inorganic phosphate.
- **High-energy phosphoanhydride bond (~)** — bond hydrolyzed by ATPases; ~7.3 kcal/mol standard ΔG°.
- **Oxidation / reduction (redox)** — loss / gain of electrons (often as H atoms).
- **NAD⁺ / NADH** — electron carrier; one H + 2 e⁻ added, plus free H⁺.
- **FAD / FADH₂** — electron carrier; two H atoms added.
- **Pellagra** — niacin deficiency: dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia, death.
- **Photosynthesis / cell respiration** — solar → chemical / chemical → ATP + heat; reciprocal reactions.
