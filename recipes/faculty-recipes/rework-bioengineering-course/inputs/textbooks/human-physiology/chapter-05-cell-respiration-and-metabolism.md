## Section 5.1 — Glycolysis and the Lactate Pathway (pp. 105–108)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Glycolysis** (pp. 105–106)
- **Lactate Pathway (Anaerobic Respiration)** (pp. 106–108)

### Core claims
- **Metabolism** = the sum of all chemical reactions in the body. **Catabolism** breaks molecules down releasing energy; **anabolism** builds them up consuming energy.
- **Aerobic cell respiration** of one glucose proceeds in three stages: (1) **glycolysis** in the cytoplasm; (2) **citric acid (Krebs) cycle** in the mitochondrial matrix; (3) **electron transport / oxidative phosphorylation** on the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- **Glycolysis (Greek *glykys* = sweet, *lysis* = breakdown)** is a 9-step cytoplasmic pathway that **cleaves a 6-carbon glucose into two 3-carbon pyruvates**:
  - 2 ATP are *invested* in the early phosphorylation steps (steps 1, 3).
  - 4 ATP are *produced* by substrate-level phosphorylation (steps 6, 9 of each three-carbon branch).
  - 2 NADH are produced (step 5 of each branch).
  - **Net per glucose: 2 ATP, 2 NADH, 2 pyruvate.**
- Glycolysis does *not* require oxygen — it can run anaerobically.
- **Anaerobic / lactate pathway:** if O₂ is limiting (or if mitochondria are absent), pyruvate is reduced to **lactate** by **lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)**, regenerating NAD⁺ for continued glycolysis:
  $$\mathrm{Pyruvate} + \mathrm{NADH} + \mathrm{H^+} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Lactate} + \mathrm{NAD^+}$$
- Why lactate fermentation matters:
  - **Red blood cells** lack mitochondria — must respire anaerobically.
  - **Astrocytes** (Ch 7) preferentially run lactate fermentation and "export" lactate to neurons.
  - **Skeletal muscle** during intense exercise outruns its O₂ supply.
  - **Cardiac muscle** under ischemia switches to lactate; prolonged switch can kill myocardium → **myocardial infarction**. **Angina pectoris** is the chest pain of ischemic lactate production; treated with vasodilators (e.g., nitroglycerin).
- Lactate is *not* the same as **lactic acid**: at physiological pH, lactic acid (pKa ≈ 3.9) is fully dissociated to lactate⁻ + H⁺. The accumulating H⁺ causes the **acidosis** that limits prolonged anaerobic effort.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "All of the reactions in the body that involve energy transformation are collectively termed **metabolism**." (p. 105)
- > "The complete combustion of glucose requires the presence of oxygen and yields about 30 ATP for each molecule of glucose. However, some energy can be obtained in the absence of oxygen by the pathway that leads to the production of lactate." (p. 105)
- > "Glycolysis is the metabolic pathway by which glucose — a six-carbon (hexose) sugar — is converted into 2 molecules of pyruvate, or pyruvic acid." (p. 105)
- > "In glycolysis, 1 glucose is converted into 2 pyruvic acids, which immediately ionize to form pyruvate, in nine separate steps." (p. 105)
- > "Because 2 ATP were used at the beginning, however, the net gain is 2 ATP per glucose." (p. 105)
- > "Red blood cells can only metabolize anaerobically because they lack mitochondria." (p. 108)
- > "**Lactate fermentation** is a normal, daily occurrence during activities of short duration that causes no harm to the muscles or the individual." (p. 108)

### Equations / quantitative
**Glycolysis (overall):**
$$\mathrm{Glucose} + 2\,\mathrm{NAD^+} + 2\,\mathrm{ADP} + 2\,\mathrm{P_i} \to 2\,\mathrm{Pyruvate} + 2\,\mathrm{NADH} + 2\,\mathrm{H^+} + 2\,\mathrm{ATP} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O}$$

**Anaerobic regeneration of NAD⁺ via lactate:**
$$\mathrm{Pyruvate} + \mathrm{NADH} + \mathrm{H^+} \xrightarrow{\text{LDH}} \mathrm{Lactate} + \mathrm{NAD^+}$$

## Section 5.2 — Aerobic Respiration (pp. 109–113)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The Citric Acid Cycle** (pp. 109–111)
- **Electron Transport and Oxidative Phosphorylation** (pp. 111–113)
  - **Function of Oxygen** (pp. 112–113)
  - **ATP Balance Sheet** (p. 113)

### Core claims
- **Mitochondrial entry:** pyruvate enters the matrix via a pyruvate carrier; the **pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDH)** decarboxylates pyruvate (releasing CO₂) and attaches the remaining two-carbon acetyl group to **coenzyme A**, forming **acetyl-CoA** + NADH (Fig 5.5).
- **Citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle, TCA cycle)** in the mitochondrial **matrix** (Fig 5.7):
  - Acetyl-CoA (C₂) condenses with **oxaloacetate (C₄)** → **citrate (C₆)** (step 1).
  - 8 enzymatic steps regenerate oxaloacetate while releasing 2 CO₂ and harvesting electrons.
  - **Per turn:** 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 GTP (≡ 1 ATP), 2 CO₂.
  - Glucose → 2 pyruvates → 2 acetyl-CoA → **2 turns of the cycle per glucose**.
- **Electron transport chain (ETC)** on the inner mitochondrial membrane (Fig 5.8):
  - Four multi-protein **respiratory complexes (I, II, III, IV)** plus two mobile carriers (**ubiquinone / coenzyme Q** and **cytochrome c**) form a downhill cascade of electron transfers between Fe-containing cytochromes (Fe³⁺ ⇌ Fe²⁺).
  - **NADH** donates electrons to **complex I (NADH-CoQ reductase)**.
  - **FADH₂** donates electrons to **complex II (succinate dehydrogenase)** — bypasses complex I, hence yields less ATP per FADH₂.
  - Electrons traverse complexes III (cytochrome c reductase) → cytochrome c → IV (cytochrome c oxidase).
  - **Oxygen is the final electron acceptor:** $\mathrm{\tfrac{1}{2} O_2 + 2 e^- + 2 H^+ \to H_2O}$ at complex IV. Without O₂, the chain backs up, NADH/FADH₂ can't be reoxidized, the citric acid cycle halts, and only anaerobic glycolysis remains.
- **Oxidative phosphorylation / chemiosmosis (Mitchell, 1961 Nobel)** (Fig 5.9):
  - Complexes I, III, and IV are **proton pumps** — they translocate H⁺ from matrix to intermembrane space (4 + 4 + 2 per pair of electrons).
  - This builds a **proton gradient** (electrochemical potential) across the inner membrane.
  - **ATP synthase (complex V)** is a rotary turbine; H⁺ flows back through it down its electrochemical gradient, and the rotation drives **ADP + Pᵢ → ATP**.
- **ATP yield** (Table 5.1, modern textbook standard):
  - Glycolysis: 2 ATP (substrate level) + 2 NADH (~5 ATP via ETC).
  - Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA: 2 NADH (~5 ATP).
  - Citric acid cycle: 2 ATP + 6 NADH (~15 ATP) + 2 FADH₂ (~3 ATP).
  - **Total: ~30 ATP per glucose under aerobic conditions** (textbook gives ~30; older texts say 36–38). Compared to ~2 ATP per glucose under anaerobic / lactate conditions — a **15-fold gain**.
- **Mitochondrial disease (Clinical Application):** mitochondrial DNA mutations cause variable disorders preferentially affecting brain and skeletal muscle. **MELAS** = mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and strokelike episodes. Mitochondria are maternally inherited.
- **Free radicals & antioxidants:** **superoxide (·O₂⁻)** can leak from the ETC; reactive oxygen species (ROS) cause oxidative stress; antioxidants (vitamins C, E; SOD; catalase) scavenge them.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Each glucose molecule, in being converted into 2 molecules of pyruvic acid in glycolysis, also produces 2 molecules of NADH." (p. 109)
- > "The citric acid cycle … takes place in the mitochondrial matrix." (p. 109)
- > "For each 'turn' of the cycle, 1 ATP, 3 NADH, and 1 FADH₂ are produced." (p. 111)
- > "The first three of these respiratory complexes serve as **proton pumps**." (p. 111)

- > "Oxygen, from the air we breathe, allows electron transport to continue by functioning as the **final electron acceptor** of the electron-transport chain." (p. 112)
- > "Mitochondrial DNA is inherited from the oocyte, and so is passed from mother to child." (p. 112)

### Equations / quantitative

**Pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction:**
$$\mathrm{Pyruvate} + \mathrm{NAD^+} + \mathrm{CoA-SH} \to \mathrm{Acetyl\text{-}CoA} + \mathrm{CO_2} + \mathrm{NADH}$$

**Krebs cycle per turn:**
$$\mathrm{Acetyl\text{-}CoA} + 3\,\mathrm{NAD^+} + \mathrm{FAD} + \mathrm{GDP} + \mathrm{P_i} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} \to \mathrm{CoA\text{-}SH} + 3\,\mathrm{NADH} + 3\,\mathrm{H^+} + \mathrm{FADH_2} + \mathrm{GTP} + 2\,\mathrm{CO_2}$$

**Cytochrome iron redox:**
$$\mathrm{Fe^{3+}} + e^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Fe^{2+}}$$

**Complex IV terminal step:**
$$2\,\mathrm{H^+} + 2\,e^- + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{O_2} \to \mathrm{H_2O}$$

**Aerobic glucose oxidation (overall):**
$$\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6} + 6\,\mathrm{O_2} + \approx 30\,\mathrm{ADP} + 30\,\mathrm{P_i} \to 6\,\mathrm{CO_2} + 6\,\mathrm{H_2O} + \approx 30\,\mathrm{ATP}$$

## Section 5.3 — Interconversion of Glucose, Lactate, and Glycogen (pp. 113–116)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Glycogenesis and Glycogenolysis** (pp. 113–115)
- **The Cori Cycle** (pp. 115–116)

### Core claims
- The body holds ~80–90 g glycogen in the liver and ~350 g in skeletal muscle — but the liver pool is the primary blood-glucose reservoir because **liver has glucose 6-phosphatase**; skeletal muscle does not.
- **Glycogenesis** (glucose → glycogen): glucose → glucose 6-phosphate → glucose 1-phosphate → UDP-glucose → glycogen. Stimulated by **insulin** after a carbohydrate meal.
- **Glycogenolysis** (glycogen → glucose): glycogen → glucose 1-phosphate → glucose 6-phosphate. In liver, **glucose 6-phosphatase** then releases free glucose into blood. Stimulated by **glucagon** (fasting) and **epinephrine** (fight-or-flight).
- **Gluconeogenesis** (in liver and kidney during fasting): makes glucose de novo from non-carbohydrate precursors — **lactate** (from RBCs and exercising muscle), **glycerol** (from triglyceride breakdown), and **glucogenic amino acids** (most aas; alanine in particular).
- **Cori cycle** (Fig 5.11): exercising muscle exports lactate to the liver; the liver runs gluconeogenesis on that lactate; the resulting glucose is released back to blood; muscle reabsorbs it and oxidizes or restocks glycogen. Net effect: muscle generates ATP via glycolysis even when its O₂ supply is limited, "paying back" the deficit to the liver during recovery (the **oxygen debt**).
- **Hormonal control:** insulin is the only major anabolic ("storage") hormone — it drives glycogenesis, lipogenesis, and protein synthesis. Glucagon (and to a lesser extent epinephrine, cortisol, growth hormone) drive glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and lipolysis. Most of this control is mechanistically detailed in Ch 19.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The liver contains between 80 and 90 grams of glycogen, which can be converted to glucose and used by other organs." (p. 114)
- > "Glycogen formation from glucose is called **glycogenesis**; the breakdown of glycogen back to glucose is called **glycogenolysis**." (p. 114)
- > "The liver, but not the muscle, contains the enzyme **glucose 6-phosphatase**; this allows the liver to release free glucose into the blood." (p. 114)
- > "In the **Cori cycle**, lactate produced by exercising skeletal muscles is delivered by the blood to the liver, where it is converted to glucose." (p. 115)

## Section 5.4 — Metabolism of Lipids and Proteins (pp. 116–127)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Lipid Metabolism** (pp. 116–118)
  - **Breakdown of Fat (Lipolysis)** (p. 116)
  - **Function of Brown Fat** (p. 117)
  - **Ketone Bodies** (pp. 117–118)
- **Amino Acid Metabolism** (pp. 119–120)
  - **Transamination** (pp. 119–120)
  - **Oxidative Deamination** (p. 120)
- **Uses of Different Energy Sources** (pp. 120–127)

### Core claims
- **Triglyceride storage and lipolysis:** white adipose tissue holds the bulk of long-term energy reserves (~140,000 kcal). Triglycerides are hydrolyzed by **hormone-sensitive lipase** during fasting and exercise into **3 fatty acids + 1 glycerol**.
  - **Glycerol** is converted by the liver to dihydroxyacetone phosphate → enters glycolysis or gluconeogenesis.
  - **Fatty acids** are transported in blood bound to **albumin**, then taken up by tissues and oxidized.
- **β-oxidation** (Fig 5.14) in the mitochondrial matrix. Each cycle of β-oxidation:
  1. Activates the fatty acid → acyl-CoA in the cytosol (using ATP) and transports it into the mitochondrion via the **carnitine shuttle**.
  2. Repeatedly cleaves a 2-carbon acetyl-CoA fragment from the carboxyl end of the chain, also producing 1 NADH and 1 FADH₂ per cycle.
  3. The acetyl-CoA enters the Krebs cycle; the NADH/FADH₂ enter the ETC.
- **Energy yield example:** complete β-oxidation of palmitic acid (C₁₆) yields **8 acetyl-CoA + 7 NADH + 7 FADH₂**, then via Krebs + ETC → **~106 ATP per palmitate** (vs. ~30 per glucose) — explaining why fat is the densest fuel.
- **Brown fat** contains **UCP-1 (thermogenin / uncoupling protein 1)** in mitochondrial inner membranes; UCP-1 short-circuits the proton gradient, generating heat instead of ATP — critical for neonatal thermogenesis and rediscovered in adult humans.
- **Ketone bodies** (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone) are produced by the liver when acetyl-CoA from accelerated β-oxidation exceeds Krebs-cycle capacity (e.g., during fasting, low-carb diet, untreated type 1 diabetes). They serve as alternative fuel for brain and muscle.
  - **Ketosis** = mild controlled rise in ketones (fasting, ketogenic diet).
  - **Ketoacidosis** = pathological elevation, especially in **type 1 diabetes** — acidemia, dehydration, ketonuria, fruity breath, can be life-threatening.
- **Amino acid metabolism:** nitrogen enters the body as dietary protein and leaves as urinary urea. **Positive nitrogen balance** in growing children, pregnancy, and recovery; **negative nitrogen balance** in starvation, wasting disease.
- **9 essential amino acids** must come from diet: His (in children), Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr, Trp, Val.
- **Transamination (Fig 5.15):** amine group is transferred from one amino acid to a keto acid, swapping their identities. Catalyzed by aminotransferases (AST = aspartate AT; ALT = alanine AT — clinically used to detect hepatocyte injury, Ch 13).
- **Oxidative deamination (Fig 5.16):** glutamate releases its amine as ammonia (NH₃), regenerating α-ketoglutarate (an entry point into the Krebs cycle). Liver converts NH₃ to **urea** via the **urea cycle**.
- The **carbon skeletons** of amino acids may be **glucogenic** (enter as pyruvate, oxaloacetate, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, fumarate — feed gluconeogenesis), **ketogenic** (enter as acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate — feed ketone synthesis), or both.
- **Interconvertibility** (Fig 5.18 / Table 5.4): glycogen, fat, and protein are interconvertible via the central acetyl-CoA + Krebs cycle hub, with two key restrictions: (1) acetyl-CoA **cannot** be net-converted to glucose in humans (no glyoxylate cycle); (2) essential amino acids cannot be synthesized de novo.
- **Respiratory quotient (RQ)** = CO₂ produced / O₂ consumed:
  - Carbohydrate ≈ 1.00.
  - Fat ≈ 0.70.
  - Protein ≈ 0.80.
  Used to estimate substrate mix during exercise and fasting (Ch 16, 19).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Beta-oxidation refers to the metabolic pathway by which fatty acids are broken down within the mitochondrial matrix into two-carbon fragments." (p. 117)
- > "Ketone bodies are small, water-soluble molecules produced in the liver and derived from fatty acids during the breakdown of stored triglycerides (fat)." (p. 118)
- > "Type 1 diabetes mellitus may cause an excessive amount of ketone body production, resulting in **ketoacidosis**." (p. 118)
- > "An adequate amount of all 20 amino acids is required to build proteins for growth and to replace the proteins that are turned over. However, only 8 of these (9 in children) cannot be produced by the body and must be obtained in the diet. These are the **essential amino acids**." (p. 120)
- > "This type of reaction, in which the amine group is transferred from one amino acid to form another, is called **transamination**." (p. 120)
- > "Brown fat … is specialized for the production of heat. This is also known as nonshivering thermogenesis." (p. 117)

## Clinical apparatus

- **Clinical Investigation: Andrea** — opens chapter. Andrea has metabolic syndrome, goes on a low-carb diet, and tests her urine for ketones. Teaching: ketosis vs. ketoacidosis, fasting metabolism, brown-fat thermogenesis.
- **Clinical Application — Myocardial ischemia & lactate** (§ 5.1): inadequate coronary blood flow forces ischemic myocardium to lactate fermentation; prolonged lactate buildup causes cell death and myocardial infarction. Angina pectoris signals reversible ischemia; nitroglycerin treats it by vasodilation.
- **Clinical Application — Mitochondrial diseases & MELAS** (§ 5.2): mtDNA mutations preferentially harm brain and skeletal muscle (high ATP demand); MELAS = mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, strokelike episodes. Maternally inherited.
- **Clinical Application — Free radicals & antioxidants** (§ 5.2): superoxide leakage from the ETC drives oxidative stress; antioxidants scavenge ROS.
- **Clinical Application — Ketogenic diet & ketoacidosis** (§ 5.4): controlled ketosis from fasting or low-carb diet is benign; type-1 diabetic ketoacidosis is pathological.
- **Clinical Application — Oxygen debt / EPOC** (§ 5.1): excess post-exercise oxygen consumption pays back lactate via the Cori cycle and replenishes muscle phosphocreatine, glycogen, and myoglobin O₂.
- **Clinical Application — Gout** (§ 5.4 context): purine catabolism endpoint is uric acid; impaired excretion produces hyperuricemia and monosodium urate crystals in joints.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Metabolism / catabolism / anabolism** — sum of biochemical reactions / break-down / build-up.
- **Aerobic vs. anaerobic respiration** — with O₂ (full Krebs + ETC; ~30 ATP/glucose) vs. without (glycolysis + lactate; 2 ATP/glucose).
- **Glycolysis** — 9-step cytoplasmic pathway; glucose → 2 pyruvate; net +2 ATP, +2 NADH.
- **Pyruvate / lactate** — three-carbon end product of glycolysis / its reduced form under anaerobiosis.
- **Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)** — interconverts pyruvate and lactate.
- **Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex** — pyruvate + NAD⁺ + CoA → acetyl-CoA + NADH + CO₂.
- **Acetyl-CoA** — 2-carbon entry to the Krebs cycle; also the lipid synthesis precursor.
- **Citric acid cycle (Krebs / TCA cycle)** — mitochondrial matrix cycle yielding 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP, 2 CO₂ per turn.
- **Electron transport chain (ETC)** — inner mitochondrial-membrane electron cascade; four complexes + Q, cyt c.
- **Oxidative phosphorylation / chemiosmosis** — coupling of e⁻ transport to ATP synthesis via the proton gradient.
- **ATP synthase (complex V)** — rotary H⁺-driven ATP-producing turbine.
- **Final electron acceptor** — O₂ at complex IV; reduced to H₂O.
- **NADH / FADH₂** — electron carriers that deliver e⁻ to complexes I / II.
- **Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)** — maternally inherited; codes for some ETC subunits.
- **MELAS** — mitochondrial encephalomyopathy with lactic acidosis and strokelike episodes.
- **Free radical / superoxide / antioxidant** — ROS from ETC leakage / a specific ROS / scavenger.
- **Glycogen / glycogenesis / glycogenolysis** — branched glucose polymer / glucose → glycogen / glycogen → glucose.
- **Glucose 6-phosphatase** — liver enzyme that releases free glucose from G6P; muscle lacks it.
- **Gluconeogenesis** — de novo glucose synthesis from lactate, glycerol, glucogenic AAs.
- **Cori cycle** — muscle lactate → liver glucose → back to muscle.
- **Oxygen debt / EPOC** — post-exercise O₂ consumption above resting baseline.
- **Insulin / glucagon / epinephrine** — the principal hormones of fuel storage / mobilization.
- **Lipolysis / β-oxidation** — triglyceride → fatty acids + glycerol / fatty acid → acetyl-CoA cycles.
- **Carnitine shuttle** — transports long-chain fatty acids across the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- **Brown fat / UCP-1 / nonshivering thermogenesis** — heat-generating adipose / uncoupling protein / heat as ATP alternative.
- **Ketone body / ketogenesis / ketosis / ketoacidosis** — acetoacetate, β-OH-butyrate, acetone / their synthesis / mild rise / pathological rise.
- **Essential amino acids** — 9 must come from diet (Lys, Trp, Phe, Thr, Val, Met, Leu, Ile, His).
- **Nitrogen balance** — positive in growth, negative in starvation/disease.
- **Transamination / AST / ALT** — amine swap between AA and keto acid / aspartate AT / alanine AT.
- **Oxidative deamination** — glutamate → α-KG + NH₃.
- **Urea cycle** — liver conversion of NH₃ to urea.
- **Glucogenic / ketogenic amino acids** — feed gluconeogenesis / feed ketone synthesis.
- **Respiratory quotient (RQ)** — CO₂/O₂; 1.0 for CHO, 0.7 for fat.
