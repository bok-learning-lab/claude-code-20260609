### Core claims
- CNS = brain + spinal cord; PNS = cranial + spinal nerves and their ganglia.
- A neuron has three regions: **cell body** (with nucleus, Nissl bodies = stacked rough ER), **dendrites** (graded, receptive), **axon** (action potentials, conducted away). Origin at the **axon hillock**; up to 200+ **axon collaterals** can synapse with 30,000–60,000 other neurons.
- **Axonal transport** is bidirectional and ATP-dependent:
  - **Anterograde** (cell body → terminal): **kinesin** along microtubules. Fast component ~200–400 mm/day (vesicles, mitochondria, channels); two slow components ~0.2–8 mm/day (cytoskeleton, soluble proteins).
  - **Retrograde** (terminal → cell body): **dynein** + dynactin. Returns endosomes, autophagosomes — also the route by which **herpes**, **rabies**, **tetanus toxin** invade the CNS from the periphery.
- **Functional classification:** sensory (afferent), motor (efferent), interneuron (entirely within CNS). Motor neurons subdivide into **somatic** (skeletal muscle) and **autonomic** (smooth, cardiac, glands; Ch 9). Autonomic motor cell bodies sit in **ganglia** outside the CNS.
- **Structural classification:**
  - **Pseudounipolar** — one process branching into a peripheral and central limb; sensory neurons.
  - **Bipolar** — two processes; retina, cochlea.
  - **Multipolar** — many dendrites, one axon; motor neurons and most interneurons.
- A **nerve** is a bundle of axons in the PNS; **tract** = bundle in the CNS. **Ganglion** = cell-body cluster in PNS; **nucleus** = cell-body cluster in CNS.
- **Neuroglia of the PNS:**
  - **Schwann cells** — myelinate one segment of one axon; also wrap unmyelinated axons; cytoplasm and nucleus reside outside the myelin → form the **neurilemma**.
  - **Satellite cells** — support cell bodies in sensory and autonomic ganglia.
- **Neuroglia of the CNS:**
  - **Oligodendrocytes** — like an octopus, one cell myelinates several axons; CNS axons lack a neurilemma.
  - **Microglia** — uniquely embryologically derived from yolk-sac macrophages; phagocytose pathogens and debris; activated microglia change shape and migrate to sites of injury; also "prune" synapses.
  - **Astrocytes** — most abundant glia (≤ 90% of nervous tissue in some areas); ~10 named functions.
  - **Ependymal cells** — line ventricles and central canal; cooperate with choroid plexus to produce CSF.
- **PNS myelin formation:** Schwann cells wrap an axon repeatedly; cytoplasm is squeezed to the outer layer (the neurilemma). Each Schwann cell wraps ~1 mm of axon; gaps between adjacent cells = **nodes of Ranvier** with high Na⁺-channel density. Unmyelinated axons (< 2 μm) are wrapped only once by Schwann cells.
- **CNS myelin formation:** oligodendrocytes extend multiple processes that wrap several axons; the white matter is white because of myelin.
- **Demyelinating diseases** (Clinical Application):
  - **Guillain-Barré syndrome** — T-cell attack on **PNS** myelin; muscle weakness (including diaphragm), autonomic dysfunction.
  - **Multiple sclerosis** (MS) — autoimmune T-cell attack on **CNS** myelin; demyelination, gliosis, axon degeneration; 20–40 y onset; 2× more common in women; chronic relapsing-remitting course.
- **Axon regeneration:**
  - **PNS:** Schwann-cell **regeneration tube** guides the proximal axon stump back to its target. Schwann cells secrete attractant chemicals; major nerves can be surgically reconnected.
  - **CNS:** poor regeneration; **Nogo** (oligodendrocyte protein) and the **glial scar** (astrocytes) are major inhibitors; antibodies to Nogo + neural stem cells + growth factors have restored some hindlimb function in spinal-cord-transected rats.
- **Neurotrophins:** NGF (1986 Nobel) maintains sympathetic and sensory neurons; BDNF, NT-3, NT-4, GDNF (from astrocytes; supports dopaminergic and spinal motor neurons). In adults, neurotrophins regulate neural stem cells in hippocampus and subventricular zone, and mediate synaptic changes in learning.
- **Ten functions of astrocytes** (consolidated from the chapter list):
  1. Take up extracellular K⁺ liberated by neural activity.
  2. Take up **glutamate** from synapses and convert it to **glutamine** (via glutamine synthetase; uniquely astrocyte); release glutamine for re-use by glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons.
  3. End-feet on capillaries take up **glucose** → produce **lactate** for neurons.
  4. Provide **lactate** as a preferred fuel for active neurons (astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle); essential for long-term memory consolidation in hippocampus.
  5. Required for synapse formation, maturation, and maintenance.
  6. Regulate adult **neurogenesis** in hippocampus and subventricular zone.
  7. Secrete **GDNF** for spinal motor neurons and midbrain dopaminergic neurons.
  8. Induce formation of the **blood–brain barrier**.
  9. Receive neuronal signals (glutamate → astrocyte Ca²⁺ rise) — bidirectional **neuron–glia crosstalk**.
  10. Release **gliotransmitters** (glutamate, ATP, adenosine, D-serine) that modulate surrounding neurons.
- **Blood–brain barrier (BBB):** CNS capillary endothelial cells are joined by **tight junctions**, eliminating paracellular pores. Molecules must cross **transcellularly** via diffusion, channels, carriers, or vesicles. O₂, CO₂, ethanol, barbiturates cross freely (lipid solubility). Glucose uses **GLUT1**, always present, insulin-independent. **Pericytes** surround endothelial cells and contribute. Drug consequences: **L-dopa** crosses BBB (dopamine does not); only certain antibiotics cross to treat meningitis.

### Core claims
- **Resting membrane potential (RMP)** ~ −70 mV in neurons, ~ −85 mV in cardiac muscle; depends on Na⁺/K⁺ pump activity and the fact that the membrane is much more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺.
- **Depolarization** (inside less negative or positive) = excitatory; **repolarization** = return to RMP; **hyperpolarization** (inside more negative) = inhibitory.
- **Voltage-gated channels** open in response to depolarization to threshold; **chemically (ligand)-gated channels** open in response to neurotransmitter binding (mostly at dendrites/cell body); plus always-open **leak channels** (K⁺ leak dominates at rest).
- **Na⁺ channel kinetics:** at rest, gated channels closed but occasionally flicker open (small Na⁺ leak). At threshold (~ −55 mV), they open quickly, then **inactivate** within ~1 ms via a "ball and chain" or shape-change mechanism. Inactivated channels cannot reopen until repolarization restores the resting (closed-but-openable) state.
- **K⁺ channel kinetics:** gated K⁺ channels open with a delay relative to Na⁺ channels; they close more slowly, allowing slight after-hyperpolarization.
- **Action potential** (Fig 7.14):
  1. Threshold depolarization (~ −55 mV) opens voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → **positive feedback** explosive Na⁺ influx → membrane shoots toward ENa (+66 mV) but peaks ~ +30 mV because Na⁺ channels inactivate.
  2. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open → K⁺ outflux repolarizes the membrane.
  3. Continued K⁺ efflux briefly drives membrane past RMP → **after-hyperpolarization** (toward EK).
  4. K⁺ channels close → RMP re-established. Total duration ≈ 3 ms.
- The Na⁺/K⁺ pump is **not** directly involved in the AP — it restores the gradient afterward. A cyanide-poisoned axon can fire several APs before the gradient runs down.
- **All-or-none law:** AP amplitude does not depend on stimulus strength once threshold is reached; it is always ~ 100 mV (−70 to +30 mV).
- **Stimulus intensity coding** is **frequency-modulated** (FM), not amplitude-modulated. Stronger stimulus → higher AP firing rate. In a population of axons, stronger stimuli also **recruit** axons with higher thresholds.
- **Refractory periods:**
  - **Absolute refractory period** — Na⁺ channels inactivated; no further AP possible regardless of stimulus strength.
  - **Relative refractory period** — K⁺ channels still open (hyperpolarizing the membrane); a stronger-than-normal stimulus can still elicit an AP.
- **Cable properties** are *poor*: depolarization decays within ~1–2 mm. Axons cannot conduct by cable alone — they must regenerate the AP step by step.
- **Conduction in an unmyelinated axon:** each AP "injects" positive charges (Na⁺) that depolarize the adjacent membrane to threshold. The previously fired patch is refractory, so conduction is **unidirectional**.
- **Conduction in a myelinated axon (saltatory conduction):** Na⁺ channels concentrated at nodes of Ranvier (~10,000/μm²); APs leap from node to node. Faster because:
  1. Cable spread is the rate-limiting step → faster cable means faster conduction.
  2. Fewer regenerations per length of axon.
  3. Myelinated axons are also generally thicker → less cytoplasmic resistance.
- **Local anesthetics** (Clinical Application): cocaine, procaine, lidocaine bind axonal Na⁺ channels, preventing AP production in sensory axons. **Saxitoxin** (red-tide dinoflagellates → contaminated shellfish, paralytic shellfish poisoning) and **tetrodotoxin** (pufferfish) also block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels — potentially lethal from diaphragm paralysis.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "All cells in the body maintain a potential difference (voltage) across the membrane, or resting membrane potential (rmp), in which the inside of the cell is negatively charged in comparison to the outside of the cell." (p. 170)
- > "Depolarization of a dendrite or cell body is excitatory, whereas hyperpolarization is inhibitory, in terms of their effects on the production of nerve impulses." (p. 171)
- > "A positive feedback loop is thus created, causing the rate of Na⁺ entry and depolarization to accelerate in an explosive fashion." (p. 172)
- > "The amplitude (size) of action potentials is therefore all-or-none." (p. 174)

### Equations / quantitative

**Action-potential excursion** (typical mammalian neuron):
$$\mathrm{RMP} \approx -70\,\mathrm{mV} \to +30\,\mathrm{mV} \to -70\,\mathrm{mV} \quad (\sim 3\,\mathrm{ms})$$

**Per-AP ion exchange** (typical mammalian axon): about **1 K⁺ in 3,000** is exchanged for an Na⁺ — the gradient is barely disturbed by a single AP, yet the pump still does enormous cumulative work (a typical neuron has ~1 million Na⁺/K⁺ pumps moving ~200 million ions/s).

  - **Actions of Neurotransmitter** (pp. 181–182)

### Core claims
- **Synapse** = functional connection between a neuron and another cell. Most are chemical, one-way, and have a ~10-nm cleft. **Neuromuscular junction** = neuron-to-muscle synapse. Synapses are named axodendritic, axosomatic, or axoaxonic by the postsynaptic location.
- **Historical context:** Otto Loewi's 1921 experiment with two isolated frog hearts established chemical synaptic transmission via "Vagusstoff" (later identified as ACh).
- **Electrical synapses (gap junctions):** apposed cells are joined by **connexin** hemichannels (six connexins per cell membrane), forming aqueous pores that pass ions and small molecules directly. Found in cardiac muscle (cell-to-cell AP spread), most smooth muscle (uterus during labor), and some CNS regions; also between neuroglia. Newer evidence: gap junctions can be regulated and can interact functionally with chemical synapses.
- **Chemical synapses:**
  - Presynaptic **terminal boutons** house synaptic vesicles containing neurotransmitter.
  - **Cell adhesion molecules** (CAMs) bridge pre- and postsynaptic membranes across the cleft, holding alignment.
  - APs depolarize the bouton → open **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels** → Ca²⁺ enters cytoplasm.
- **Vesicle docking and exocytosis (2013 Nobel):**
  1. **SNARE complex** bridges vesicle and plasma membrane: **synaptobrevin-2** (vesicle) + **syntaxin** + **SNAP-25** (plasma membrane).
  2. Ca²⁺ binds **synaptotagmin** (vesicle Ca²⁺ sensor).
  3. Ca²⁺–synaptotagmin engages the SNARE complex → membrane fusion → pore opens → neurotransmitter exocytosis in < 1 ms.
- **Toxins (Clinical Application):**
  - **Tetanus toxin** — protease cleaving synaptobrevin-2 on inhibitory neurons → loss of inhibition → spastic paralysis ("lockjaw").
  - **Botulinum toxin** — cleaves SNAP-25 on cholinergic excitatory neurons → no ACh release → flaccid paralysis. Therapeutic use as **Botox**.
- **Postsynaptic action of neurotransmitter:** binds a **receptor protein** that may itself be a ligand-gated ion channel (next section), or coupled to one via second messengers.
  - **EPSP** — depolarization (Na⁺ in / Ca²⁺ in) toward threshold.
  - **IPSP** — hyperpolarization (Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out) away from threshold.
- **Functional specialization** within a multipolar neuron (Fig 7.24): dendrites and cell body → ligand-gated channels → graded EPSPs/IPSPs → spread by cable properties to **axon initial segment** (5–80 μm of unmyelinated axon at the hillock with very high voltage-gated channel density) → AP initiation → conduction along axon → release at terminal boutons.

- > "A synapse is the functional connection between a neuron and a second cell." (p. 178)
- > "In gap junctions, the membranes of the two cells are separated by only 2 nanometers." (p. 179)
- > "Exocytosis of synaptic vesicles, and the consequent release of neurotransmitter molecules into the synaptic cleft, is triggered by action potentials that stimulate the entry of Ca²⁺ into the terminal bouton through voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels." (p. 180)
- > "Tetanus toxin and botulinum toxin (Botox) are potentially deadly bacterial products that block the release of neurotransmitters." (p. 181)

## Section 7.4 — Acetylcholine as a Neurotransmitter (pp. 182–188)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Chemically Regulated Channels** (pp. 183–186)
  - **Ligand-Gated Channels** (pp. 183–184)
  - **G-Protein-Coupled Channels** (pp. 185–186)
- **Acetylcholinesterase (AChE)** (pp. 186–187)
- **Acetylcholine in the PNS** (p. 187)
- **Acetylcholine in the CNS** (p. 188)

### Core claims
- **Two ACh receptor subtypes,** named for selective toxins:
  - **Nicotinic ACh receptors** — also activated by nicotine; **ligand-gated cation channels** (the receptor *is* the channel). Found in skeletal muscle (motor end plate), autonomic ganglia, and specific CNS regions. Five polypeptide subunits, two of which bind ACh. Binding → channel opens → simultaneous Na⁺ in / K⁺ out → **EPSP** (depolarization, but does not reverse polarity because both ions move at once).
  - **Muscarinic ACh receptors** — also activated by muscarine (from Amanita muscaria); **GPCRs**, no built-in channel. Five subtypes (M1–M5). Found in smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands, brain.
- **Mechanism of muscarinic action** (G-protein cycle): ACh binds the receptor → α subunit exchanges GDP for GTP → α dissociates from βγ → α or βγ binds a separate ion channel or enzyme to open/close it → GTP hydrolyzed → reaggregation. The **βγ complex** binds K⁺ channels in cardiac pacemaker cells, opening them → K⁺ outflow → **IPSP**, slowing heart rate (the vagus's effect on the SA node). In smooth muscle of the stomach, the **α subunit** *closes* previously open K⁺ channels → depolarization → contraction.
- **EPSP vs. AP comparison** (Table 7.4): EPSP is graded, summates, no refractory period, no positive feedback, peaks near 0 mV, decays over 1–2 mm; AP is all-or-none, no summation, refractory, positive feedback on Na⁺ gating, peaks at +30–40 mV, conducts without decrement.
- **Drug pharmacology of cholinergic transmission** (Table 7.5):
  - **Botulinum toxin** — blocks ACh release (SNARE protease).
  - **Curare** — antagonist at nicotinic receptors → flaccid paralysis (South American blow-gun poison; clinical muscle relaxant).
  - **α-Bungarotoxin** — irreversibly binds nicotinic receptors (krait snake venom).
  - **Saxitoxin / tetrodotoxin** — block voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
  - **Nerve gas / organophosphate pesticides** — inhibit AChE → cholinergic overstimulation.
  - **Neostigmine, physostigmine, pyridostigmine** — therapeutic AChE inhibitors (myasthenia gravis, Alzheimer's).
  - **Strychnine** — blocks glycine receptors in spinal cord → uncontrolled motor neuron firing → spastic paralysis → death by diaphragm contraction.
- **Acetylcholinesterase (AChE):** hydrolyzes ACh into acetate + choline in the synaptic cleft. Recycled choline is taken back into the terminal bouton and used to resynthesize ACh.
- **Myasthenia gravis** (Clinical Application): autoimmune antibodies against the **nicotinic ACh receptors** at the motor end plate → muscle weakness, especially of eyes, eyelids, face. Treated with AChE inhibitors.
- **ACh in the PNS:** somatic motor neurons release ACh at neuromuscular junctions → EPSPs called **end-plate potentials** open adjacent voltage-gated channels → APs along the muscle plasma membrane (sarcolemma) → contraction (Ch 12). Parasympathetic axons release ACh at autonomic effectors via muscarinic receptors.
- **ACh in the CNS:** many cholinergic neurons; the dendrites and cell body house the receptors, EPSPs travel to the axon initial segment to generate APs.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Acetylcholine (ACh) is used as an excitatory neurotransmitter by some neurons in the CNS and by somatic motor neurons at the neuromuscular junction. At autonomic nerve endings, ACh may be either excitatory or inhibitory, depending on the organ involved." (p. 182)
- > "The stimulatory effect of ACh on skeletal muscle cells is produced by the binding of ACh to nicotinic ACh receptors." (p. 182)
- > "Muscarinic receptors are thus members of a family of receptors called G-protein-coupled receptors." (p. 185)
- > "The inactivation of ACh is achieved by means of an enzyme called acetylcholinesterase, or AChE, which is present on the postsynaptic membrane or immediately outside the membrane, with its active site facing the synaptic cleft." (p. 186)
- > "Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disease caused by antibodies that block the nicotinic ACh receptors, particularly in the motor end plates of skeletal muscle cells." (p. 183)

## Section 7.5 — Monoamines as Neurotransmitters (pp. 188–192)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Serotonin as a Neurotransmitter** (pp. 190–191)
- **Dopamine as a Neurotransmitter** (p. 191)
  - **Nigrostriatal Dopamine System** (p. 191)
  - **Mesolimbic Dopamine System** (p. 191)
- **Norepinephrine as a Neurotransmitter** (pp. 191–192)

### Core claims
- **Monoamines** are amine-derived neurotransmitters:
  - **Catecholamines** (from tyrosine; common catechol ring): **dopamine, norepinephrine (NE), epinephrine** (last is mainly an adrenal hormone, Ch 9, 11).
  - **Serotonin (5-HT)** — from tryptophan; midline brain-stem **raphe nuclei**; mood, appetite, cerebral circulation.
  - **Histamine** — from histidine; CNS wakefulness (hence antihistamine drowsiness, Ch 8).
- **Inactivation pathway** (Fig 7.30): (1) exocytotic release into cleft → (2) reuptake by presynaptic transporters → (3) intracellular degradation by **monoamine oxidase (MAO)**.
- **Second-messenger action** (Fig 7.31): monoamine binds GPCR → α dissociates with GTP → binds **adenylate cyclase** → produces **cAMP** from ATP → cAMP activates **protein kinase A** → phosphorylation cascade opens ion channels and produces cellular effects.
- **Serotonin** functions: mood, behavior, appetite, cerebral circulation. Classical hallucinogens (LSD, mescaline, psilocybin) act at serotonin receptors. **SSRIs** (Prozac, Zoloft, Paxil, Lexapro, Luvox) inhibit reuptake at the SERT transporter → more synaptic serotonin → treats depression.
- **Dopamine** systems originate in midbrain:
  - **Nigrostriatal** — substantia nigra → corpus striatum (caudate, putamen); motor control. Degeneration of these dopaminergic neurons causes **Parkinson's disease** (tremor, rigidity, slowed movement). Treatment: L-dopa (crosses BBB; dopamine does not), MAO inhibitors.
  - **Mesolimbic** — midbrain → nucleus accumbens and limbic targets (Ch 8); behavior, reward. **D2** receptor implicated in alcoholism and other addictions; addictive drugs (cocaine, morphine, amphetamines, nicotine) activate this pathway. First-generation antipsychotics are D2 antagonists → Parkinsonian side effects. Atypical antipsychotics target multiple receptors.
- **Norepinephrine** is the **sympathetic** postganglionic neurotransmitter at smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands (Ch 9). In the CNS, NE-using neurons drive arousal and mood; tricyclic antidepressants enhance NE transmission centrally but also peripherally → hypertension risk.
- **MAO inhibitors (Clinical Application)**: block MAO → ↑ all monoamines (dopamine, NE, serotonin, melatonin). Used for depression, panic, Parkinson's. Dangerous interactions with **tyramine** in aged cheese, beans, pickled and fermented foods (tyramine is normally MAO-degraded; MAOI + tyramine load → ↑ NE release by sympathetic axons → **hypertensive crisis**).
- **Cocaine (Lifestyle Application):** triple reuptake inhibitor (dopamine, NE, serotonin) → mesolimbic dopamine surge; *also* blocks axonal voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → cardiovascular toxicity (coronary constriction, ↑ HR, ↑ BP), seizures, GI ulcers, kidney damage.

- > "The action of monoamine neurotransmitters at the synapse is stopped by (1) reuptake of the neurotransmitter molecules from the synaptic cleft into the presynaptic terminal bouton, and then (2) degradation of the monoamine by an enzyme called monoamine oxidase (MAO)." (p. 189)
- > "Monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors are drugs that block the degradation of monoamine neurotransmitters … such interactions could provoke a hypertensive crisis." (p. 189)
- > "Parkinson's disease is caused by degeneration of the dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra." (p. 191)
- > "Addictive drugs promote the activity of dopaminergic neurons that arise in the midbrain and terminate in the nucleus accumbens." (p. 191)
- > "Cocaine … blocks the reuptake transporters for norepinephrine and serotonin — it is a triple reuptake inhibitor. In addition to this broad action, cocaine is dangerous because it also blocks membrane Na⁺ channels." (p. 192)

## Section 7.6 — Other Neurotransmitters (pp. 192–197)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Amino Acids as Neurotransmitters** (pp. 192–194)

  - **Inhibitory Neurotransmitters** (pp. 193–194)
- **Polypeptides as Neurotransmitters** (pp. 194–195)
  - **Endogenous Opioids** (pp. 194–195)
  - **Neuropeptide Y** (p. 195)
- **Endocannabinoids as Neurotransmitters** (pp. 195–196)
- **Gases as Neurotransmitters** (p. 196)
- **ATP and Adenosine as Neurotransmitters** (p. 196)

### Core claims
- **Glutamate (glutamic acid)** is the **major excitatory neurotransmitter** in the brain; ≥ 80% of cerebral cortical synapses use it. EPSPs consume the brain's largest fraction of metabolic energy. Three ionotropic glutamate receptor subtypes:
  - **AMPA** — fast Na⁺ inflow → standard EPSP.
  - **NMDA** — Mg²⁺-blocked at rest; requires *both* glutamate (and glycine or D-serine as coagonist) *and* depolarization (typically from AMPA EPSP) to open; Ca²⁺ influx is the key second messenger for **LTP** and memory.
  - **Kainate** — third subtype.
- **Aspartate (aspartic acid)** — minor excitatory.
- **GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid)** — major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain; ~⅓ of brain neurons release it. Binds GABA-A receptor → opens **Cl⁻ channel** → hyperpolarization → **IPSP**.
- **Glycine** — major inhibitory neurotransmitter in spinal cord, brain stem, retina. Opens Cl⁻ channels → IPSP. Critical for reciprocal inhibition of antagonist muscles during flexion (Ch 12). **Strychnine** blocks glycine receptors → spastic paralysis, death from diaphragm contraction.
- **Huntington's disease**: degeneration of GABAergic neurons (caudate, putamen) → uncontrolled movements; caused by CAG-repeat expansion in HTT gene (Ch 3).
- **Benzodiazepines** (Valium, Xanax — Clinical Application): bind a GABA-A receptor allosteric site → ↑ Cl⁻ permeability when GABA is also bound → enhanced inhibition; treat anxiety, insomnia, epilepsy.
- **Polypeptide neurotransmitters / neuropeptides:** many gut hormones double as central neurotransmitters (CCK → satiety; substance P → pain; oxytocin, vasopressin; LHRH, TRH; angiotensin II; insulin, glucagon; somatostatin). Often co-released with classical neurotransmitters from separate vesicles.
- **Endogenous opioids:** 3 receptor types (μ, δ, κ); 30+ peptides in three families — **endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins**. Functions: analgesia (gating pain transmission via presynaptic inhibition of substance P release), reward, mood. Naloxone is a μ-receptor antagonist. β-endorphin rises with intense exercise (> 60% VO₂max).
- **Neuropeptide Y (NPY)** — most abundant brain neuropeptide; stimulates appetite; inhibits glutamate release in hippocampus (anti-seizure). **Leptin** (Ch 19) inhibits NPY release → suppresses appetite.
- **Endocannabinoids** (anandamide, 2-arachidonoyl glycerol [2-AG]) — lipid neurotransmitters. Made on demand from membrane lipids in the postsynaptic neuron; released from dendrites and cell body; diffuse **retrograde** to bind presynaptic CB1 (G-protein-coupled) receptors → inhibit neurotransmitter release. **Depolarization-induced suppression of inhibition** (DSI): postsynaptic depolarization → 2-AG release → suppression of presynaptic GABA release → disinhibition. Endocannabinoid receptors are the most widespread GPCRs in the brain. Implicated in reward, learning, memory.
- **Gases as neurotransmitters:**
  - **Nitric oxide (NO)** — produced by NO synthase from L-arginine. Lipid-soluble → diffuses freely; can act as a **retrograde** neurotransmitter (LTP) or as a paracrine smooth-muscle relaxant (vasodilation; penile erection; Viagra augments). Second messenger: **cGMP**.
  - **Carbon monoxide (CO)** — from heme; may regulate cerebellum, olfactory adaptation, hypothalamic neuroendocrine function; also stimulates cGMP.
  - **Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)** — proposed neurotransmitter, toxic at supraphysiological doses.
- **ATP and adenosine (purines):** released as **cotransmitters** with other classical neurotransmitters; bind P1 (adenosine) and P2 (ATP) purinergic receptors. ATP from astrocytes triggers cerebral vessel dilation; caffeine antagonizes adenosine A1 receptors → delays sleep onset.

- > "Glutamic acid (or glutamate) is the major excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain, producing excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) in at least 80% of the synapses in the cerebral cortex." (p. 192)
- > "There are two inhibitory neurotransmitters in the CNS: GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid), a derivative of glutamic acid, and glycine." (p. 192)
- > "When GABA and glycine bind to their respective receptors, they open channels for Cl⁻ that hyperpolarize the postsynaptic membrane and produce an IPSP." (p. 192)

## Section 7.7 — Synaptic Integration (pp. 197–199)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Synaptic Plasticity** (pp. 197–198)
- **Synaptic Inhibition** (pp. 198–199)

### Core claims
- **Convergence** — many presynaptic axons → one postsynaptic neuron (up to ~1,000 inputs per neuron). **Divergence** — one axon to many targets via collaterals.
- **Spatial summation** — simultaneous EPSPs from multiple converging inputs sum at the cell body. **Temporal summation** — rapidly successive EPSPs from the same presynaptic neuron sum because EPSPs have no refractory period.
- **Long-term potentiation (LTP)** — high-frequency stimulation strengthens synaptic transmission for hours to weeks. Studied mainly in hippocampus, where most axons release glutamate. Mechanism (Fig 8.16):
  - High-frequency glutamate → AMPA receptor depolarization → Mg²⁺ ejected from NMDA pore → NMDA opens → Ca²⁺ influx → activates **CaMKII** → inserts more AMPA receptors at the postsynaptic membrane and increases AMPA channel conductance.
  - Postsynaptic Ca²⁺ also triggers **retrograde messengers** (NO, 2-AG) that modulate presynaptic neurotransmitter release.
  - Persistent change involves CREB-mediated gene transcription, new protein synthesis, and histone acetylation (epigenetic).
- **Long-term depression (LTD)** — low-frequency stimulation weakens synapses. Involves **removal** of AMPA receptors from postsynaptic membrane and shrinkage/disappearance of dendritic spines. Both LTP and LTD depend on Ca²⁺ — fast and large for LTP, slow and modest for LTD.
- **Excitotoxicity** (Clinical Application): excessive synaptic glutamate (e.g., **ischemic stroke**) → excessive postsynaptic Ca²⁺ → activates proteases → ROS → apoptosis. Contributes to neuron death in stroke, Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, MS, ALS.
- **Postsynaptic inhibition** — IPSPs (Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out) hyperpolarize the postsynaptic membrane, reducing the probability of AP. GABA in brain; mostly glycine in spinal cord. EPSPs and IPSPs sum algebraically at the axon hillock.
- **Presynaptic inhibition** — axoaxonic synapse on the presynaptic terminal bouton reduces Ca²⁺ inflow there → less neurotransmitter release. Opioid analgesia uses this on substance-P-releasing terminals in pain pathways.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Spatial summation occurs due to the convergence of terminal boutons from different presynaptic axons (up to a thousand in some cases)." (p. 197)
- > "Long-term potentiation (LTP) may favor transmission along frequently used neural pathways and thus may represent a mechanism of neural 'learning.'" (p. 197)
- > "Both LTP and LTD depend on a rise in Ca²⁺ concentration within the postsynaptic neuron. A rapid rise in the Ca²⁺ concentration causes potentiation (LTP) of the synapse, whereas a smaller but more prolonged rise … results in depression (LTD)." (p. 198)
- > "Excitotoxicity is responsible for neuron death in ischemic strokes." (p. 198)
- > "The ability of the opiates to promote analgesia (reduce pain) is an example of such presynaptic inhibition." (p. 199)
