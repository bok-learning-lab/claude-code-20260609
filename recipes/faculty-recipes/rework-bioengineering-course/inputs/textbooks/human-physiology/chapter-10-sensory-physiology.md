## Section 10.1 — Characteristics of Sensory Receptors (pp. 266–268)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Categories of Sensory Receptors** (pp. 266–267)
  - **Functional Categories** (pp. 266–267)
  - **Tonic and Phasic Receptors: Sensory Adaptation** (p. 267)
- **Law of Specific Nerve Energies** (p. 267)
- **Generator (Receptor) Potential** (pp. 267–268)

### Core claims
- **Transduction.** Sensory receptors convert one form of stimulus energy into action potentials in sensory neurons; the brain *constructs* the perception, which corresponds only indirectly to the physical world ("there is no such thing as cold in the physical world, only varying degrees of heat").
- **Functional categories by modality** (Table 10.1):
  - **Mechanoreceptors** — touch, pressure, hair cells in vestibular/cochlea.
  - **Chemoreceptors** — taste, smell; aortic/carotid bodies (interoceptors).
  - **Photoreceptors** — rods and cones.
  - **Thermoreceptors** — heat and cold receptors.
  - **Nociceptors** — pain; respond to noxious stimuli including extreme heat, acid, bradykinin, prostaglandins, NO, adenosine, ATP. Transmit via glutamate and substance P. Modulated by endogenous opioids (β-endorphin).
- **By information conveyed:** proprioceptors (muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs, joint receptors); cutaneous receptors; the **special senses** (sight, hearing, equilibrium, taste, smell).
- **By location of stimulus:** **exteroceptors** (external environment) vs. **interoceptors** (internal — e.g., blood vessel baroreceptors, blood pH/O₂ chemoreceptors).
- **Tonic vs. phasic.** Phasic receptors burst when a stimulus appears, then quickly adapt; tonic receptors maintain firing throughout stimulation. **Pacinian corpuscles** are exemplars of phasic receptors (the onionlike capsule provides the rapid adaptation — strip the capsule, and the bare ending fires tonically).
- **Sensory adaptation.** Phasic receptors account for our ability to stop noticing constant stimuli (odors, clothing texture). Pain is one of the least adaptable.
- **Law of specific nerve energies.** A sensory neuron evokes only one perceived modality — its **adequate stimulus** — regardless of how the neuron is activated. Mechanical stimulation of the retina (a punch to the eye) is perceived as flashes of light because the visual pathway encodes only "light." Paradoxical cold: a 45 °C rod can re-evoke cold sensation by injuring the cold receptor's ending and generating an "injury current."
- **Generator (receptor) potential.** Local graded depolarizations in sensory dendrites (analogous to EPSPs). Once threshold is reached, action potentials fire; **stimulus intensity is encoded by action potential frequency** (because spike amplitude is all-or-none).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Our perceptions of the world — its textures, colors, and sounds; its warmth, smells, and tastes — are created by the brain from electrochemical nerve impulses delivered to it from sensory receptors." (p. 266)
- > "The perception of cold is entirely a product of the nervous system — there is no such thing as cold in the physical world, only varying degrees of heat." (p. 266)
- > "Nociceptors are pain receptors that depolarize in response to stimuli that accompany tissue damage." (p. 266)
- > "According to the law of specific nerve energies, the sensation evoked by each sensory neuron is that produced by its normal stimulus, or adequate stimulus." (p. 267)
- > "After a threshold depolarization is produced, increases in the amplitude of the generator potential result in increases in the frequency with which action potentials are produced." (p. 268)
- > "The frequency of action potentials that are conducted into the central nervous system serves as the code for the strength of the stimulus." (p. 268)

## Section 10.2 — Cutaneous Sensations (pp. 268–273)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Neural Pathways for Somatesthetic Sensations** (p. 270)
- **Receptive Fields and Sensory Acuity** (pp. 271–272)
  - **Two-Point Touch Threshold** (pp. 271–272)
- **Lateral Inhibition** (pp. 272–273)

### Core claims
- **Five cutaneous receptor classes** (Table 10.2, Fig 10.4):
  - **Free nerve endings** — light touch, hot, cold, pain; throughout skin, around hair follicles.
  - **Merkel's discs** — sustained touch and indentation depth; base of epidermis.
  - **Ruffini corpuscles** — skin stretch; deep dermis/hypodermis.
  - **Meissner's corpuscles** — texture and slow vibration; upper dermis (papillary layer).
  - **Pacinian corpuscles** — deep pressure and fast vibration; deep dermis; up to 4 mm long; 30–50 onion-like lamellae provide phasic adaptation.
- **Low-threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMRs)** are the touch endings of pseudounipolar dorsal-root-ganglion neurons. **C-fiber LTMRs** carry nonpainful gentle-touch sensation as well.
- **Cold and heat receptors** are free endings. **TRP channels** are the molecular receptors:
  - **TRPV1 (capsaicin receptor)** opens at >43 °C and on capsaicin binding; admits Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ → depolarization → pain/heat perception.
  - **Cold/menthol TRP** opens at 8–28 °C and on menthol binding.
  - There are far more free endings for cold than warm.
- **Nociceptors** are stimulated by ATP (from damaged cells), low pH (acid-sensing ion channels — peptides from black mamba venom that block these channels are powerful analgesics), bradykinin, prostaglandins, capsaicin. Initial sharp pain travels in myelinated **A-δ** fibers; dull persistent ache in unmyelinated **C-fibers**.
- **Itch (pruritus)** is distinct from pain. Acute itch responds to histamine (treatable with antihistamines); chronic itch involves non-histamine pathways.
- **Two ascending pathways** for somatesthesia:
  - **Dorsal column–medial lemniscus** — proprioception, touch, pressure; thick myelinated A-α and A-β fibers; ascend ipsilaterally to medulla, decussate, ascend in medial lemniscus to thalamus → postcentral gyrus (somatosensory cortex).
  - **Anterolateral / spinothalamic tract** — temperature and pain; thin A-δ and C-fibers; decussate at the spinal level, ascend contralaterally to thalamus → postcentral gyrus.
  - **The somatosensory map is distorted** — face and hands occupy disproportionately large cortical areas (higher receptor density).
- **Gate control theory of pain.** Rubbing a bruise activates fast A-β touch fibers that excite inhibitory interneurons in the dorsal horn, partially blocking ascending pain signals from the slower A-δ/C fibers.
- **Emotional pain component** projects from thalamus to **anterior cingulate gyrus** (limbic system), separate from the cortical localization of pain.
- **Referred pain.** Visceral afferents converge with somatic afferents on the same spinal interneurons (e.g., cardiac ischemia → angina perceived in the left arm; gallstone → pain under right scapula).
- **Phantom limb.** ~70% of amputees experience pain in a missing limb. Mechanisms include peripheral neuromas, but also cortical reorganization following loss of afferent input.
- **Receptive field & acuity.** Receptive field = area of skin whose stimulation alters a neuron's firing. Acuity ∝ receptor density. The **two-point touch threshold** (Table 10.3) is 2 mm at the first finger and 3 mm at the thumb, vs. 42 mm at the back and 48 mm at the calf. Braille dots are spaced 2.5 mm apart, just above fingertip threshold.
- **Lateral inhibition** (Fig 10.6). The most strongly stimulated sensory neurons inhibit their less-stimulated neighbors via interneurons in the CNS, sharpening sensation boundaries. Lateral inhibition is a general principle in touch, hearing, olfaction, and vision.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Sensations of touch are mediated by naked dendritic endings surrounding hair follicles, and by sensory dendrites associated with non-neural cells." (p. 269)
- > "Pacinian corpuscles, like Meissner's corpuscles, contain flattened cells that form concentric lamellae (layers) around the sensory fibers." (p. 269)
- > "The cold/menthol receptors and the heat/capsaicin receptors are members of the same family of cation (Na⁺ and Ca²⁺) channels, called the transient receptor potential (TRP) channels." (p. 270)
- > "Sensory information from cutaneous mechanoreceptors monitoring touch and pressure, as well as information from proprioceptors, is first carried by large, myelinated axons (classified as A-beta fibers) that ascend in the dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway." (p. 270)
- > "Pain in the back, under the right scapula, is caused by a gallstone when the gallbladder contracts. Referred pains are believed to result because visceral sensory and somatic sensory neurons can synapse on the same interneurons in the spinal cord." (pp. 270–271)
- > "Lateral inhibition and the resultant sharpening of sensation occur within the central nervous system." (p. 272)

## Section 10.3 — Taste and Smell (pp. 273–277)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Taste** (pp. 273–275)
- **Smell** (pp. 275–277)

### Core claims
- **Taste buds** are barrel-shaped clusters of 50–100 neuroepithelial cells with apical microvilli ("taste hairs") bathed in saliva (Fig 10.7). Found in **fungiform** (anterior tongue), **circumvallate** (posterior), and **foliate** (lateral) papillae. Innervated by **chorda tympani (CN VII)** anteriorly and **glossopharyngeal (CN IX)** posteriorly → medulla → thalamus → **insular gustatory cortex** + somatosensory tongue area + prefrontal/orbitofrontal cortex.
- **Five taste modalities** (Fig 10.8) with distinct molecular mechanisms:
  - **Salty** — Na⁺ enters taste cells through ion channels → depolarization.
  - **Sour** — H⁺ enters (and other intracellular effects of acid) → depolarization.
  - **Sweet** — G-protein-coupled receptors with multiple ligand binding sites for sugars; activate adenylate cyclase → cAMP → closes K⁺ channels → depolarization.
  - **Umami** ("savory"; Japanese) — G-protein-coupled receptors activated by L-glutamate and L-aspartate; explains MSG flavor.
  - **Bitter** — G-protein-coupled receptors; uses IP₃/Ca²⁺ second-messenger pathway; broadly tuned for many structurally unrelated toxins; aversive warning signal.
- The G-proteins involved in taste are called **gustducins**. All taste-cell ligand binding ultimately ends in depolarization, transmitter release onto sensory afferent, action potentials to brain.
- **All five taste qualities are distributed across the whole tongue** (the "tongue map" is a myth). A single taste bud may contain cells tuned to each modality. The dominant theory is the **labeled-line theory** — each taste cell carries one quality.
- **Flavor** = taste × smell × trigeminal (V) somatosensation (temperature, texture). Pinching the nose abolishes most flavor.
- **Olfactory epithelium** (in the upper nasal cavity) contains olfactory sensory neurons (bipolar), supporting (sustentacular) cells, and basal stem cells that replace damaged neurons every 1–2 months.
- **~380 olfactory receptor genes** (from ~1,000 in the genome — most are pseudogenes). Each olfactory neuron expresses **one** receptor protein (2004 Nobel: Buck and Axel). Receptors are **G-protein coupled** → up to 50 G-proteins per receptor → adenylate cyclase → cAMP → opens nonselective Na⁺/Ca²⁺ channels → depolarization (Fig 10.11). The amplification (~50× per binding event) explains why we can detect a billionth of an ounce of perfume.
- **Olfactory bulb** sits above the cribriform plate of the ethmoid. Olfactory neurons send unmyelinated axons through the cribriform → synapse in **glomeruli**. Each glomerulus collects from one receptor type, regardless of where those receptors lie in the epithelium. Within glomeruli, axons synapse on **mitral and tufted cells** (secondary neurons). Peri-glomerular GABA interneurons provide lateral inhibition.
- **Olfaction is the only sensory modality projected directly to cerebral cortex without first synapsing in thalamus.** Projection goes via lateral olfactory tracts to piriform cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex — explaining the close linkage of smell with emotion and memory.
- ~10,000 odors can be distinguished from only ~380 receptors because each odorant binds multiple receptors with varying affinity — the brain reads the *pattern* across glomeruli as a unique olfactory "fingerprint."

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Although these sensory epithelial cells are not neurons, they behave like neurons; they become depolarized when stimulated appropriately, produce action potentials, and release neurotransmitters that stimulate sensory neurons associated with the taste buds." (p. 273)
- > "Four different categories of taste are traditionally recognized: salty, sour, sweet, and bitter. There is also a more recently discovered fifth category of taste, termed **umami** (a Japanese term for 'savory')." (p. 274)
- > "All areas of the tongue are able to respond to all five categories of taste. This is true even for a single taste bud." (p. 274)
- > "Although humans have about a thousand genes coding for olfactory receptors, most of these have accumulated mutations that prevent them from being expressed (they are 'pseudogenes'), leaving an estimated 380 genes that code for 380 different olfactory receptor proteins." (p. 275)
- > "Each olfactory sensory neuron expresses only one gene that produces only one type of these receptor proteins." (p. 275)
- > "Unlike other sensory modalities that are first sent to the thalamus and from there relayed to the cerebral cortex, the sense of smell is transmitted directly to the cerebral cortex." (p. 276)
- > "The interconnections between the olfactory and limbic system may explain the close relationship between the sense of smell and emotions, and how a particular odor can evoke emotionally charged memories." (p. 277)

### Figures

#### Figure 10.8 — The five major categories of taste *(viewed)*

A four-panel diagram (the "sweet and umami" panel groups two together) showing each taste modality's transduction mechanism within a taste cell.

**Salty (leftmost panel).** A taste cell with apical microvilli at the top; arrows show **Na⁺** entering through ion channels in the apical membrane. The Na⁺ flow depolarizes the cell, opening **Ca²⁺ channels**, leading to neurotransmitter release at the basal sensory nerve synapse.

**Sour.** Almost identical architecture, but with **H⁺** entering through ion channels (and producing other intracellular effects via the dropping pH). Result: depolarization, Ca²⁺ entry, transmitter release.

**Sweet and Umami.** A G-protein-coupled receptor on the apical membrane; **sugars** (sucrose, glucose) or **amino acids** (L-glutamate for umami; L-aspartate) bind. Dissociation of G-proteins (gustducin) activates a second-messenger system that **closes K⁺ channels**, producing depolarization → Ca²⁺ entry → transmitter release.

**Bitter.** Architecture similar to sweet/umami: a G-protein-coupled receptor; **quinine** (and other bitter molecules) bind. G-protein dissociation activates a second-messenger system that **releases Ca²⁺ from endoplasmic reticulum** (IP₃ pathway) → depolarization → transmitter release.

All four panels share a common ending — apical reception, depolarization mechanism, basal sensory-nerve synapse with vesicular transmitter release. The figure highlights that the two ion-channel tastes (salty/sour) act directly on the membrane potential, while the three G-protein tastes (sweet/umami/bitter) work via second messengers.

> Vision note: This is the canonical taste-transduction figure. Anchor for any "how does X taste work" query — the salty/sour vs. GPCR distinction is the key conceptual divide.

## Section 10.4 — Vestibular Apparatus and Equilibrium (pp. 277–281)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Sensory Hair Cells of the Vestibular Apparatus** (pp. 278–279)
- **Utricle and Saccule** (p. 279)
- **Semicircular Canals** (pp. 279–281)
  - **Neural Pathways** (pp. 280–281)
  - **Nystagmus and Vertigo** (p. 281)

### Core claims
- The **vestibular apparatus** sits in the **membranous labyrinth** inside the bony labyrinth of the temporal bone (Fig 10.12). Two functional units:
  - **Otolith organs (utricle + saccule)** sense linear acceleration.
  - **Three semicircular canals (anterior, posterior, lateral)** sense angular (rotational) acceleration in three orthogonal planes.
- **Endolymph** (in the membranous labyrinth) has uniquely high K⁺ (higher than intracellular) and low Na⁺/Ca²⁺ — depolarization of hair cells occurs by **inward K⁺ flow** down its electrochemical gradient. **Perilymph** (in the bony labyrinth around the membranous labyrinth) is typical extracellular fluid.
- **Hair cells** are mechanoreceptive epithelial cells with **stereocilia** (rows of increasing height) topped by one taller **kinocilium**. Bending stereocilia *toward* the kinocilium → mechanotransduction K⁺ channels open at the tips → K⁺ enters → depolarization → transmitter release → ↑ sensory afferent firing. Bending the opposite way → hyperpolarization → ↓ firing (Fig 10.14).
- **Macula** of utricle/saccule contains hair cells with stereocilia embedded in a gelatinous **otolith membrane** topped with calcium carbonate **otoliths** (oto = ear, lith = stone). The mass of the otoliths gives the membrane higher inertia than the surrounding endolymph — during linear acceleration, the membrane lags behind, bending the stereocilia. The **utricle** is more sensitive to horizontal acceleration; **saccule** to vertical.
- **Crista ampullaris** sits in the swollen base of each semicircular duct (the membranous ampulla). Hair cell stereocilia are embedded in the **cupula**, a gelatinous flap sealed to the duct walls (Fig 10.16). During angular acceleration, endolymph inertia displaces the cupula opposite to the rotation; this bends the hair cells.
- Sensory afferents form the vestibular branch of the **vestibulocochlear nerve (CN VIII)** → vestibular nuclei in medulla and cerebellum (Fig 10.17). Outputs:
  - **Oculomotor center** — vestibulo-ocular reflex (stable gaze during head movement).
  - **Spinal cord motor neurons** — body posture and head/neck/limb adjustments.
- **Nystagmus** — when spinning stops, endolymph inertia continues bending the cupula → eyes drift in the previous direction then jerk back. **Vertigo** — perceived spinning of self or environment. Pathologic vertigo arises from asymmetric vestibular firing (e.g., viral vestibular neuritis, Meniere's disease).
- **Meniere's disease.** Rotational vertigo, vestibular nystagmus, tinnitus, hearing loss. Cause: excess endolymph dilating and damaging the membranous labyrinth.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The vestibular apparatus consists of two parts: (1) the otolith organs, which include the utricle and saccule, and (2) the semicircular canals." (p. 278)
- > "Endolymph is unlike any other extracellular fluid: it has a higher K⁺ concentration (higher even than in the intracellular compartment) and much lower concentrations of Na⁺ and Ca²⁺." (p. 278)
- > "When the stereocilia are bent in the direction of the kinocilium, the plasma membrane is depressed and ion channels for K⁺ are opened, allowing K⁺ to passively enter and depolarize the hair cell." (p. 279)
- > "The otolith membrane contains microscopic crystals of calcium carbonate (otoliths) from which it derives its name." (pp. 279–280)
- > "The hair cells are embedded within a gelatinous membrane called the cupula, which projects into the endolymph." (p. 280)
- > "Vertigo as a result of spinning is a natural response of the vestibular apparatus." (p. 281)

## Section 10.5 — The Ears and Hearing (pp. 281–289)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Outer Ear** (p. 282)
- **Middle Ear** (pp. 282–283)
- **Cochlea** (pp. 283–285)
- **Spiral Organ (Organ of Corti)** (pp. 285–288)
  - **Neural Pathways for Hearing** (p. 287)
  - **Hearing Impairments** (pp. 288–289)

### Core claims
- **Sound** = alternating pressure waves in a medium. **Frequency** in Hz determines pitch; **intensity** in dB determines loudness (every 10 dB = 10× sound intensity; threshold of hearing = 0 dB; speech ≈ 60 dB → tympanic membrane vibrations of about one hydrogen-molecule diameter).
- Human range: **20–20,000 Hz**, distinguishable to 0.3% in frequency and 0.1–0.5 dB in intensity.
- **Outer ear:** pinna (auricle) + external auditory meatus, channeling sound to the **tympanic membrane**.
- **Middle ear:** air-filled cavity housing the three **ossicles** — malleus (hammer) → incus (anvil) → stapes (stirrup). Stapes footplate seats on the **oval window** of the cochlea. **Tensor tympani** and **stapedius muscles** dampen ossicle vibration in loud sounds (acoustic reflex); fast-onset sounds (gunshots) outpace this reflex → sensorineural damage. The **auditory (eustachian) tube** connects middle ear to nasopharynx, equalizing pressure.
- **Conduction deafness** — anything that hinders sound transmission to oval window: cerumen, foreign body, ruptured tympanic membrane, otitis media fluid, **otosclerosis** (bone overgrowth immobilizing the stapes).
- **Cochlea** (Latin: snail) — ~34 mm long bony spiral with three coiled fluid-filled compartments (Fig 10.20):
  - **Scala vestibuli** (upper, perilymph) starts at oval window.
  - **Scala tympani** (lower, perilymph) ends at the round window — which bulges into the middle ear to compensate for fluid incompressibility.
  - **Cochlear duct (scala media)** (middle, endolymph) — sits between the upper two; bounded above by the **vestibular (Reissner's) membrane** and below by the **basilar membrane**. The two perilymph chambers connect at the apex via the **helicotrema**.
- **Pressure waves at the oval window** push perilymph in scala vestibuli. Low-frequency waves travel all the way to the helicotrema and back via scala tympani; high-frequency waves bypass this loop by crossing through the vestibular and basilar membranes near the base of the cochlea.
- **Place coding (Békésy's traveling-wave theory).** Each frequency maximally vibrates a specific location on the basilar membrane — **high pitches near the base (next to the stapes)**, **low pitches near the apex (helicotrema)** (Fig 10.21).
- **Spiral organ (organ of Corti)** sits on the basilar membrane within the cochlear duct (Fig 10.22). Contains two hair-cell populations:
  - **Inner hair cells** (~3,500; one row) — the actual mechanosensors; stereocilia not directly attached to tectorial membrane; transduce basilar-membrane shear into K⁺ inflow → release **glutamate** onto cochlear afferents (each inner hair cell synapses with 6–20 sensory neurons of CN VIII spiral ganglion).
  - **Outer hair cells** (~11,000; three to five rows) — function as the **cochlear amplifier**; receive *motor* innervation from the olivary nuclei; depolarize → shorten, hyperpolarize → lengthen; mechanical length change amplifies basilar-membrane motion up to 1000-fold, sharpening pitch resolution.
- **Tectorial membrane** overhangs the spiral organ; basilar membrane displacement creates a shearing force between the membranes that bends the stereocilia of the outer hair cells (their tips are embedded in tectorial membrane) and indirectly the inner hair cells.
- **The endolymph of the cochlear duct uniquely has a +100 mV endocochlear potential**, creating an extraordinarily steep electrochemical drive for K⁺ entry into hair cells when their tip-link channels open. Stereocilia displacement of just 0.3 nm is detectable at threshold.
- **Neural pathway (Fig 10.24):** inner hair cell → spiral ganglion → CN VIII → **cochlear nuclei** (medulla/pons junction) → either **superior olivary complex** (binaural localization) or directly → **lateral lemniscus** → **inferior colliculus (midbrain)** — *all auditory paths synapse here* → **medial geniculate body** of thalamus → **auditory cortex** (temporal lobe). **Tonotopic organization** is preserved at every level (Fig 10.25).
- **Sound localization** uses **interaural intensity differences** (for frequencies >2000 Hz; lateral superior olive) and **interaural time differences** (for <2000 Hz; medial superior olive; resolves down to ~10 μs).
- **Hearing impairment types:**
  - **Conduction deafness** — impairs all frequencies; treatable by amplification (hearing aids) or surgery (e.g., stapes prosthesis for otosclerosis).
  - **Sensorineural deafness** — degenerated cochlear hair cells (outer hair cell loss → reduced sensitivity & frequency selectivity; inner hair cell loss → only cochlear implants help). Mammalian cochlear hair cells do not regenerate.
  - **Presbycusis** — age-related high-frequency hearing loss starting after age 20.

### Core claims
- **Visible light** is the 400–700 nm portion of the electromagnetic spectrum (Fig 10.26). UV is filtered by the yellow lens (people with the lens removed can see UV; honeybees can too); IR is felt as heat but lacks the energy to excite photoreceptors.
- **Eyeball architecture** (Table 10.4, Fig 10.27): three tunics from outside in:
  - **Fibrous tunic** = sclera (white, posterior; collagen) + cornea (anterior; transparent dense connective tissue).
  - **Vascular tunic (uvea)** = choroid (vascular, pigmented; posterior) + ciliary body (anterior; smooth muscle + glandular epithelium that secretes aqueous humor) + iris (anterior; pigment + smooth muscle, the diaphragm of the eye).
  - **Neural tunic (retina)** — innermost; photoreceptors + integrative neurons.
  - The **lens** is suspended between the iris and the vitreous chamber by zonular fibers attached to the ciliary body.
- **Pupil control** (Fig 10.28):
  - **Constriction** (parasympathetic, CN III oculomotor) by the circular muscle fibers of the iris.
  - **Dilation** (sympathetic, postganglionic from superior cervical ganglion) by the radial muscle fibers.
- **Aqueous humor** is secreted by the ciliary body into the posterior chamber, flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber, drains via the **scleral venous sinus (canal of Schlemm)** (Fig 10.29). Nourishes the avascular cornea and lens; maintains intraocular pressure (normal 10–21 mmHg).
- **Glaucoma** — damaged retinal ganglion cell axons (optic nerve fibers) usually correlated with high intraocular pressure. **Open-angle** (most common; impaired drainage despite open angle; painless; gradual peripheral vision loss). **Angle-closure** (sudden obstruction; emergency).
- **Vitreous humor** fills the chamber behind the lens.
- **Retina view (Fig 10.30):** Through an ophthalmoscope, the optic disc (where ganglion cell axons leave as the optic nerve and blood vessels enter/exit — the **blind spot**) and the **macula lutea** with its central **fovea centralis** are visible.
- **Refraction.** Light bends most at the air-cornea interface (refractive indices: air 1.00, cornea 1.38, aqueous humor 1.33, lens 1.40). The cornea provides most refraction; the lens provides **variable** fine focus.
- **Image is inverted and reversed on the retina** (Figs 10.31, 10.32). The right visual field is projected to the left half of *each* retina, and vice versa.
- **Accommodation** (Figs 10.33, 10.34):
  - **Distant vision (>20 ft):** ciliary muscle *relaxed* → zonular fibers tight → lens *flat* (low refractive power).
  - **Close vision:** ciliary muscle *contracts* → zonular fibers slack → lens *rounded* by inherent elasticity (high refractive power).
  - **Presbyopia** — loss of accommodation with age (>45) due to reduced lens flexibility.
- **Refractive errors** (Fig 10.35):
  - **Emmetropia** — normal.
  - **Myopia (nearsightedness)** — eyeball too long; image focuses *in front of* retina; corrected with concave (diverging) lens.
  - **Hyperopia (farsightedness)** — eyeball too short; image focuses *behind* retina; corrected with convex (converging) lens.
  - **Astigmatism** — uneven cornea or lens curvature; corrected with cylindrical lens.
- **LASIK (laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis)** — reshapes the cornea: reduces curvature for myopia, increases for hyperopia, restores symmetry for astigmatism. Cannot correct presbyopia.

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

- **Visual Acuity and Sensitivity** (pp. 302–303)
- **Neural Pathways from the Retina** (pp. 303–306)
  - **Neural Control of Eye Movements** (pp. 305–306)

### Core claims
- **Retinal architecture is inverted** (Fig 10.36): light passes through ganglion cells → amacrine cells → bipolar cells → horizontal cells → finally reaches the photoreceptor outer segments at the back. The pigment epithelium and choroid sit behind the receptors and absorb stray light.
- **Photoreceptor anatomy** (Fig 10.37): inner segment (organelles, nucleus, synaptic ending) + outer segment (stacked membranous discs containing photopigment). Discs are continuously made at the base and shed/phagocytosed at the tip (retinal pigment epithelial cells phagocytose ~10% of discs from each receptor per day — the most phagocytically active cells in the body).
- **Rhodopsin** in rods consists of **11-cis-retinal** (a vitamin-A derivative) bound to the protein **opsin**. Photoabsorption (max ~500 nm, blue-green) isomerizes retinal to **all-trans**, which dissociates from opsin — the **bleaching reaction** (Fig 10.38). All-trans-retinal is trafficked to the pigment epithelium for re-isomerization by **cis-trans isomerase** and returned to the photoreceptor — the **visual cycle of retinal**.
- **Dark current and phototransduction** (Figs 10.39, 10.40):
  - In the **dark**: cGMP holds open cGMP-gated Na⁺ channels in the outer segment → Na⁺ flows in continuously (the **dark current**) → photoreceptor is partially depolarized → continuously releases **inhibitory** transmitter at its synapse with bipolar cells → bipolar cells are hyperpolarized → do not stimulate ganglion cells.
  - In the **light**: photon → 11-cis → all-trans → opsin shape change → activates transducin (G-protein) → α subunit activates cGMP **phosphodiesterase** → cGMP → GMP → Na⁺ channels close → dark current stops → photoreceptor hyperpolarizes → stops releasing inhibitory transmitter → bipolar cells now fire glutamate onto ganglion cells → ganglion cells fire action potentials.
  - One photon can block over a million Na⁺ ions — massive amplification.
- **Dark adaptation:** light bleaches photopigments; sensitivity is low when first entering darkness. Over ~20 min, rhodopsin regenerates and other subtle changes yield up to a **100,000-fold sensitivity gain**.
- **Trichromatic color vision (cones).** Three cone types (Fig 10.41):
  - **S (blue) cones** — absorption max ~420 nm.
  - **M (green) cones** — ~530 nm.
  - **L (red) cones** — ~562 nm.
  - Each uses retinal bound to a distinct **photopsin** protein. M and L cone genes are on the X chromosome — red-green colorblindness affects ~8% of men, ~0.5% of women.
- **Photoreceptors, bipolars, horizontals all use graded potentials only** — only ganglion cells and amacrine cells fire all-or-none action potentials.
- **Fovea centralis** sits inside the macula lutea. Center of fovea contains *only cones* arranged 1:1 with bipolar cells and ganglion cells — maximum acuity. Each foveal ganglion cell sees an area ~one cone wide (~2 μm). Peripheral retina: many rods converge on one bipolar, many bipolars on one ganglion — high sensitivity to dim light but poor acuity. Total: ~120 million rods, ~6 million cones, ~1.2 million optic nerve axons (105:1 convergence ratio overall).
- **Averted vision:** dim stars disappear when fixated (light falls on rod-free fovea) but reappear when looked at obliquely (light falls on rod-rich periphery).
- **Visual pathway** (Fig 10.44):
  - **Geniculostriate system** (~70–80% of fibers): retinal ganglion cells → optic nerve → **optic chiasm** (nasal fibers cross, temporal fibers don't) → optic tract → **lateral geniculate body** of thalamus → striate cortex (V1; area 17) → visual association areas 18, 19. This is the "what" system.
  - **Tectal system** (~20–30%): retinal ganglion cells → superior colliculus of midbrain → motor output for eye and body movements; controls pupillary reflex and accommodation. This is the "where" system.
- **Eye movements:** saccades (high-velocity gaze shifts, 400–800°/s, target foveal); smooth pursuit (≤30°/s, tracking); vergence (30–150°/s, convergence/divergence for depth). **Fixational microsaccades** prevent retinal adaptation from bleaching during fixation.
- **Pupillary reflex.** Shining light into one eye constricts both pupils via superior-colliculus parasympathetic activation.
- **Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells** contain the pigment **melanopsin** and respond directly to luminance (without rods/cones). They drive non-image functions: the pupillary reflex, circadian entrainment (project to suprachiasmatic nucleus), and melatonin suppression.

## Section 10.8 — Neural Processing of Visual Information (pp. 306–309)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Ganglion Cell Receptive Fields** (pp. 306–307)
- **Lateral Geniculate Nuclei** (p. 307)
- **Cerebral Cortex** (pp. 307–309)

### Core claims
- **Ganglion cell receptive fields** are concentric — a center and an antagonistic surround (Fig 10.46). Two types:
  - **On-center / off-surround** — light in the center excites the cell; light in the surround inhibits.
  - **Off-center / on-surround** — opposite.
  - Diffuse light over both center and surround produces conflicting signals and a weaker response than a pinpoint. This is **lateral inhibition at the ganglion-cell level**, sharpening edges and contours.
- **Direction-selective ganglion cells** respond to moving edges in a preferred direction; >30 ganglion-cell types have been identified.
- **Color opponency.** Certain ganglion cells subtract activity between cone types: **L − M** (red vs. green) and **S − (L + M)** (blue vs. yellow) — explains color perception as a neural computation, not a literal one-cone-to-one-color mapping.
- **Lateral geniculate nucleus** layers preserve eye-of-origin information but each LGN neuron responds to only one eye. Its receptive fields are circular with antagonistic center/surround, like ganglion cells.
- **Striate (primary visual / V1) cortex** receives LGN input. Cortical neurons (Fig 10.47):
  - **Simple cells** — rectangular receptive fields; respond best to *bars or slits of light* at a specific position and orientation. Constructed from LGN inputs whose receptive fields lie along a line.
  - **Complex cells** — receive input from simple cells; respond to oriented bars *moving* in a preferred direction across a larger field.
  - **Hypercomplex cells** — respond to specific corners, angles, curves of specific length and orientation.
  - Areas 18 and 19 contain only complex and hypercomplex cells.

- > "A spot of light in the center of an 'on' receptive field stimulates the ganglion cell, whereas a spot of light in its surround inhibits the ganglion cell." (paraphrased from p. 306; explicit quote follows)
- > "The reason wide illumination of the retina has a weaker effect than pinpoint illumination is now clear; diffuse illumination gives the ganglion cell conflicting orders — on and off." (p. 306)
- > "The antagonistic center and surround of the receptive field of ganglion cells provide lateral inhibition, which enhances contours and provides better visual acuity." (p. 306)
- > "Simple cortical neurons are best stimulated by a slit or bar of light located in a precise part of the visual field (of either eye) at a precise orientation." (p. 307)
- > "Complex neurons receive input from simple cells, and hypercomplex neurons receive input from complex cells." (p. 307)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Sensory receptor** — structure that transduces a stimulus into electrical activity in a sensory neuron.
- **Adequate stimulus** — the modality requiring the least energy to activate a given receptor.
- **Law of specific nerve energies** — each sensory neuron evokes only one perceptual modality (its adequate stimulus), regardless of how it is stimulated.
- **Generator (receptor) potential** — graded depolarization of a sensory ending; frequency of action potentials encodes its amplitude.
- **Phasic / tonic receptor** — fast-adapting / slow-adapting.
- **Mechanoreceptor / chemoreceptor / photoreceptor / thermoreceptor / nociceptor** — receptor classes by adequate stimulus.
- **Proprioceptor** — muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ, joint receptor.
- **Free nerve ending** — unmyelinated cutaneous receptor for light touch, pain, temperature.
- **Pacinian (lamellated) corpuscle** — encapsulated deep-pressure / vibration receptor; phasic; up to 4 mm long.
- **Meissner's corpuscle** — encapsulated texture / vibration receptor in dermal papillae.
- **Merkel's disc** — sustained-touch receptor at the base of the epidermis.
- **Ruffini ending** — skin-stretch receptor.
- **Low-threshold mechanoreceptor (LTMR)** — touch ending of dorsal-root-ganglion sensory neuron.
- **TRP channel** — transient receptor potential family; TRPV1 = heat/capsaicin; cold/menthol receptor is another TRP.
- **Dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway** — touch and proprioception ascending tract; decussates in the medulla.
- **Spinothalamic (anterolateral) tract** — pain and temperature; decussates at spinal level.
- **Receptive field** — area of sensory surface whose stimulation alters a neuron's firing.
- **Two-point touch threshold** — minimum spacing for two touches to be felt as separate; smallest at fingertips.
- **Lateral inhibition** — neighboring sensory neurons suppress each other to sharpen sensation borders.
- **Referred pain** — visceral pain perceived in a somatic location due to convergence on spinal interneurons.
- **Phantom limb** — perception of sensation in an amputated limb.
- **Taste bud / taste cell** — neuroepithelial cluster of 50–100 cells with apical microvilli.
- **Salty, sour, sweet, umami, bitter** — the five taste modalities.
- **Gustducin** — G-protein of sweet/umami/bitter taste cells.
- **Olfactory sensory neuron** — bipolar neuron with ciliated dendrite in nasal epithelium; expresses one of ~380 receptor genes.
- **Glomerulus (olfactory)** — synaptic cluster in olfactory bulb collecting from one receptor type.
- **Mitral / tufted cell** — secondary olfactory neurons in the bulb.
- **Vestibular apparatus** — utricle + saccule + three semicircular canals.
- **Endolymph / perilymph** — high-K⁺ membranous-labyrinth fluid / normal-extracellular bony-labyrinth fluid.
- **Hair cell** — mechanoreceptive epithelial cell with stereocilia and (in vestibular system) a kinocilium.
- **Otolith / otolithic membrane** — calcium-carbonate crystals embedded in gelatinous matrix over hair cells of utricle/saccule.
- **Cupula** — gelatinous flap in the ampulla of a semicircular duct.
- **Crista ampullaris** — sensory ridge in the ampulla.
- **Vestibulocochlear nerve (CN VIII)** — sensory cranial nerve from vestibular apparatus and cochlea.
- **Nystagmus / vertigo** — oscillatory eye movements / illusion of spinning.
- **Cochlea** — coiled bony auditory organ; ~34 mm long.
- **Scala vestibuli / scala tympani / scala media (cochlear duct)** — three coiled cochlear compartments.
- **Tympanic membrane** — eardrum.
- **Ossicles** — malleus, incus, stapes.
- **Oval window / round window** — cochlear interfaces with middle ear.
- **Helicotrema** — apical connection between scala vestibuli and scala tympani.
- **Basilar membrane** — frequency-tuned membrane carrying the spiral organ; high pitches base, low pitches apex.
- **Spiral organ (organ of Corti)** — sensory structure with inner and outer hair cells.
- **Inner hair cell** — primary mechanosensory transducer; releases glutamate.
- **Outer hair cell** — cochlear amplifier; motor innervation from olivary nuclei.
- **Tectorial membrane** — gelatinous flap overlying spiral organ; bends stereocilia during basilar-membrane shear.
- **Tonotopic organization** — preserved frequency map from cochlea to auditory cortex.
- **Conduction deafness** — impaired external/middle ear transmission.
- **Sensorineural deafness** — cochlear hair cell or nerve damage.
- **Otosclerosis** — bony overgrowth fixing the stapes.
- **Presbycusis** — age-related high-frequency hearing loss.
- **Sclera / cornea / choroid / ciliary body / iris / retina** — eye tunics and structures.
- **Aqueous humor** — fluid in anterior/posterior chambers; secreted by ciliary body; drained via scleral venous sinus.
- **Vitreous humor** — jelly filling the posterior vitreous chamber.
- **Glaucoma** — optic nerve damage; usually elevated intraocular pressure.
- **Accommodation** — lens curvature change by ciliary muscle to focus near or far.
- **Presbyopia** — age-related loss of accommodation.
- **Myopia / hyperopia / astigmatism** — refractive errors corrected by concave / convex / cylindrical lenses.
- **LASIK** — laser corneal reshaping.
- **Rhodopsin** — rod pigment; opsin + 11-cis-retinal.
- **Transducin** — G-protein in photoreceptors.
- **cGMP / dark current** — open Na⁺ channels in dark; closed in light; light hyperpolarizes the photoreceptor.
- **Bipolar / horizontal / amacrine / ganglion cells** — retinal interneurons; only ganglion and amacrine cells fire APs.
- **Trichromatic vision** — three cone types (S/M/L).
- **Fovea centralis** — cone-only foveal pit, 1:1 cone:ganglion, highest acuity.
- **Macula lutea** — yellow area surrounding fovea.
- **Optic chiasm** — crossing of nasal-half-retina fibers.
- **Lateral geniculate nucleus** — thalamic relay for vision.
- **Striate (V1) cortex** — primary visual cortex in occipital lobe.
- **Simple / complex / hypercomplex cell** — cortical visual neurons differing in stimulus selectivity.
- **Tectal system** — retina → superior colliculus → eye movements & pupillary reflex; "where" pathway.
- **Geniculostriate system** — retina → LGN → V1; "what" pathway.
- **Melanopsin** — photopigment in intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells.
