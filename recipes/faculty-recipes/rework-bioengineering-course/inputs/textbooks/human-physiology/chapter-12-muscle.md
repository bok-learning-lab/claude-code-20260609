## Section 12.1 — Skeletal Muscles (pp. 359–363)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Structure of Skeletal Muscles** (pp. 359–360)
- **Motor End Plates and Motor Units** (pp. 360–363)

### Core claims
- A skeletal muscle is wrapped in nested connective tissue sheaths: **epimysium** (around the whole muscle), **perimysium** (around each **fascicle**), and **endomysium** (around each muscle fiber, also the muscle fiber's basement membrane). All three are continuous with the tendons, so contraction does not pull fibers out of the tendon.
- A **muscle fiber** is a single, multinucleated cell formed by fusion of embryonic **myoblasts** (a **syncytium**). Each fiber contains **myofibrils** (~1 μm diameter) running end-to-end; myofibrils contain **myofilaments** of actin and myosin.
- The light-microscope striations are produced by alternating dark **A bands** (anisotropic, thick filaments) and light **I bands** (isotropic, thin filaments), with thin dark **Z lines** at the center of each I band.
- **Skeletal muscle actions (Table 12.1):** extensor / flexor / abductor / adductor / levator / depressor / rotator / sphincter. The **agonist** is the prime mover; antagonistic muscles act on the same joint in opposite directions; **synergists** facilitate the agonist.
- The **plasma membrane** of a muscle fiber is the **sarcolemma**, the cytoplasm is the **sarcoplasm**, and the muscle stem cell is the **satellite cell** (between sarcolemma and endomysium).
- **Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD)** is an X-linked recessive disease (1 in 3,500 boys; symptoms by age 3–5). Mutations disable **dystrophin**, the protein that bridges the cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix beneath the sarcolemma; activity-induced sarcolemmal damage cannot be repaired, leading to necrosis and fibrofatty replacement. The dystrophin gene contains 79 exons and harbors >7,000 known mutations; CRISPR-Cas9 therapies are being investigated.
- The **neuromuscular junction** is the synapse between a somatic motor neuron's terminal bouton and the **motor end plate** (a specialized sarcolemma region with deep folds). Crests of the folds carry **ACh receptors**; troughs carry voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
- ACh binding opens ligand-gated channels; net Na⁺ inflow produces an **end-plate potential** that triggers an action potential propagating along the sarcolemma and into the T-tubules.
- A **motor unit** is one somatic motor neuron plus all the muscle fibers it innervates. The **innervation ratio** ranges from ~1:23 (extraocular, for fine control) to ~1:1,000–1:2,000 (gastrocnemius, for power).
- **Recruitment of motor units:** smaller motor units fire first; with increasing demand, larger motor units are added — known as the **size principle**. Smooth sustained contractions in vivo arise from **asynchronous activation** of multiple motor units.
- **Botulinum toxin (Botox)** is produced by *Clostridium botulinum*; it cleaves the SNARE protein SNAP-25, preventing ACh exocytosis at the neuromuscular junction. It is used to treat strabismus and muscle spasms and (cosmetically) to flatten skin wrinkles by paralyzing facial muscles.

## Section 12.2 — Mechanisms of Contraction (pp. 363–373)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

- **Regulation of Contraction** (pp. 368–373)
  - **Role of Ca²⁺ in Muscle Contraction** (pp. 369–370)
  - **Excitation-Contraction Coupling** (pp. 370–372)
  - **Muscle Relaxation** (p. 372)

### Core claims
- A **sarcomere** is the basic contractile unit from one Z disc to the next. In a relaxed sarcomere: I band = thin filaments only (light); A band = thick filaments overlapping thin at the edges (dark); H band = thick filaments only at the center of the A band (lighter); M line = protein scaffold at the middle of the A band that anchors thick filaments together.
- **Titin** is the largest protein in the human body (>1 μm). Each titin spans half a sarcomere from a Z disc through the I band and into the thick filament, ending at the M line. The springlike I-band domain refolds during contraction and unfolds under stretch, contributing **passive tension** and elastic recoil. Two other giant proteins act as scaffolding: **nebulin** (along thin filaments) and **obscurin** (around sarcomere periphery).
- **Sliding filament theory (Table 12.2):**
  1. The muscle shortens by movement of its insertion toward its origin.
  2. Myofibrils shorten because **sarcomeres** shorten — Z-to-Z distance decreases.
  3. **Filament lengths do not change.** Thin filaments slide over thick filaments.
  4. Power strokes by myosin cross bridges pull thin filaments toward the sarcomere center.
  5. **A bands stay the same length;** I bands and H bands shorten.
- **Cross-bridge cycle (Fig 12.12):** at rest, the myosin head carries ADP·Pᵢ (cocked). The cycle:
  1. Resting fiber: cocked myosin head is detached from actin (with ADP and Pᵢ bound).
  2. Myosin head binds to actin → **cross bridge** forms.
  3. **Pᵢ released** → conformational change in myosin.
  4. **Power stroke** pulls actin ~6 nm toward the M line; ADP is then released.
  5. A new **ATP** binds the myosin head → cross bridge releases from actin (rigor state ends).
  6. ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP + Pᵢ → head re-cocks to step 1.
- **Rigor mortis** is the failure of step 5 — dead cells lack ATP, so myosin heads cannot release from actin. (Also, SERCA pumps fail, so Ca²⁺ stays high.) Rigor resolves as muscle proteins degrade.
- **Power stroke geometry:** one power stroke moves actin ~6 nm; muscles can shorten up to ~60% of resting length; the cycle must repeat many times. Force is produced by the **number of cross bridges engaged**, not by stroke amplitude — power strokes are asynchronous (tug-of-war analogy).
- **Troponin–tropomyosin Ca²⁺ switch (Fig 12.13, 12.14):** the actin (F-actin) helix carries **tropomyosin** in its groove, which physically blocks myosin binding sites at rest. **Troponin** has three subunits: **troponin I** (inhibits cross-bridge binding), **troponin T** (binds tropomyosin), **troponin C** (binds Ca²⁺). When Ca²⁺ binds troponin C, the troponin–tropomyosin complex shifts and exposes the myosin-binding sites.
- **Excitation–contraction coupling (Fig 12.16, 12.17):**
  1. ACh at the motor end plate triggers an end-plate potential → action potential along the sarcolemma.
  2. The action potential propagates into **transverse (T) tubules** — invaginations of the sarcolemma that bring the action potential deep into the fiber.
  3. T-tubule **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (dihydropyridine, DHP, receptors)** undergo a conformational change.
  4. DHP receptors are **mechanically coupled** to **Ca²⁺ release channels (ryanodine receptors, RyR)** in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). Their conformational change opens the RyR.
  5. Ca²⁺ floods out of the SR (resting [Ca²⁺]ᵢ ≪ 10⁻⁶ M → quickly rises above 10⁻⁶ M) and binds troponin C → contraction.
  6. The SR also has **Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release** channels (more important in cardiac muscle).
- **Skeletal muscle E-C coupling is electromechanical:** DHP and RyR are physically linked, so SR Ca²⁺ release does not require extracellular Ca²⁺ entry.
- **Muscle relaxation** requires (1) cessation of action potentials → RyR closes; (2) **SERCA pumps** (sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase) actively transport Ca²⁺ back into the SR. SERCA pumping requires ATP — **ATP is needed for relaxation as well as contraction.**

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Shortening of the sarcomeres is produced, not by shortening of the filaments, but rather by the sliding of thin filaments over and between the thick filaments." (p. 366)
- > "The I bands (containing only thin filaments) and H bands (containing only thick filaments) thus get shorter during contraction." (p. 366)
- > "A single cross-bridge power stroke pulls the actin filament a distance of 6 nanometers (6 nm)." (p. 368)
- > "Note that the splitting of ATP is required before a cross bridge can attach to actin and undergo a power stroke, and that the attachment of a new ATP is needed for the cross bridge to release from actin at the end of a power stroke." (p. 368)
- > "In a relaxed muscle, the position of the tropomyosin in the thin filaments is such that it physically blocks the myosin heads from bonding to specific attachment sites in the actin." (p. 368)
- > "Muscle contraction is turned on when sufficient amounts of Ca²⁺ bind to troponin. This occurs when the Ca²⁺ concentration of the sarcoplasm rises above 10⁻⁶ molar." (p. 370)
- > "There is a direct molecular coupling between these channels on the transverse tubules and the calcium release channels (ryanodine receptors) in the sarcoplasmic reticulum." (p. 372)
- > "Because active transport pumps are powered by the hydrolysis of ATP, ATP is required for muscle relaxation as well as for muscle contraction." (p. 372)

### Equations / quantitative
**Threshold sarcoplasmic Ca²⁺ for contraction:**
$$[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]_{\mathrm{sarcoplasm}} > 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{M}$$

**Cross-bridge ATPase reaction (myosin head):**
$$\mathrm{Myosin\text{-}ATP} \to \mathrm{Myosin\text{-}ADP\text{-}P_i^{*}} \xrightarrow{\text{binds actin}} \mathrm{Actomyosin\text{-}ADP\text{-}P_i} \xrightarrow{-P_i} \mathrm{Actomyosin\text{-}ADP} \xrightarrow{\text{power stroke}} \mathrm{Actomyosin} + \mathrm{ADP}$$

**Power-stroke displacement per cycle:** ~6 nm; muscle can shorten up to ~60% of resting length, so ~10⁵ cycles per shortening event per myosin head.

#### Figure 12.12 — The cross-bridge cycle *(viewed)*

A six-step circular cycle of cartoon panels around a small icon of an intact muscle. The thick filament is shown in purple at the bottom of each panel; the thin filament (actin in green with associated proteins) sits above. **Step 1 (top center, "Resting fiber"):** the myosin head is detached from actin and carries **ADP** and **Pᵢ** (small pink and yellow balls); the head is "cocked." **Step 2:** the myosin head has bound actin → **cross bridge formed**, still carrying ADP and Pᵢ. **Step 3:** **Pᵢ is released** from the head, causing a conformational change in myosin. **Step 4:** **Power stroke** — the myosin head pivots, sliding the thin filament leftward; **ADP is released** at the end of the stroke. **Step 5:** a new **ATP** (yellow ball) binds the myosin head, allowing it to release from actin. **Step 6:** ATP is hydrolyzed; phosphate re-binds myosin, re-cocking the energized head to its original orientation — return to step 1. **Top half (Fig 12.15):** a 3D cutaway of a single muscle fiber's interior. The **sarcolemma** is at the surface; running parallel into the depth are **myofibrils** (red-pink rods showing A bands and I bands). > Vision note: This pair anchors the canonical triad architecture and the electromechanical-coupling mechanism that distinguishes skeletal muscle from cardiac muscle (which uses calcium-induced calcium release, Fig 12.34). Key contrast for any cardiac-vs-skeletal E-C coupling question.

#### Figure 12.21 — The length–tension relationship in skeletal muscles *(viewed)*

A line graph (top): x-axis is **percentage of resting length** (60 → 160); y-axis is **relative tension** (0 → 1.0). ## Section 12.3 — Contractions of Skeletal Muscles (pp. 373–376)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Twitch, Summation, and Tetanus** (pp. 373–374)
  - **Treppe** (p. 374)
- **Types of Muscle Contractions** (pp. 374–375)
- **Series-Elastic Component** (p. 375)
- **Length-Tension Relationship** (pp. 375–376)

### Core claims
- A **twitch** is a single rapid contraction–relaxation cycle in response to one above-threshold stimulus. Latent period of ~2 ms precedes mechanical response; the twitch itself can last 10–100 ms depending on the muscle.
- **Summation**: a second stimulus arriving before complete relaxation produces a larger contraction than the first.
- **Incomplete tetanus**: rapid summation produces a sustained but oscillating contraction (e.g., 5–10 shocks/s).
- **Complete tetanus**: at a "fusion frequency" (e.g., 60 shocks/s) twitches fuse to produce a smooth, sustained contraction. With continued stimulation, the muscle eventually fatigues.
- **In vivo**, smooth contractions arise not from very-high-frequency firing but from **asynchronous activation of motor units**.
- **Treppe (staircase)**: at maximal-voltage stimulation of a fresh muscle, successive twitches grow stronger before plateauing — attributed to a build-up of intracellular Ca²⁺ ("warm-up").
- **Contraction types:**
  - **Isometric** ("same length"): muscle generates tension but does not shorten (load equals or exceeds force).
  - **Isotonic** ("same tension"): muscle shortens against a constant load.
  - **Concentric** (shortening): tension exceeds load and the muscle shortens.
  - **Eccentric** (lengthening): the muscle lengthens despite contracting because the load exceeds tension (e.g., lowering a dumbbell, walking downhill).
- **Series-elastic component:** tendons, connective tissue, and titin must be pulled tight before muscle shortening can move the insertion. The series elastic component absorbs and stores elastic energy and explains why a single twitch generates less force than tetanus (slack must first be taken up). Elastic recoil of the chest wall after inspiration is a major driver of normal (passive) expiration (Ch 16).
- **Force–velocity curve (Fig 12.20):** as the load opposing contraction increases, the velocity of shortening decreases hyperbolically. At zero load, velocity is maximum (V_max — extrapolated). At sufficient load, velocity = 0 (isometric contraction).
- **Length–tension relationship (Fig 12.21):** maximum tension occurs at sarcomere length 2.0–2.25 μm (the body's normal resting length, defended by stretch reflexes). Tension falls to zero at 3.65 μm (no thick-thin overlap) and at 1.25 μm (filaments collide with Z discs and double-overlap interferes with cross bridges). This is the cellular basis of the Frank-Starling mechanism in cardiac muscle (Ch 14).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "A second shock delivered to the muscle before it has had a chance to relax fully from its first twitch results in a second twitch that summates with the first to produce a stronger contraction." (p. 373)
- > "This smooth, sustained contraction is called **complete tetanus**." (p. 373)
- > "Our muscles normally contract in vivo with complete tetanus, where the contraction is sustained by complete fusion of twitches… we do this by the asynchronous activation of motor units." (p. 374)
- > "When the force opposing contraction (the load) becomes sufficiently great, the muscle is unable to shorten when it exerts a given tension. That is, its velocity of shortening is zero. At this point… the contraction is called an **isometric** (literally, 'same length') contraction." (p. 375)
- > "When a force exerted on a muscle to stretch it is greater than the force of muscle contraction, the muscle will be stretched by that force. In other words, the muscle will lengthen despite its contraction. This is known as an **eccentric** (or lengthening) contraction." (p. 375)
- > "This tension is maximal when the sarcomeres are at a length of 2.0 to 2.25 μm." (p. 375)
- > "When the sarcomeres reach a length of about 3.6 μm, there is no overlap of thick and thin filaments, and no interactions can occur between myosin and actin." (p. 376)

### Equations / quantitative

**Force-velocity hyperbola (Hill equation form, conceptual):**
$$(F + a)(v + b) = (F_0 + a)\,b$$

where $F$ is the load, $v$ is shortening velocity, $F_0$ is the isometric tension at $v = 0$, and $a, b$ are empirical constants; the curve passes through $v = V_{\max}$ at $F = 0$ and through $v = 0$ at $F = F_0$.

**Length–tension optimum:**
$$T_{\max}\ \text{at sarcomere length } 2.0\ \mu\mathrm{m} \le L_s \le 2.25\ \mu\mathrm{m}$$

**Tension limits:** $T = 0$ at $L_s = 3.65$ μm (no overlap) and $L_s = 1.25$ μm (Z-disc collision).

## Section 12.4 — Energy Requirements of Skeletal Muscles (pp. 376–383)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Metabolism of Skeletal Muscles** (pp. 377–379)
  - **Maximal Oxygen Uptake and Lactate Threshold** (pp. 377–378)
  - **Energy Usage During Exercise** (pp. 378–379)
  - **Oxygen Debt** (p. 378)
  - **Phosphocreatine** (pp. 378–379)
- **Slow- and Fast-Twitch Fibers** (pp. 379–381)
- **Muscle Fatigue** (p. 381)
- **Adaptations of Muscles to Exercise Training** (pp. 381–383)
- **Muscle Damage and Repair** (p. 383)

### Core claims
- ~70% of muscle ATP fuels **myosin ATPase** (cross-bridge cycling); the rest goes to (1) SR SERCA pumping for relaxation and (2) Na⁺/K⁺ pumps to restore the resting potential after each action potential.
- **Resting muscle** burns fatty acids aerobically. **Exercising muscle** progressively recruits muscle glycogen, blood glucose, and stored intramuscular triglyceride, with the mix shifting with intensity and duration (Fig 12.22).
- **GLUT4 insertion** into the sarcolemma is driven by both **muscle contraction itself** and **insulin** — via different signaling paths. Contraction-stimulated GLUT4 insertion happens primarily in the T-tubules (largest surface area). The two stimuli are **additive**, which is why exercise helps control type 2 diabetes (Ch 19).
- **Phosphocreatine (creatine phosphate)** is the rapid-buffer high-energy phosphate reserve, present at ~3× the cell's [ATP]. The enzyme **creatine kinase (CK)** catalyzes:
  $$\mathrm{Phosphocreatine} + \mathrm{ADP} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Creatine} + \mathrm{ATP}$$
  During exercise the forward direction predominates; at rest the reverse replenishes the reserve. Even during heavy exercise, [ATP] falls only slightly in aerobically adapted muscle.
- **Creatine kinase isoenzymes** are clinically used: **CK-MM** is released by damaged skeletal muscle (e.g., rhabdomyolysis); **CK-MB** by damaged cardiac muscle (myocardial infarction). Modern troponin assays (cardiac troponin T or I) are even more heart-specific than CK-MB.
- **Maximal oxygen uptake (V̇O₂ max, aerobic capacity)** is the peak rate of O₂ consumption during aerobic exercise. It ranges from ~12 mL O₂·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ in older sedentary people to ~84 mL O₂·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ in elite young male athletes (females average ~15–20% lower; training can increase V̇O₂ max by ~20%).
- **Lactate (anaerobic) threshold** = the percentage of V̇O₂ max at which blood lactate begins to rise sharply (~50–70% for average people; up to 80% for trained athletes).
- **Energy mix during exercise:**
  - **Light exercise (~25% V̇O₂ max):** mostly fatty-acid oxidation, from adipose and intramuscular triglyceride.
  - **Moderate exercise (50–70% V̇O₂ max):** roughly equal contributions of fatty acids and glucose (muscle glycogen + blood plasma).
  - **Heavy exercise (>70% V̇O₂ max):** glucose (predominantly muscle glycogen) supplies ~2/3 of energy.
- **Oxygen debt (EPOC):** the elevated post-exercise O₂ consumption used to oxidize lactate (Cori cycle to liver), replenish phosphocreatine, restock myoglobin O₂, and pay back O₂ withdrawn from hemoglobin.
- **Three human muscle fiber types (Table 12.3):**
  - **Slow oxidative (type I, red):** small diameter, high myoglobin/capillaries/mitochondria, aerobic, fatigue-resistant, used most often.
  - **Fast oxidative-glycolytic (type IIA, red):** intermediate diameter, both aerobic and glycolytic capacity, fast contracting.
  - **Fast glycolytic (type IIX, white):** large diameter, low myoglobin/capillaries, anaerobic, fast and powerful but fatigue rapidly.
- A given motor neuron innervates fibers of one type only. Recruitment order is type I → IIA → IIX.
- **Genetic variation in fiber composition is large:** ~20% type I in elite sprinters to ~95% type I in elite marathoners (Fig 12.26). Training can shift type IIX toward type IIA.
- **Muscle fatigue causes:**
  1. **Extracellular K⁺ accumulation** in the T-tubules (from repeated action potential repolarization) lowers the resting membrane potential and reduces excitability — dominant cause in sustained maximal contraction.
  2. **Decline in ATP** at T-tubule/SR junctions impairs SERCA pumps.
  3. **Muscle glycogen depletion** appears to decrease SR Ca²⁺ release.
  4. **Increased Pᵢ** (from phosphocreatine breakdown) reduces cross-bridge force.
  5. **Increased cytoplasmic ADP** slows shortening velocity.
  6. **Central fatigue:** reduced descending drive from upper motor neurons — explains why voluntary force often falls before peripheral muscle exhaustion.
- **Endurance training (Table 12.4):** increases mitochondrial number/size, capillary density, myoglobin content, fatty-acid oxidation, intramuscular triglyceride; decreases type IIX, increases type IIA; raises V̇O₂ max ~20%; lowers lactate produced at any given workload.
- **Resistance training:** causes **hypertrophy** (increased cell size) of type II fibers via myofibril thickening, sarcomere addition, and myofibril splitting. Satellite cells fuse into hypertrophying fibers, donating extra nuclei.
- **Satellite cells** (muscle stem cells) are normally quiescent. After injury or in response to resistance training, they differentiate into myoblasts and fuse with existing muscle fibers (or each other) to regenerate fibers. With advanced age they enter an **irreversible senescent state**, contributing to **sarcopenia** — average humans lose ~45% of muscle mass between their 20s and 80s. Exercise partially reverses this.
- **Myostatin** is an autocrine cytokine that suppresses muscle growth; myostatin-knockout mice and cattle are massively muscular.
- **Myokines** are cytokines secreted by exercising muscle. **Irisin** promotes the "browning" of white adipose tissue (Ch 19), increasing metabolic rate, and stimulates the brain to produce **BDNF**. **Adipostatin** drives production of insulin-sensitive immature adipocytes. Myokines have an anti-inflammatory effect that helps protect against cardiovascular disease.

### Subsection headings (verbatim)

### Core claims
- **Lower motor neurons** (somatic motor neurons in brain stem and spinal cord ventral horn) are the **final common pathway** for skeletal muscle control. **Upper motor neurons** are CNS interneurons that influence them via descending tracts (pyramidal and extrapyramidal).
- **Muscle spindles** are intrafusal muscle fibers wrapped in a connective tissue capsule, arranged **in parallel** with the surrounding extrafusal (ordinary) fibers. They are most dense in muscles requiring fine control (e.g., hand muscles) and provide the major **proprioceptive** input.
- Each spindle contains two intrafusal-fiber subtypes: **nuclear bag fibers** (nuclei clumped centrally) and **nuclear chain fibers** (nuclei in a row). Sensory innervation:
  - **Primary (anulospiral) endings** wrap around the central regions; respond to **velocity** of stretch (phasic).
  - **Secondary (flower-spray) endings** at the contractile poles; respond to **sustained stretch** (tonic).
- A sudden stretch activates both endings; a slow stretch primarily activates secondary endings.
- **Two lower motor neuron classes:**
  - **Alpha motor neurons** innervate extrafusal fibers (the force-producing fibers); 60–90 m/s conduction.
  - **Gamma motor neurons** innervate the contractile poles of intrafusal fibers — keep the spindle tight even when the muscle shortens; 10–40 m/s conduction.
- **Coactivation:** during voluntary contraction, upper motor neurons stimulate alpha and gamma motor neurons simultaneously, so that the spindle continues to detect length changes even while the muscle shortens (otherwise the spindle would go slack).
- **Monosynaptic stretch reflex (knee-jerk, Fig 12.28):**
  1. Tap on patellar tendon stretches quadriceps and its spindles.
  2. Stretch activates primary endings → action potentials in Ia sensory fibers.
  3. Sensory fibers enter via dorsal root and **monosynapse** with alpha motor neurons in the ventral horn.
  4. Alpha motor neuron action potentials travel through the ventral root to the quadriceps.
  5. ACh release at the motor end plate triggers quadriceps contraction → knee extends → spindle stretch is relieved (negative feedback closes the loop).
- **Golgi tendon organs (Fig 12.29)** monitor tendon tension. Their afferents synapse on inhibitory interneurons → **disynaptic** reflex that **inhibits** the alpha motor neuron of the contracting muscle. Protects against excessive tendon tension.
- **Reciprocal innervation (Fig 12.30):** when the agonist is stimulated by the stretch reflex, an inhibitory interneuron simultaneously inhibits the alpha motor neuron of the antagonist, so the antagonist relaxes.
- **Crossed-extensor reflex (Fig 12.31):** stepping on a tack → flexion + reciprocal extensor inhibition in the injured leg AND simultaneous extension + flexor inhibition in the contralateral leg (to support body weight) — **double reciprocal innervation**.
- **Upper motor neuron tracts:** the **pyramidal tracts** (lateral and ventral corticospinal) originate in the precentral gyrus and decussate at the medullary pyramids. The major **extrapyramidal tract** is the reticulospinal tract (from medulla/pons reticular formation). The cerebellum has no direct descending tract — all its output is inhibitory, modulating motor activity via the vestibular nuclei, red nucleus, and basal nuclei.
- **Basal nuclei** (caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus, with thalamus, subthalamus, substantia nigra, red nucleus) inhibit lower motor neurons (via the reticular formation). Damage → akinesia, chorea, resting tremor. **Parkinson's disease** is degeneration of dopaminergic axons from the substantia nigra → resting tremor.
- **Upper motor neuron damage symptoms (Table 12.7):** Babinski reflex, spastic paralysis, hemiplegia, paraplegia, quadriplegia, chorea, resting tremor (basal nuclei), intention tremor (cerebellum).
- **ALS (Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis, Lou Gehrig's disease):** degeneration of BOTH lower motor neurons (atrophy) and upper motor neurons (sclerosis of lateral corticospinal tracts). Progressive muscle weakness and spastic paralysis; death usually from respiratory failure within ~5 years. ~20% of familial cases involve SOD1 mutations (superoxide dismutase).

## Section 12.6 — Cardiac and Smooth Muscles (pp. 390–397)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Cardiac Muscle** (pp. 391–392)
- **Smooth Muscle** (pp. 392–396)
  - **Single-Unit and Multiunit Smooth Muscles** (pp. 393–394)
  - **Autonomic Innervation of Smooth Muscles** (p. 394)
  - **Excitation-Contraction Coupling in Smooth Muscles** (pp. 394–396)

### Core claims
- **Cardiac muscle** is striated and uses sliding-filament contraction, but myocardial cells are short, branched, and joined end-to-end by **intercalated discs** containing **gap junctions** and desmosomes. Gap junctions allow action potentials to spread cell-to-cell, so an entire myocardium contracts as a **functional syncytium** ("wholehearted" contraction; the heart cannot grade contraction by recruiting fibers — see Ch 14 for grading by Frank–Starling and inotropy).
- Cardiac action potentials originate spontaneously in **pacemaker cells**; rate is modulated by the autonomic nervous system (Ch 14).
- **Cardiac E-C coupling (Fig 12.34) — calcium-induced calcium release (CICR):** action potentials in T-tubules open T-tubule **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels** → a small Ca²⁺ influx from extracellular fluid → triggers nearby **Ca²⁺ release channels** in the SR to open (these RyRs are 10× larger than the voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels) → bulk Ca²⁺ flood from SR → troponin binding → contraction. Unlike skeletal muscle, the T-tubule channel and the SR release channel are NOT mechanically coupled, and **extracellular Ca²⁺ entry is required**.
- **Cardiac troponins** (cardiac-specific isoforms of troponin T or troponin I) released into blood after myocardial cell death are the most cardiac-specific clinical markers of myocardial infarction — even more specific than CK-MB.
- **Smooth muscle** lacks sarcomeres but contains a great deal of actin and some myosin (thin:thick ratio ~16:1 vs. 2:1 in striated muscle). Thin filaments attach to **dense bodies** (analogous to Z discs) and to the plasma membrane; myosin proteins are stacked perpendicular to the thick-filament long axis, so myosin heads can interact with actin along the entire thick filament. This architecture lets smooth muscle generate force across enormous length ranges (urinary bladder: ~2.5× resting length; uterus: ~8× by term).
- **Single-unit smooth muscle** (most viscera: digestive tract, uterus) has many gap junctions; cells behave electrically as a unit. Displays **pacemaker activity** and **myogenic contraction** in response to stretch (autonomous of nerves).
- **Multiunit smooth muscle** (arrector pili, ciliary muscle of the eye) has few or no gap junctions; each cell must be neurally activated.
- **Varicosities and en passant synapses:** autonomic axons have bead-like swellings (varicosities) that release neurotransmitter along a stretch of the axon, reaching many smooth muscle cells. The entire smooth-muscle plasma membrane bears receptors.
- **Smooth muscle E-C coupling (Fig 12.37):**
  1. Depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in the sarcolemma (graded — more depolarization, more Ca²⁺, stronger contraction).
  2. Ca²⁺ also enters from the SR (less developed than in striated muscle), triggered either by CICR or by **IP₃** generated from hormone-activated plasma membrane signaling (Ch 11).
  3. Cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ binds **calmodulin** (the smooth-muscle analog of troponin).
  4. Calmodulin–Ca²⁺ activates **myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK)**, which phosphorylates myosin light chains → cross-bridge cycling can occur.
  5. Relaxation: **myosin light-chain phosphatase (MLCP)** dephosphorylates myosin → cross bridges inactivate.
- **Latch state:** smooth muscle cross bridges can remain attached at high force with very slow cycling — maintaining tone in vascular walls and sphincters with minimal ATP cost.
- **Calcium-channel blockers** target voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels of cardiac and vascular smooth muscle.
  - **Dihydropyridines** (e.g., nifedipine): preferentially vascular → vasodilation → lower BP.
  - **Verapamil:** preferentially cardiac → treats angina, arrhythmias.
  - **Diltiazem (Cardizem):** does both — lowers heart rate AND vasodilates.

- > "Because all cells in a myocardium are electrically joined, a myocardium behaves as a single functional unit." (p. 391)
- > "Measurement of cardiac-specific troponin T or troponin I is now the preferred blood test for detecting myocardial infarction (MI, or 'heart attack')." (p. 391)

- **Clinical Investigation: Mia** — Botox injections for cosmetic purposes; nocturnal leg cramps (low blood Ca²⁺ → increased nerve excitability); chest pain & palpitations with normal CK-MB and troponin T (excluding MI); diagnosed with hypertension and prescribed a calcium-channel blocker (likely diltiazem/Cardizem — slows heart and vasodilates).
- **Clinical Application — Duchenne muscular dystrophy** (p. 359): X-linked recessive; dystrophin gene (79 exons, on Xp21); >7,000 mutations identified; activity-induced sarcolemmal damage, fibrofatty replacement; CRISPR-Cas9 therapies under investigation.
- **Clinical Application — Botulinum toxin** (p. 362): cleaves SNAP-25, blocks ACh exocytosis; therapeutic uses include strabismus, spasms, and cosmetic wrinkle treatment.
- **Clinical Application — Rigor mortis** (p. 369): post-mortem failure of step 5 of the cross-bridge cycle (no new ATP → myosin heads cannot release from actin). Also fails to pump Ca²⁺ back into SR.
- **Clinical Application — Creatine kinase (CK / CPK)** (p. 379): CK-MM in skeletal muscle damage (rhabdomyolysis), CK-MB in MI. Cardiac troponin assays are even more specific.
- **Clinical Application — ALS (Lou Gehrig's disease)** (p. 384): both lower and upper motor neuron degeneration; ~20% of familial cases involve SOD1 mutations; death in ~5 years from respiratory failure.
- **Clinical Application — Calcium-channel blockers** (p. 396): dihydropyridines (vascular smooth muscle → BP control); verapamil (cardiac → angina/arrhythmia); diltiazem (both).
- **Lifestyle Application — Active lifestyle benefits** (p. 382): cognitive and cardiovascular benefits via exercise-induced GLUT4 insertion and myokine release.
- **Lifestyle Application — Muscle atrophy in microgravity** (p. 383): disuse atrophy in astronauts; possible role of altered glucocorticoids/GH.
- **Exercise Application — Stretching** (p. 386): slow stretches preferentially activate secondary (tonic) spindle endings, minimizing reflex spasm; "bouncing" rapid stretches activate primary endings and provoke painful stretch reflexes.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Sarcomere** — Z-to-Z segment of a myofibril; basic contractile unit; ~2 μm at rest.
- **A band / I band / H band / Z disc / M line** — banding pattern: A = thick + overlap; I = thin only; H = thick only central; Z = thin-filament anchor; M = thick-filament anchor.
- **Thick filament / myosin** — bipolar bundle of myosin molecules with projecting heads.
- **Thin filament / F-actin / G-actin** — helical polymer of globular G-actin; carries tropomyosin and troponin.
- **Tropomyosin** — rod-like protein in the actin groove that blocks myosin binding at rest.
- **Troponin (I, T, C)** — Ca²⁺-sensitive switch: I inhibits cross-bridge, T binds tropomyosin, C binds Ca²⁺.
- **Titin / nebulin / obscurin** — giant scaffolding proteins: titin (sarcomeric spring), nebulin (along actin), obscurin (sarcomere periphery).
- **Cross-bridge cycle** — ATP-driven cyclic attachment, power stroke, detachment of myosin heads on actin.
- **Power stroke** — ~6 nm pivoting of the myosin head after Pᵢ release, sliding actin toward the M line.
- **Rigor state / rigor mortis** — tight actin–myosin binding in the absence of ATP.
- **Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)** — modified SER storing Ca²⁺; terminal cisternae flank T-tubules.
- **Transverse tubule (T-tubule)** — sarcolemmal invagination conducting action potentials into the fiber.
- **Triad** — T-tubule + two flanking SR terminal cisternae.
- **DHP receptor / dihydropyridine receptor** — T-tubule voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel; mechanically coupled to the SR RyR in skeletal muscle.
- **Ryanodine receptor (RyR)** — SR Ca²⁺-release channel.
- **SERCA** — SR/ER Ca²⁺-ATPase that pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR for relaxation.
- **Calcium-induced calcium release (CICR)** — Ca²⁺ entry through T-tubule channels triggers SR Ca²⁺ release; dominant in cardiac muscle.
- **Motor unit** — one motor neuron + all the fibers it innervates.
- **Innervation ratio** — motor-neuron-to-muscle-fiber ratio; ~1:23 (extraocular) → 1:1,000–2,000 (gastrocnemius).
- **Recruitment / size principle** — smaller motor units fire first; larger units recruited for greater force.
- **End-plate potential** — graded depolarization at the motor end plate produced by ACh.
- **Twitch / summation / tetanus / treppe** — single contraction / overlapping twitches / fused contraction / staircase warm-up.
- **Isometric / isotonic / concentric / eccentric** — same length, no shortening / same tension, shortening / shortening / lengthening despite contraction.
- **V_max / isometric tension F₀** — extrapolated zero-load velocity / contraction force at zero shortening velocity.
- **Length-tension relationship** — peak tension at sarcomere length 2.0–2.25 μm; basis of Frank-Starling mechanism (Ch 14).
- **Series-elastic component** — tendon/connective tissue/titin elasticity in series with sarcomere force.
- **Phosphocreatine / creatine kinase (CK)** — high-energy phosphate reservoir / interconverting enzyme.
- **CK-MM / CK-MB** — skeletal muscle / cardiac muscle isoenzyme of CK.
- **Cardiac troponin T / I** — current gold-standard MI markers.
- **V̇O₂ max / aerobic capacity** — maximum O₂ consumption rate during exercise.
- **Lactate threshold** — % V̇O₂ max above which blood lactate rises sharply.
- **Oxygen debt / EPOC** — post-exercise elevated O₂ uptake to restore phosphocreatine, myoglobin O₂, and oxidize lactate.
- **Slow oxidative (type I) / fast oxidative-glycolytic (IIA) / fast glycolytic (IIX)** — three human fiber types.
- **Myoglobin** — red O₂-binding pigment in slow-twitch (red) fibers.
- **Muscle fatigue (peripheral / central)** — failure of the muscle vs. failure of CNS drive.
- **Satellite cells** — quiescent muscle stem cells; activated for hypertrophy and repair.
- **Myostatin** — autocrine cytokine inhibiting muscle growth.
- **Myokine / irisin** — muscle-derived signaling cytokines; irisin browns white fat.
- **Sarcopenia** — age-related muscle loss; satellite-cell senescence.
- **Intercalated disc** — cardiac cell-to-cell junction with gap junctions + desmosomes.
- **Functional syncytium** — electrically coupled mass of cardiac cells contracting as one.
- **Pacemaker cell** — myocardial cell with spontaneous depolarization setting heart rate.
- **Dense body** — smooth-muscle cytoplasmic anchor analogous to a Z disc.
- **Calmodulin** — smooth-muscle Ca²⁺-binding regulator (analog of troponin C).
- **MLCK / MLCP** — myosin light chain kinase / phosphatase; phosphorylation switch for smooth-muscle cross-bridge cycling.
- **Latch state** — sustained smooth-muscle force with very low ATP cost.
- **Single-unit / multiunit smooth muscle** — electrically coupled / individually innervated.
- **Varicosity / en passant synapse** — bead-like axonal swelling that releases neurotransmitter to multiple smooth-muscle targets.
- **Calcium-channel blocker (dihydropyridine / verapamil / diltiazem)** — antihypertensive / antiarrhythmic agents.
- **Muscle spindle / intrafusal fiber / nuclear bag / nuclear chain** — proprioceptor / spindle muscle cell / two morphological subtypes.
- **Primary (anulospiral) / secondary (flower-spray) endings** — velocity-sensitive / length-sensitive sensory endings.
- **Alpha / gamma motor neuron** — innervates extrafusal / intrafusal fibers.
- **Coactivation** — simultaneous alpha + gamma stimulation maintains spindle tension during shortening.
- **Golgi tendon organ** — tension sensor in tendons; inhibitory disynaptic reflex.
- **Reciprocal innervation** — agonist excitation paired with antagonist inhibition.
- **Crossed-extensor reflex** — withdrawal reflex with contralateral postural extension.
- **Upper / lower motor neuron** — descending interneuron / final-pathway somatic motor neuron.
- **Pyramidal tract** — corticospinal tract; decussates at medullary pyramids.
- **Extrapyramidal tract** — reticulospinal, vestibulospinal, rubrospinal.
- **Babinski reflex / spastic paralysis / clonus** — signs of upper motor neuron damage.
- **ALS / Lou Gehrig's disease** — combined upper and lower motor neuron degeneration.
- **Parkinson's disease** — substantia nigra dopaminergic loss → basal nuclei dysfunction → resting tremor.
