# Chapter 27 — Mechanics of Ventilation

> Section V · The Respiratory System · pp. 606–627 · Author: Walter F. Boron
> This chapter is the mechanical bridge between **Ch 26 (organization of the respiratory system; lung volumes / spirometry, dead space, anatomy)** and **Chs 30–31 (gas exchange and V̇/Q̇ matching)**. It develops two parallel narratives — **static mechanics** (the elastic balance between lung recoil and chest-wall recoil, surface tension, surfactant, the P_TP–V_L compliance curve and its hysteresis) and **dynamic mechanics** (airway resistance, Poiseuille, the laminar / transitional / turbulent distinction, the time-constant view of dynamic compliance, the transmural-pressure account of expiratory airway compression and effort-independent flow). The key bookkeeping identity that ties everything together is the intrapleural-pressure decomposition $P_{ip} = -P_{TP} + P_A$ (Eq. 27-12).

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Static properties of the lung** (pp. 606–616) — opposing elastic recoils of lung and chest wall create a subatmospheric $P_{ip}$; the apex-to-base $P_{ip}$ gradient; muscles of inspiration (diaphragm, parasternal/external intercostals, bucket-handle/water-pump-handle; accessory muscles); muscles of expiration (passive at rest; abdominal + caudal intercostals when forced); pneumothorax / atelectasis and the transmural-pressure account of lung re-expansion; the static $P_{TP}$–$V_L$ curve and its hysteresis; static compliance; restrictive (fibrosis) vs. emphysematous compliance curves; surface tension; Laplace's law; bubble instability and alveolar interdependence; pulmonary surfactant — DPPC, type II cells, apoproteins SP-A/B/C/D, lamellar bodies, IRDS; surfactant's three effects (↑compliance, anti-edema, alveolar size uniformity / dynamic braking).
2. **Dynamic properties of the lung** (pp. 616–627) — airflow $\dot V = \Delta P / R_{aw}$ (Eq. 27-7); Poiseuille for laminar flow (Eq. 27-8); $R_{aw}$ measurement by body plethysmograph (DuBois); laminar / transitional / turbulent flow and the Reynolds criterion (Eq. 27-11); aggregate $R_{aw}$ along the tracheobronchial tree (Table 27-2); modulators of $R_{aw}$ — vagal/M3, sympathetic/β2, histamine, leukotrienes, lung volume + mechanical tethering; the **intrapleural decomposition** $P_{ip} = -P_{TP} + P_A$ (Eq. 27-12); the respiratory-cycle pressure-volume-flow time course (Fig. 27-15); dynamic compliance and the time constant $\tau = R_{aw} \cdot C$; transmural pressure profiles along an airway during inspiration, rest, and expiration (Fig. 27-17); **equal-pressure-point / dynamic compression** mechanism for **effort-independent maximal expiratory flow** at low lung volumes (Fig. 27-18); pursed-lip (puffing) breathing in emphysema; obstructive vs. restrictive contrast (Table 27-3).

## Section 1 — Static properties of the lung (pp. 606–616)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The balance between the outward elastic recoil of the chest wall and the inward elastic recoil of the lungs generates a subatmospheric intrapleural pressure** (pp. 606–607)
- **Contraction of the diaphragm and selected intercostal muscles increases the volume of the thorax, producing an inspiration** (pp. 607–608)
- **Relaxation of the muscles of inspiration produces a quiet expiration** (p. 608)
- **An increase of the static compliance makes it easier to inflate the lungs** (pp. 608–611)
- **Surface tension at the air-water interface of the airways accounts for most of the elastic recoil of the lungs** (pp. 611–613)
- **Pulmonary surfactant is a mixture of lipids—mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine—and apoproteins** (pp. 613–614)
- **Pulmonary surfactant reduces surface tension and increases compliance** (pp. 614–616)

### Core claims

#### The recoil balance — why $P_{ip}$ is subatmospheric
- The lung's intrinsic elastic recoil pulls the parenchyma **inward**; the chest wall's recoil at FRC pulls the rib cage **outward**. The two surfaces couple — not by direct attachment but **across the intrapleural space** (5–35 μm thick; pleural fluid film). Because lung and chest wall pull away from one another across that thin space, the space is a **relative vacuum**: $P_{ip} < P_B$.
- "$P_{ip}$" is the textbook label for the intrathoracic pressure of all "virtual spaces and interstitia" outside vascular, lymphatic, and airway lumens — not literally the cleft between the pleurae (p. 606).
- Convention: $P_{ip}$ is reported in **cm H₂O relative to barometric** (historically measured with water manometers; respiratory pressures are small). At rest after a quiet expiration ($V_L$ = FRC), the textbook reference value is $P_{ip} \approx -5$ cm H₂O (whole-lung average).
- **Apex-to-base $P_{ip}$ gradient (Fig. 27-2).** Upright, $P_{ip}$ is about **−10 cm H₂O at the apex** and **−2.5 cm H₂O at the base** for a ~30 cm tall lung. Gravity pulls the dense lung mass downward, deepening the apical vacuum and shallowing the basal one; head-down posture inverts the gradient; lateral recumbency produces a smaller side-to-side gradient; in microgravity the gradient vanishes. This gradient is the mechanical cause of the **regional ventilation differences** developed in Ch 31 (V̇/Q̇).

#### Inspiration is active; quiet expiration is passive
- **Diaphragm** (C3–C5 via phrenic) — the dominant inspiratory muscle. It contracts and descends ~1 cm into the abdomen during quiet ventilation; this **rostral-caudal lengthening** is the largest single contributor to thoracic volume change.
- **Intercostals** — not all externals are inspiratory and not all internals are expiratory. Inspiratory neurons preferentially stimulate **rostral / dorsal external intercostals + parasternal internal intercostals**, which have inspiratory mechanical advantages. Contraction of these stiffens the rib cage (resisting indrawing by the more-negative $P_{ip}$) and rotates ribs 2–10 upward / outward (**bucket-handle effect** → increased transverse diameter; **water-pump-handle effect** → increased anterior-posterior diameter).
- **Accessory muscles of inspiration** (forced inspiration only): scalenes, sternocleidomastoids, neck/back muscles (raise pectoral girdle, extend back), upper-respiratory-tract muscles (reduce airway resistance).
- **Quiet expiration is passive** — the energy stored in the lung's elastic recoil during inspiration drives the return to FRC. There are therefore **no primary muscles of expiration**.
- **Forced or pathological expiration** recruits abdominal muscles (rectus, internal/external oblique, transverse abdominal — raise intra-abdominal pressure, push diaphragm up) and the caudal / ventral subset of external intercostals + the dorsal-lateral subset of caudal internal intercostals + the triangularis sterni.

#### Pneumothorax, transmural pressure, and re-expansion (Fig. 27-4)
- A chest wall puncture admits atmospheric air to the intrapleural space → $P_{ip} \to P_B$ → the alveolar transmural pressure collapses → **alveoli collapse (atelectasis)**. Proximal airways collapse before distal ones, so the residual trapped gas is ~10% of TLC (~500 mL) rather than zero.
- The general airway-distending pressure is the **transmural pressure** $P_{TM}$ — the radial pressure difference at any point along the tracheobronchial tree:

$$\boxed{P_{TM} = P_{aw} - P_{ip}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-1})$$

  where $P_{aw}$ is the pressure **inside** that airway and $P_{ip}$ is the pressure in the perimural interstitium.

- The special case for the alveolar wall is **transpulmonary pressure** $P_{TP}$:

$$\boxed{P_{TP} = P_A - P_{ip}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-2})$$

- Under **static conditions** with the glottis open, $P_A = 0$ (no airflow → no driving gradient → alveolar pressure equilibrates with atmosphere), so

$$P_{TP} = -P_{ip} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-3})$$

  i.e., the distending pressure on the alveoli is just the magnitude of the intrapleural vacuum.

- Operationally, the lung can be re-expanded **either** by raising $P_A$ (positive-pressure mechanical ventilation in the ICU) **or** by lowering $P_{ip}$ (physiological inspiration, or chest-tube evacuation of a pneumothorax). The two are interchangeable for $V_L$ as long as $P_{TP}$ ends up the same.

#### Static compliance and the $P_{TP}$–$V_L$ curve (Figs 27-4B, 27-5)
- During graded re-expansion the lung traces a sigmoid $P_{TP}$–$V_L$ curve in four stages: (1) **stable $V_L$** at low $P_{TP}$ — surface tension keeps collapsed airways shut; (2) **opening of airways** beyond ~$P_{TP}$ = +8 cm H₂O — proximal airways pop open one by one; (3) **linear expansion** of all-open airways with constant slope; (4) **plateau / limit of inflation** as $V_L$ approaches TLC.
- Inflation and deflation follow **different paths** → **hysteresis** loop. The inflation limb sits to the right of the deflation limb because a previously closed airway requires a greater $P_{TP}$ to open (surface-tension deficit) than to keep open. During normal tidal breathing the loop is much smaller and lies close to the deflation limb.
- **Static compliance** is the local slope of the curve:

$$\boxed{C = \frac{\Delta V_L}{\Delta P_{TP}}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-4})$$

  with worked normal value $C \approx 0.2$ L / cm H₂O for a 500 mL tidal volume produced by a 2.5 cm H₂O fall in $P_{ip}$. **Elastance** $E = 1/C$.

- Static compliance is mainly a property of **alveoli**, with only a small contribution from conducting airways. Compliance **falls with rising $V_L$** (the curve flattens toward TLC).
- **Restrictive lung disease** (fibrosis): the curve is shifted right and the slope is reduced — the same $\Delta P_{TP}$ produces a smaller $\Delta V_L$ (↓C, ↑elastic recoil). Patients compensate with **rapid shallow breathing**.
- **Emphysema**: the curve is shifted left and is much steeper — same $\Delta P_{TP}$ produces a larger $\Delta V_L$ (↑C, ↓elastic recoil). Patients exert *less* effort to inflate, but **lose elastic recoil for expiration** and **lose alveolar tethering of small airways** (Section 2) — net catastrophe.

#### Surface tension is most of the recoil (Figs 27-6, 27-7)
- **von Neergaard's 1929 experiment.** A degassed lung reinflated with **saline** has (a) much less hysteresis and (b) much greater compliance than the same lung inflated with **air**. Difference = the air-water interface. Surface tension at that interface, not tissue elasticity, supplies most of the elastic recoil of a normal lung.
- **Surface tension definition** (Fig. 27-6C). Surface water molecules experience an unbalanced inward pull (no water above them) → tension in the plane of the surface. For length $l$ of contact and applied force $F$ needed to stretch the interface:

$$T = \frac{F}{l} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-5})$$

  For a pure air-water interface at 37°C, $T \approx 70$ dynes/cm.

- **Laplace's law for a spherical air bubble in water** (Fig. 27-6D):

$$\boxed{P = \frac{2T}{r}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-6})$$

  $P$ is the gas pressure inside the bubble required to balance the inwardly-directed surface tension at the air-water interface. Worked numbers (Fig. 27-6E): two bubbles in water, $T = 70$ dynes/cm — a bubble of radius 0.010 cm needs $P = 14$ cm H₂O; a bubble of radius 0.005 cm needs $P = 28$ cm H₂O. **Smaller bubbles → higher pressures** at the same surface tension.

- **Implication for alveoli.** Two interconnected unequal alveoli are **mechanically unstable**: the smaller (higher-pressure) one empties into the larger one — alveolar collapse. In the lung this would proceed until pulmonary architecture stopped it; the brakes are (i) **mechanical interdependence** (each alveolus is tethered to its neighbors), and (ii) **surfactant**, which dynamically changes surface tension with radius.
- Laplace's law for the lung is the same equation applied to blood vessels on pp. 455–457 — see Ch 19 (arteries / veins).

#### Pulmonary surfactant (Figs 27-7, 27-8, 27-9; Table 27-1)
- **Surfactant = surface-active agent.** A molecule with a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tail; it adsorbs at the air-water interface (head down, tail up), reducing the surface density of water molecules at the interface and **lowering surface tension** — to ~25 dynes/cm or less during quiet breathing (vs 70 for pure water).
- **Composition.** ~90% lipid; about half is **dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC, "dipalmitoyl lecithin")** — phosphatidylcholine with two saturated 16-carbon (palmitate) tails (Fig. 27-8). Second-most-abundant: unsaturated phosphatidylcholines. Phosphatidylglycerol (~11%) is overrepresented vs ordinary cell membranes. ~10% protein: plasma albumin + secretory IgA + four **apoproteins** SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D.
- **Apoprotein functions (Table 27-1).**

| Apoprotein | Solubility | Functions |
|---|---|---|
| **SP-A** | Water-soluble (collagen-like) | Innate immunity (opsonin for bacteria/viruses → macrophage phagocytosis); feedback control of surfactant secretion; tubular myelin formation |
| **SP-B** | Lipid-soluble (intrinsic membrane) | Speeds adsorption of surfactant monolayer at the air-water interface; tubular myelin formation. **Hereditary SP-B deficiency** is fatal without lung transplant. |
| **SP-C** | Lipid-soluble (intrinsic membrane) | Speeds monolayer formation |
| **SP-D** | Water-soluble (collagen-like) | Innate immunity; possibly surfactant metabolism |

- **Cellular life cycle (Fig. 27-9).** Type II alveolar cells (cuboidal; coexist with the thin type I pneumocytes) and Clara cells of respiratory bronchioles synthesize the components. Final assembly happens in **lamellar bodies** (~1 μm, concentric layers of lipid + protein). Constitutive exocytosis at ~10%/h of lamellar-body content. In the aqueous alveolar film surfactant forms **tubular myelin** (mesh-like, SP-A rich) before reaching the air-water interface. Removal: alveolar macrophages degrade some; type II cells endocytose and either recycle or destroy the rest.
- **Fetal physiology.** Surfactant synthesis and secretion are low until immediately before birth, when a **surge in maternal glucocorticoids** triggers production. Preterm infants therefore commonly develop **infant respiratory distress syndrome (IRDS)** — surfactant-deficiency syndrome with severely reduced compliance (curve shifted toward the fibrosis curve of Fig. 27-5). This is the developmental anchor for Ch 56–57 (pregnancy / fetal-neonatal physiology).
- **Stimuli for adult surfactant secretion.** Hyperinflation (sighing, yawning), exercise, β-adrenergic agonists, Ca²⁺ ionophores.

#### Surfactant's three physiological effects
1. **↑ compliance / ↓ elastic recoil.** Sudden disappearance of surfactant would at least double total elastic recoil and cause widespread small-airway collapse (acquired surfactant-deficiency syndromes include **ARDS** in adults and **IRDS** in neonates).
2. **Anti-edema.** Without surfactant, large surface tension in the alveolar fluid film would suck fluid out of the interstitium into the alveolar space (a Laplace-driven negative interstitial pressure). With surfactant, surface tension is low and the balance with interstitial hydrostatic pressure ($P_{ip}$) keeps the alveolar film thin.
3. **Alveolar size uniformity / dynamic braking** (Fig. 27-10). Surfactant's surface concentration changes with the alveolar surface area. **Rapidly expanding alveoli** dilute surfactant faster than it can adsorb → surface tension rises → elastic recoil rises → expansion is braked. **Rapidly contracting alveoli** concentrate surfactant faster than it can desorb → surface tension falls (can halve below the FRC value) → elastic recoil falls → re-expansion tendency rises → contraction is braked. Net effect: surfactant **equalizes the rates of expansion and contraction across alveoli of differing time constants**, preserving uniform ventilation despite heterogeneous mechanical loads, and producing the small hysteresis of the normal tidal $P_{ip}$–$V_L$ loop.

### Equations (Section 1 — static mechanics)

- **Transmural pressure across any airway wall:**
$$P_{TM} = P_{aw} - P_{ip} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-1})$$

- **Transpulmonary pressure (alveolar transmural):**
$$P_{TP} = P_A - P_{ip} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-2})$$

- **Static condition (glottis open, no airflow → $P_A = 0$):**
$$P_{TP} = -P_{ip} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-3})$$

- **Static compliance (slope of $P_{TP}$–$V_L$ curve):**
$$C = \frac{\Delta V_L}{\Delta P_{TP}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-4})$$

  with elastance $E = 1/C$.

- **Surface tension of a liquid film** (force per unit length of contact):
$$T = \frac{F}{l} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-5})$$

- **Laplace's law for a spherical air-water interface:**
$$P = \frac{2T}{r} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-6})$$

  Two-bubble worked example: $r_1 = 100$ μm, $r_2 = 50$ μm, $T = 70$ dynes/cm → $P_1 = 14$ cm H₂O, $P_2 = 28$ cm H₂O → smaller bubble empties into the larger one when connected; the basis of alveolar instability without surfactant.

### Box 27-1 — Restrictive Pulmonary Disease (p. 611)
Any disorder that reduces FRC, VC, or TLC by stiffening the lung-thorax system. Targets:
- **Lung parenchyma** — decreased static compliance; *IRDS* (surfactant deficiency), *pulmonary edema*, *diffuse interstitial pulmonary fibrosis*.
- **Pleura** — *pneumothorax* (air) or *pleural effusion* (fluid) restricts alveolar expansion.
- **Chest wall** — *ankylosing spondylitis* (reduced bucket-handle rotation), *kyphoscoliosis* (deformed rib/spine geometry); both impair coughing → infections.
- **Neuromuscular** — *polio* (brainstem); *ALS* (motor neurons; dyspnea on exertion is early sign); *barbiturate poisoning* (brainstem); pain from chest surgery / broken ribs; **flail chest** with paradoxical inspiratory in-drawing.

## Section 2 — Dynamic properties of the lung (pp. 616–627)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Airflow is proportional to the difference between alveolar and atmospheric pressure, but inversely proportional to airway resistance** (pp. 616–617)
- **In the lung, airflow is transitional in most of the tracheobronchial tree** (pp. 617–618)
- **The smallest airways contribute only slightly to total airway resistance in healthy lungs** (pp. 618–620)
- **Vagal tone, histamine, and reduced lung volume all increase airway resistance** (p. 620)
- **Intrapleural pressure has a static component ($-P_{TP}$) that determines lung volume and a dynamic component ($P_A$) that determines airflow** (pp. 620–622)
- **During inspiration, a sustained negative shift in $P_{ip}$ causes $P_A$ to become transiently more negative** (pp. 622–623)
- **Dynamic compliance falls as respiratory frequency rises** (pp. 623–624)
- **Transmural pressure differences cause airways to dilate during inspiration and to compress during expiration** (pp. 624–626)
- **Because of airway collapse, expiratory flow rates become independent of effort at low lung volumes** (pp. 626–627)

### Core claims

#### The pulmonary Ohm's law (Eq. 27-7)
The flow of air through the tracheobronchial tree obeys the same proportionality as the flow of blood through vessels or current through wires (Ch 17, Eq. 17-1):

$$\boxed{\dot V = \frac{\Delta P}{R_{aw}} = \frac{P_A - P_B}{R_{aw}}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-7})$$

- $\dot V$ (L/s, time derivative of $V_L$): air flow. Convention: $\dot V < 0$ = inflow (because $V_L$ rises during inspiration when $P_A < P_B$).
- $\Delta P = P_A - P_B$: the difference between alveolar pressure and barometric pressure is the **dynamic driving force**.
- $R_{aw}$: airway resistance, mainly a property of the **conducting airways**.

#### Poiseuille for laminar airflow (Eq. 27-8)
For laminar flow, $R$ of a single tube:

$$\boxed{R = \frac{8 \eta l}{\pi r^4}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-8})$$

- $\eta$ = gas viscosity, $l$ = tube length, $r$ = radius.
- $\eta$ and $l$ are essentially fixed for a given individual; the critical variable is $r$. Because $R \propto 1/r^4$, a **10% decrease in radius produces a 52% increase in $R$ and a 34% decrease in $\dot V$** at fixed driving pressure — a one-line explanation for why even modest small-airway narrowing in asthma or COPD is so functionally devastating.
- A small note on gas viscosity: $\eta_{\text{He}} > \eta_{\text{N}_2}$, so breathing helium-air mixtures (heliox) actually *increases* $R$ if flow were purely laminar; the clinical benefit of heliox comes through its lower **density**, which matters when flow is **turbulent** (see below).

#### Measuring $R_{aw}$ — the body plethysmograph (DuBois 1956; Fig. 27-11)
Practically, $R_{aw}$ is computed from Eq. 27-7 rearranged:

$$R_{aw} = \frac{\Delta P}{\dot V} = \frac{P_A - P_B}{\dot V} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-9})$$

with $\dot V$ measured at the mouth by a **pneumotachometer** and $P_A$ measured via the **body plethysmograph**:
1. Subject sits in a sealed box; the spirometer of an ordinary system is replaced by a pressure transducer in the box wall measuring $P_{\text{box}}$.
2. Subject inhales against a **closed shutter** at the mouth → no airflow → mouth pressure $P_M$ = $P_A$. As the thorax expands, it encroaches on the box gas, raising $P_{\text{box}}$. The ratio $\Delta P_A / \Delta P_{\text{box}}$ provides a calibration.
3. With the **shutter open**, the subject inhales. At first the thorax expands faster than air can enter; $P_A$ falls, $P_{\text{box}}$ rises; from the calibration ratio one converts $\Delta P_{\text{box}}$ to $\Delta P_A$.
4. Computing $R_{aw} = \Delta P_A / \dot V$. Worked example (Eq. 27-10): $\dot V = -0.5$ L/s, $P_A = -1$ cm H₂O → $R_{aw} = 2$ cm H₂O / (L/s).
- **Normal $R_{aw} \approx 1.5$ cm H₂O / (L/s)** (range 0.6–2.3). Pulmonary tissue resistance (friction of lung and chest-wall tissues as they slide past one another) adds another ~20%. Severely obstructed patients: >10 cm H₂O / (L/s).

#### Laminar / transitional / turbulent flow and the Reynolds criterion (Fig. 27-12)
- **Laminar** flow: smooth, parabolic velocity profile (faster on axis; zero at wall — Newtonian no-slip). $\dot V \propto \Delta P$; Poiseuille governs.
- **Turbulent** flow: random vortices, much higher effective resistance. $\dot V \propto \sqrt{\Delta P}$ — must work much harder per unit increase in flow. Density matters more than viscosity.
- **Transitional** flow: laminar-with-eddies, occurring at branch points and bifurcations. Resistance higher than laminar but pressure-flow relationship still approximately linear.
- **Reynolds number** (dimensionless):

$$\boxed{Re = \frac{2 r \bar v \rho}{\eta}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-11})$$

  $r$ = tube radius, $\bar v$ = mean cross-sectional velocity, $\rho$ = gas density, $\eta$ = gas viscosity. For long, straight, smooth tubes: $Re < 2000$ → laminar; $Re > 3000$ → turbulent. **For the lung's bumpy, branched geometry, the critical $Re$ is far lower** — $Re < 1$ is required for guaranteed laminar flow. Result: **truly laminar flow exists only distal to the terminal bronchioles** (smallest airways), the trachea typically has **turbulent** flow (especially during exercise or coughing), and **transitional flow occupies most of the tracheobronchial tree**.

#### Aggregate resistance and the small-airway puzzle (Table 27-2)
A typical normal partitioning of $R_{aw} = 1.5$ cm H₂O/(L/s):

| Location | Normal $R_{aw}$ | COPD $R_{aw}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Pharynx–larynx | 0.6 | 0.6 |
| Airways > 2 mm diameter | 0.6 | 0.9 |
| Airways < 2 mm diameter | 0.3 | 3.5 |
| **Total** | **1.5** | **5.0** |

- **Counterintuitive observation.** Although each small airway has a huge individual resistance (Poiseuille's $r^4$), **the small airways are arranged in massive parallel** — billions of them — so their aggregate resistance is the smallest of the three compartments.
- **Clinical implication for COPD.** The pathology starts in the small airways but their aggregate $R_{aw}$ is so low that a 2-fold increase produces only a tiny change in total resistance — **early COPD is undetectable by ordinary resistance screening**. By the time total $R_{aw}$ has tripled, the small-airway compartment has increased ~12-fold. Approaches that detect non-uniformity of ventilation (Ch 31, pp. 687–689) are more sensitive for early disease.

#### Modulators of $R_{aw}$
- **Vagal / parasympathetic tone.** Acetylcholine → M3 muscarinic on bronchial smooth muscle → **bronchoconstriction** (↑$R_{aw}$). Blocked by atropine and by inhaled anticholinergics (especially useful in COPD). Irritants (cigarette smoke) produce reflex bronchoconstriction via this efferent arm.
- **Sympathetic tone.** Norepinephrine → β2 → bronchodilation (↓$R_{aw}$). Endogenous NE is a poor β2 agonist, so the resting sympathetic contribution is weak — but **circulating epinephrine** (adrenal) is a much better β2 agonist, and exogenous **β2-agonists** (albuterol, salmeterol) are the mainstay bronchodilators in asthma.
- **Humoral.** Histamine, leukotrienes (LTC₄, LTD₄ — most potent bronchoconstrictors) → ↑$R_{aw}$.
- **Lung volume** (Fig. 27-13A). $R_{aw}$ falls steeply with rising $V_L$: at RV it's very high; near TLC it's very low. Two reasons: (i) the conducting airways themselves dilate as the lung expands ($r^4$); (ii) **mechanical tethering / interdependence** — the alveolar walls attached radially to a conducting airway pull it open as they expand (Fig. 27-13B). Patients with obstructive disease compensate by breathing at a higher resting $V_L$ (higher FRC; "hyperinflation"), which keeps their airways relatively dilated — they trade WOB at high lung volume against catastrophically high $R_{aw}$ at low lung volume.

#### Static vs. dynamic decomposition of $P_{ip}$ (Eq. 27-12; Fig. 27-14; Table 27-3)
The book's central organizing identity, rearranging Eq. 27-2:

$$\boxed{P_{ip} = -P_{TP} + P_A} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-12})$$

That is, the brain-controlled intrapleural pressure has a **static component** ($-P_{TP}$, which sets $V_L$ via the static compliance curve) and a **dynamic component** ($P_A$, which sets $\dot V$ via airway resistance). Table 27-3 lays out the dichotomy explicitly:

| Property | **Static** | **Dynamic** |
|---|---|---|
| Anatomical correlate | Alveoli | Conducting airways |
| Key "constant" | Static compliance $C$ | Airway resistance $R_{aw}$ |
| Key pressure | $P_{TP} = P_A - P_{ip}$ | $P_A$ |
| Key parameter | $V_L$ (from $C = \Delta V_L / \Delta P_{TP}$) | $\dot V$ (from $\dot V = P_A / R_{aw}$) |
| Pathology | Restrictive (e.g., fibrosis) — ↓$C$ | Obstructive (e.g., COPD) — ↑$R_{aw}$ |

This decomposition organizes the rest of the chapter, and it organizes Ch 28–32 (acid-base, gas exchange, V̇/Q̇, control) as well.

#### The respiratory-cycle time course (Fig. 27-15)
The chapter's idealized 5-second cycle (12 breaths/min, $V_T = 500$ mL):
- Point **a** (pre-inspiration, static, $V_L$ = FRC, $P_A = 0$, $P_{ip} = -5$, $P_{TP} = +5$).
- Point **b** (mid-inspiration, dynamic, $V_L$ = FRC + 250, $P_A = -1.25$, $P_{ip} = -7.5$, $P_{TP} = +6.25$).
- Point **c** (end-inspiration, static again, $V_L$ = FRC + 500, $P_A = 0$, $P_{ip} = -7.5$, $P_{TP} = +7.5$).
- Point **d** (mid-expiration, dynamic, $V_L$ = FRC + 250, $P_A = +1.25$, $P_{ip} = -5$, $P_{TP} = +6.25$).
- Back to **a** (end-expiration / next-cycle start).

The textbook's "Julius-Caesar-conquering-Gaul" analogy (p. 623): during inspiration, the body invests an increasing fraction of $\Delta P_{ip}$ in $-P_{TP}$ (maintaining the newly expanded $V_L$, just as Caesar's resources shift from expansion to territory maintenance) and a decreasing fraction in $P_A$ (further expansion).

#### Dynamic compliance and the time constant (Eq. 27-13; Fig. 27-16)
The lung as a single RC compartment has a time constant:

$$\boxed{\tau = R_{aw} \cdot C}$$

For normal lungs $\tau \approx 0.2$ s. Inspiration is ~63% complete at $t = \tau$, 86% at $2\tau$, 95% at $3\tau$, etc. At 12 breaths/min (2.5 s per inspiration ≈ 12 $\tau$), the inspired volume is essentially the asymptotic value.

**Dynamic compliance** is the operational compliance computed from a cyclic-breathing tidal volume / intrapleural-pressure swing:

$$\boxed{C_{\text{dynamic}} = \frac{\Delta V_L}{-\Delta P_{ip}}} \quad (\text{Eq. 27-13})$$

At frequency = 0, $C_{\text{dynamic}} = C_{\text{static}}$. As frequency rises, $C_{\text{dynamic}}$ falls (less time to reach the static $V_L$ at each cycle). The frequency-dependent fall-off **becomes severe earlier** when $\tau$ is increased (i.e., when $R_{aw}$ or $C$ is elevated). For normal lungs the fall is ~5% at 48 breaths/min; for a lung with 5× normal $R_{aw}$, dynamic compliance falls by >50% over the same frequency range. This is the asthma-pattern signature (Box 27-3): $R_{aw}$ ↑, $C_{\text{static}}$ ~normal, $C_{\text{dynamic}}$ falls steeply with frequency.

In real lungs each alveolus has its own $\tau_i = R_i \cdot C_i$; disease heterogeneity broadens the $\tau_i$ distribution; at high respiratory frequency the slow units ($\tau_i \gg 1/f$) "drop out" — they contribute progressively less ventilation. This **frequency-dependent unit recruitment** is the mechanical basis for the V̇/Q̇ heterogeneity developed in Ch 31.

#### Transmural pressure profiles along an airway (Fig. 27-17)
At the same $V_L$ = FRC throughout, the airway-wall transmural pressure profile differs sharply between conditions:

**Static (B):** $P_A = 0$ throughout the airway, $P_{ip} = -5$, so $P_{TM} = P_{aw} - P_{ip} = 0 - (-5) = +5$ cm H₂O everywhere — a uniform mild distending pressure (Eq. 27-14).

**Vigorous inspiration (A):** $P_A = -15$ cm H₂O (deeply negative to drive inflow); for the alveolus to remain at FRC, $P_{TP} = +5$, so $P_{ip} = -P_{TP} + P_A = -5 + (-15) = -20$ cm H₂O. Walking out from alveolus to mouth, $P_{aw}$ decays from −15 to 0 (frictional). At a midpoint with $P_{aw} = -8$: $P_{TM} = -8 - (-20) = +12$ cm H₂O — markedly more positive than the +5 at rest, so **inspiration dilates airways** as we move proximally (Eq. 27-16). The largest airways have the largest dilating tendency.

**Vigorous expiration (C):** $P_A = +15$ cm H₂O (positive to drive outflow), $P_{TP} = +5$ for $V_L$ = FRC, so $P_{ip} = -5 + 15 = +10$ cm H₂O (intrapleural pressure now exceeds atmospheric — a positive vacuum is the wrong word; it's a positive squeeze on everything in the chest). $P_{aw}$ decays from +15 in the alveolus to 0 at the lips. At a midpoint with $P_{aw} = +8$: $P_{TM} = 8 - 10 = -2$ cm H₂O — a small **collapsing** transmural pressure (Eq. 27-18). At all proximal points $P_{TM}$ is negative and the airway is squeezed. **This is the mechanism of expiratory airway compression.**

Notice the polarity: **inspiration dilates airways; expiration compresses them.** The largest airways are most exposed to the collapsing tendency during expiration but are also the most protected by cartilage; the small airways at midcourse are the dynamically compressible bottleneck. Mechanical tethering by surrounding alveoli (Fig. 27-13B) helps resist collapse — and is **lost in emphysema**, which is why emphysematous patients suffer disproportionately in expiration.

#### Equal pressure point and effort-independent expiratory flow (Fig. 27-18)
- Define the **equal pressure point (EPP)** along the airway as the location where $P_{aw} = P_{ip}$ → $P_{TM} = 0$. Distal to the EPP (toward the alveolus), $P_{TM} > 0$ (airway open); proximal to the EPP (toward the mouth), $P_{TM} < 0$ (airway compressed). The EPP moves distally as expiration proceeds (because $P_{ip}$ stays high while $P_{aw}$ falls along the airway with progressively less alveolar driving pressure).
- **Experiment** (Fig. 27-18C): subject inhales to TLC, then makes a series of expirations with graded efforts (low / medium / high / maximal). For each effort, $\dot V$ is plotted against $V_L$ (a flow-volume curve).
- Result: **at high $V_L$ (>3–4 L), $\dot V$ depends on effort** (effort-dependent region). **At low $V_L$ (<3 L) all the curves merge** — $\dot V$ is determined by an envelope independent of effort (effort-independent region). At low $V_L$ → low mechanical tethering → airway collapses earlier in expiration → the airway segment between the EPP and the mouth determines max flow regardless of how hard the subject pushes.
- **Fig. 27-18D** transposes the same data into a flow-vs-effort plot at three fixed lung volumes: at $V_L = 5$ L, $\dot V$ rises monotonically (sublinearly) with $P_A$ — effort-dependent. At $V_L = 4$ L and $V_L = 3$ L, $\dot V$ rises with $P_A$ at low effort but **plateaus** — increasing effort increases $P_A$, but the same increase compresses the airways correspondingly, so $P_A / R_{aw}$ stays constant.
- **FEV₁ / FVC.** The clinical descendant of this experiment. **FEV₁** (forced expiratory volume in one second) and **FVC** (forced vital capacity) are read off a forced expiratory spirogram, both performed from TLC down to RV.
  - **Obstructive pattern** (asthma, COPD): FEV₁ falls disproportionately; **FEV₁/FVC < 0.7** is the diagnostic threshold.
  - **Restrictive pattern** (fibrosis): FEV₁ and FVC fall together; **FEV₁/FVC is preserved or elevated**; TLC, VC, and FRC are reduced. The shape of the maximal flow-volume loop is "scooped out" (concave upward) in obstruction and small-but-normally-shaped in restriction.
- **Pursed-lip ("puffing") breathing** (Fig. 27-17D). Emphysema patients deliberately exhale through pursed lips to create a high resistance at the mouth. Because most of the $P_{aw}$ drop now occurs across the lips, $P_{aw}$ along the intrathoracic airways stays high → $P_{TM}$ stays positive → airways stay open → less dynamic compression. Cost: low $\dot V$, low minute ventilation. This is also the principle behind continuous-positive-airway-pressure (CPAP) therapy.

#### Three coping strategies the emphysema patient adopts
1. **Exhale slowly** (low $\dot V$ → less positive $P_A$ → less positive $P_{ip}$ → less squeeze on airways).
2. **Breathe at a higher $V_L$** (hyperinflation → more mechanical tethering → less collapse; Fig. 27-13A).
3. **Pursed-lip exhalation** (Fig. 27-17D) — moves the EPP to the lips.

### Box 27-2 — Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) (p. 620)
- COPD = chronic bronchitis (chronic inflammation of bronchi / bronchioles) + emphysema (alveolar wall destruction; loss of mechanical tethering of conducting airways → exaggerated expiratory collapse) — usually both. Fourth leading cause of death in the United States.
- Major risk: **cigarette smoking**. Inherited risk: **α₁-antitrypsin deficiency** (Table 18-1) — protease imbalance lets neutrophil elastase destroy lung extracellular matrix.
- Inflammation infiltrate: macrophages, activated T lymphocytes, neutrophils in conducting airways; activated lymphocytes in alveolar walls.
- Mechanics: ↑ $R_{aw}$ (bronchitis component) + ↑ $C$ (emphysema component) → frequency-dependent dynamic compliance fall + dynamic hyperinflation + dynamic airway compression during expiration → effort-independent expiratory flow at most of the vital capacity → severe limitation of $\dot V_{\max}$ on exertion. FEV₁/FVC < 0.7.

### Box 27-3 — Asthma (p. 620)
- Prevalence 5–10% in the US. Primary process is **airway inflammation**; bronchospasm is secondary. Trigger inhalation activates immune cells in the airway walls → release of cytokines, leukotrienes (LTC₄, LTD₄), histamine → bronchospasm + edema + mucus.
- Classic presentation: shortness of breath, wheezing, cough. Trigger spectrum: allergens, heat / cold, occupational irritants, exercise.
- Spirometry: decreased FEV₁ is the diagnostic signature.
- Mechanics: $R_{aw}$ ↑, $C_{\text{static}}$ ≈ normal, $C_{\text{dynamic}}$ falls steeply with frequency.
- Therapy ladder:
  1. Inhaled short-acting β2-agonist (albuterol) as needed for infrequent attacks.
  2. + Inhaled corticosteroid daily for >1–2 attacks/week (suppress inflammation).
  3. + Long-acting β2-agonist + inhaled corticosteroid daily for moderate/severe asthma.
  4. Oral corticosteroids for severe sustained disease.
  5. Leukotriene inhibitors (e.g., montelukast); muscarinic antagonists (less effective than in COPD); theophylline (PDE inhibitor, now rarely first-line).

Two branches: **left branch** ("Static component, $-P_{TP}$ — overcomes elastic forces — maintains current lung volume") leads to the static $P_{TP}$–$V_L$ curve; **right branch** ("Dynamic component, $P_A$ — overcomes resistance and inertia — produces airflow") leads to the dynamic $P_A$–$\dot V$ curve (slope 1/R_aw). #### Figure 27-17 — Dilation and collapse of airways with airflow *(viewed)*

Four-panel composite. In all panels $V_L$ = FRC; only the dynamic state differs. **Panel A — Inspiration:** anatomical cartoon of the alveolus and one branching conducting airway exiting to the mouth; $P_A = -15$, $P_{ip} = -20$, $P_{TP} = +5$. $P_{aw}$ is labeled at successive points along the airway as the pressure falls toward zero at the mouth; transmural arrows are drawn at each point — they point **outward** (dilating) and grow longer proximally. Below the cartoon, a graph plots $P_{aw}$ (blue) and $P_{ip}$ (green) along the alveolus-to-mouth distance — both lines start at $-15$ / $-20$ and $P_{aw}$ rises monotonically to 0 at the lips while $P_{ip}$ stays flat at $-20$; $P_{TM} = P_{aw} - P_{ip}$ is the vertical gap (always positive, growing toward the mouth). **Panel B — No airflow:** $P_A = 0$, $P_{ip} = -5$, $P_{TP} = +5$. $P_{aw} = 0$ throughout the airway, $P_{ip} = -5$ throughout the chest, $P_{TM} = +5$ everywhere (mild uniform dilation). **Panel C — Expiration:** $P_A = +15$, $P_{ip} = +10$, $P_{TP} = +5$. $P_{aw}$ falls from $+15$ at the alveolus to 0 at the lips; $P_{ip} = +10$ throughout. The two curves cross at the **equal pressure point (EPP)** — between the EPP and the mouth, $P_{aw} < P_{ip}$, $P_{TM} < 0$, airway compressed (downward arrows on the cartoon, showing the squeezing tendency). **Panel D — Puffing during expiration (pursed-lip):** $P_{aw}$ falls only slightly from $+15$ along the intrathoracic airway because the high mouth resistance ("pursed lips") absorbs most of the pressure drop at the lips. The intrathoracic $P_{aw}$ stays close to $P_{ip}$, $P_{TM}$ stays near zero or slightly positive, airway stays open. The EPP has moved out of the lung and into the lips.

> Vision note: This is the chapter's mechanistic anchor for the equal-pressure-point / dynamic-compression / effort-independent-flow story, and for the pursed-lip therapeutic intervention. Anchor for emphysema mechanics, COPD breathing patterns, and for the obstructive flow-volume loop in Fig. 27-18.

#### Figure 27-18 — Dependence of airflow on effort *(viewed)*

Four panels. **Panel A — No airflow at TLC:** subject has inhaled to TLC, $P_A = 0$, $P_{TP} = +30$, $P_{ip} = -30$. **Panel B — Maximal forced expiration from TLC:** the instant after starting expiration, $P_A = +40$, $P_{TP} = +30$, $P_{ip} = +10$. **Panel C — Expiratory flow-volume curve:** x-axis $V_L$ from RV (low) to TLC (high), y-axis $\dot V$ (L/s, 0–10). Four superimposed curves for graded effort (low, medium, high, maximal). The maximal curve rises rapidly from zero at TLC to a peak just below TLC, then descends more or less linearly back to zero at RV — this defines an **envelope**. At high $V_L$ (~5 L) the four curves are distinctly separated (effort-dependent). At medium $V_L$ (~4 L) they begin to converge. At low $V_L$ (~3 L) all four curves overlap on the envelope (effort-independent). **Panel D — Expiratory flow-effort curve:** x-axis $P_A$ (effort, 0 to high), y-axis $\dot V$ (L/s). Three curves at fixed $V_L$ of 3 L, 4 L, 5 L. At $V_L = 5$ L the curve rises monotonically (sublinearly, because $R_{aw}$ rises slightly with effort). At $V_L = 4$ L the curve rises and then plateaus. At $V_L = 3$ L the curve plateaus almost immediately. The dashed "Expected $\dot V$ if resistance were fixed" reference line rises linearly through the origin with slope $1/R_{aw}$; the actual curves diverge below it.

> Vision note: This is the canonical flow-volume / flow-effort figure used in every pulmonary-function clinic. The downward-sloping envelope of the maximal flow-volume curve is the "obstructive scoop" of clinical spirometry. Anchor for FEV₁ / FVC, peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR), and the differential diagnosis of obstructive vs. restrictive defects.

## Lung volumes and capacities reference (carried from Ch 26 for context)

This material is developed in **Chapter 26 (Organization of the Respiratory System)** but is used as the substrate of every spirometric / mechanical claim in Ch 27. Reproduced here as a convenience.

| Volume / capacity | Definition | Typical value (70 kg adult ♂) |
|---|---|---|
| **TV** (V_T, tidal volume) | Volume moved per breath at rest | ~500 mL |
| **IRV** (inspiratory reserve volume) | Maximal additional inhalable volume above tidal end-inspiration | ~3000 mL |
| **ERV** (expiratory reserve volume) | Maximal additional exhalable volume below tidal end-expiration | ~1100 mL |
| **RV** (residual volume) | Volume left in lungs after a maximal expiration; cannot be exhaled | ~1200 mL |
| **IC** (inspiratory capacity) = TV + IRV | Maximal volume inhalable from FRC | ~3500 mL |
| **FRC** (functional residual capacity) = ERV + RV | Volume in lungs at end of tidal expiration; equilibrium of lung-recoil-in vs chest-wall-recoil-out | ~2300 mL |
| **VC** (vital capacity) = IRV + TV + ERV | Largest exhalable volume from a maximal inspiration to a maximal expiration | ~4600 mL |
| **TLC** (total lung capacity) = VC + RV = IC + FRC | Volume at end of maximal inspiration | ~5800 mL |

Forced spirometric parameters layered on top:
- **FVC** (forced vital capacity) — VC measured during a maximal forced expiration; usually slightly less than slow-maneuver VC.
- **FEV₁** (forced expiratory volume in 1 s) — volume exhaled in the first second of a maximal forced expiration from TLC.
- **FEV₁/FVC** — the ratio. Diagnostic axis: **< 0.7 → obstructive pattern; ≥ 0.7 with reduced FVC → restrictive pattern**.
- **PEFR** (peak expiratory flow rate) — the peak of $\dot V$ on the forced expiratory flow-volume loop; obtainable with a handheld peak flow meter for at-home asthma monitoring.
- **MMEF / FEF₂₅₋₇₅** (forced expiratory flow during the middle half of FVC) — averaged $\dot V$ between 25% and 75% of FVC; sensitive to small-airway disease (the effort-independent / low-V_L portion of the flow-volume loop).

The mechanical interpretation tying these spirometric numbers to Ch 27 mechanics:
- **TLC, VC, FRC** all measured at static balance (no airflow) → set by the **static compliance curves** of lung + chest wall.
- **FEV₁, PEFR, FEF₂₅₋₇₅** all measured under forced flow → set by the **flow-volume envelope** of Fig. 27-18 → governed by $R_{aw}$, EPP/dynamic compression, and mechanical tethering.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **TLC / VC / IC / FRC / RV / TV / IRV / ERV** — lung volumes and capacities (Ch 26).
- **FVC / FEV₁ / FEV₁/FVC / PEFR / FEF₂₅₋₇₅** — forced-spirometric parameters; FEV₁/FVC < 0.7 = obstructive; preserved with low FVC = restrictive.
- **Spirometer / pneumotachometer / body plethysmograph** — measurement instruments. Plethysmograph (DuBois) is the standard for $R_{aw}$ via Boyle's law and box-pressure tracking.
- **P_B (P_barometric)** — atmospheric pressure (~760 mm Hg at sea level; convention 0 cm H₂O relative).
- **P_A (alveolar pressure)** — pressure inside the alveolus. Zero under static conditions with open glottis; negative during inspiration; positive during expiration.
- **P_ip (intrapleural pressure)** — pressure in the intrapleural space and all virtual intrathoracic spaces. Subatmospheric (about −5 cm H₂O at FRC); apex-to-base gradient under gravity.
- **P_TP (transpulmonary pressure)** = P_A − P_ip — the alveolar distending pressure. Under static conditions = −P_ip.
- **P_TM (transmural pressure)** = P_aw − P_ip — the airway-wall distending pressure for any conducting airway.
- **Compliance C** = ΔV/ΔP — distensibility (slope of P-V curve). Static C ≈ 0.2 L/cm H₂O for normal lung. Restrictive disease ↓C; emphysema ↑C.
- **Elastance E** = 1/C — the inverse: stiffness.
- **Dynamic compliance C_dynamic** = ΔV_L / −ΔP_ip during cyclic breathing; falls below C_static as frequency rises, more so when τ is elevated.
- **Time constant τ** = R_aw · C ≈ 0.2 s for normal lung; multiple τ's in heterogeneous disease.
- **R_aw (airway resistance)** = ΔP/V̇ ≈ 1.5 cm H₂O/(L/s) normal; mainly a property of conducting airways; partitioning in Table 27-2.
- **Poiseuille's law** R = 8ηl / (πr⁴) — laminar-flow resistance; the r⁴ dependence is the single most important relation in airway pathophysiology.
- **Reynolds number Re** = 2rv̄ρ/η — laminar (<2000, ideally; <1 in branching lung), transitional, turbulent (>3000). Most of the tracheobronchial tree carries transitional flow; trachea is turbulent.
- **Laminar / transitional / turbulent flow** — pressure-flow relationships V̇ ∝ ΔP, V̇ ∝ ΔP (with extra eddies), V̇ ∝ √ΔP.
- **Laplace's law** P = 2T/r — surface-tension pressure inside an air-water bubble. Predicts smaller-alveolus instability and the need for surfactant.
- **Surface tension T** = F/l — force per unit length in the plane of an air-water interface; 70 dynes/cm pure water, ~25 dynes/cm with surfactant.
- **Pulmonary surfactant** — DPPC-rich + SP-A/B/C/D apoprotein mixture from type II alveolar cells; reduces T; raises C; prevents alveolar collapse; minimizes alveolar edema; brakes rapid expansion and rapid contraction → uniform ventilation.
- **DPPC (dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine / dipalmitoyl lecithin)** — the principal surface-active lipid of surfactant.
- **Type I / type II alveolar cells** — thin gas-exchange / cuboidal surfactant-producing.
- **Lamellar bodies / tubular myelin** — packaging and intermediate-form structures of surfactant.
- **IRDS (infant respiratory distress syndrome)** — surfactant-deficiency disease of preterm infants; treated with exogenous surfactant + ventilation.
- **ARDS (acute / adult respiratory distress syndrome)** — acquired surfactant inactivation / alveolar inflammation; ↓C, hypoxemia, diffuse infiltrates.
- **Pneumothorax** — air in intrapleural space → $P_{ip} \to P_B$ → atelectasis.
- **Atelectasis** — alveolar collapse.
- **Hysteresis** — different inflation vs deflation paths on the P_TP–V_L curve.
- **Bucket-handle effect / water-pump-handle effect** — rib-rotation contributions to transverse and A-P thoracic diameter during inspiration.
- **Mechanical tethering / interdependence** — radial pull of alveolar walls on adjacent conducting airways; helps keep small airways open during expiration; lost in emphysema.
- **Equal pressure point (EPP)** — location along an expiratory airway where P_aw = P_ip → P_TM = 0; distal: open; proximal: compressed.
- **Dynamic airway compression** — collapse of conducting airways proximal to the EPP during forced expiration.
- **Effort-independent flow** — maximal expiratory flow at low V_L set by the envelope of dynamic compression, not by P_A.
- **Pursed-lip / puffing breathing** — emphysema coping strategy that moves the EPP to the lips by adding mouth resistance.
- **Asthma vs COPD vs fibrosis mechanical signatures** — asthma: ↑R_aw, normal C_static, steep frequency-dependent C_dynamic drop; COPD: ↑R_aw + ↑C_static; fibrosis: ↓C_static, normal R_aw.
- **α₁-antitrypsin deficiency** — inherited COPD risk factor (Table 18-1).
- **Vagal M3 / sympathetic β2 / histamine / leukotrienes** — humoral modulators of R_aw.
- **Atropine / β2-agonists / inhaled corticosteroids / theophylline / leukotriene receptor antagonists** — pharmacological levers on R_aw and airway inflammation.
- **Work of breathing W_breath** = ∫ P_ip dV_L = W_elastic + W_resistive — the area of the inspiratory P-V loop. Restrictive patients minimize the elastic component by breathing fast and shallow; obstructive patients minimize the resistive component by breathing slow and deep.
- **Diaphragm (C3–C5 phrenic) / parasternal internal intercostals / external intercostals (rostral-dorsal subset) / accessory muscles (scalenes, SCM, neck-back, upper airway)** — inspiratory muscles.
- **Abdominal muscles / triangularis sterni / caudal internal intercostals / caudal-ventral external intercostals** — forced-expiratory muscles.
