# Chapter 18 — Blood

> Section IV · The Cardiovascular System · pp. 429–446 · Author: Emile L. Boulpaep
> **Second chapter of Section IV.** This chapter shifts the gaze from the vessel (Ch 17) to the **circulating tissue itself**. It defines the cellular and protein composition of blood, the hematopoietic lineage tree that produces it, the **anomalous (non-newtonian) viscosity** of whole blood — the violation of Poiseuille assumption #6 deferred from Ch 17 — and the dual machinery of **hemostasis** (platelet plug + coagulation cascade) and **fibrinolysis** that keeps blood liquid inside vessels but solid outside them. Full O₂/CO₂ transport by hemoglobin is deferred to Ch 29.

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Blood composition** (pp. 429–436) — plasma and formed elements; hematocrit; plasma proteins (albumin, globulins, fibrinogen); hematopoiesis and lineage cytokines; erythrocytes (hemoglobin, 2,3-DPG, AE1, AQP1); leukocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes, monocytes); platelets.
2. **Blood viscosity** (pp. 436–439) — anomalous viscosity of whole blood; dependence on fibrinogen, hematocrit, vessel radius (Fåhraeus–Lindqvist), velocity, and temperature; axial accumulation; tank treading; bolus flow.
3. **Hemostasis and fibrinolysis** (pp. 439–446) — vasoconstriction; platelet plug (adhesion, activation, aggregation); coagulation cascade (intrinsic, extrinsic, common pathways); thrombin's central role; anticoagulants (TFPI, antithrombin III, thrombomodulin, proteins C and S); fibrinolysis (plasminogen → plasmin via t-PA / u-PA; PAI-1/2; α₂-antiplasmin).

## Section 1 — Blood composition (pp. 429–436)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Whole blood is a suspension of cellular elements in plasma** (p. 429)
- **Bone marrow is the source of most blood cells** (p. 431)
- **RBCs are mainly composed of hemoglobin** (pp. 433–435)
- **Leukocytes defend against infections** (p. 435)
- **Platelets are nucleus-free fragments** (pp. 435–436)

### Core claims

**Plasma vs. formed elements.**
- Blood = **plasma** (extracellular fluid rich in proteins) + **formed elements** (RBCs, WBCs, platelets). Total blood volume ~70 mL/kg (adult woman), ~80 mL/kg (adult man).
- After centrifugation (~10,000 g for ~5 min) with anticoagulant: bottom = packed RBCs, middle = whitish-gray **buffy coat** (WBCs + platelets), top = clear plasma (Fig. 18-1).
- **Hematocrit** = fraction of total column occupied by RBCs. Normal: ~40% (adult ♀), ~45% (adult ♂); newborn ~55%, falls to ~35% at 2 months, rises to adult values by puberty. Hematocrit measures RBC **concentration**, not total RBC mass; pregnancy lowers Hct via plasma-volume expansion; acute hemorrhage may leave Hct transiently normal because both plasma and RBCs are lost together.
- Total RBC volume ~28 mL/kg (♀), ~36 mL/kg (♂).
- **Serum** = plasma minus fibrinogen and other clotting factors. A plasma clot does not retract because it lacks platelets; a whole-blood clot retracts via platelet actin/myosin and expels serum.

**Plasma proteins.**
- Total protein ~7.0 g/dL → colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure ~25 mm Hg (anchor for Starling forces in Ch 20). Molecular weights up to ~970 kDa (IgM).
- **Albumin** 3.5–5.5 g/dL; synthesized in liver ~120 mg/kg/day; t½ ≈ 20 days; total pool ~135 g. Falls in hepatic cirrhosis; synthesis up-regulated by low plasma oncotic pressure. Carrier for T₃, T₄, steroids, bilirubin, bile salts, fatty acids.
- **Fibrinogen** 150–300 mg/dL, made only by liver; a heterotrimer dimer (Aα, Bβ, γ chains); cleaved by thrombin → fibrin polymer. Acute-phase response (Box 18-1) sharply raises fibrinogen.
- **Globulins** (= total protein − albumin − fibrinogen). Electrophoresis (Fig. 18-2) separates them by mobility into α₁-, α₂-, β-, and γ-globulin bands. γ-globulins = immunoglobulins (IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, IgM), made by B lymphocytes / plasma cells. Liver makes most non-Ig globulins and clotting factors.
- Carrier proteins not in Table 18-1: ceruloplasmin (Cu), transcobalamin (B12), CBG (corticosteroids), IGF-binding proteins, SHBG/TeBG (sex steroids), thyroid-binding globulin (T4), vitamin D-binding protein.

**Hematopoiesis.**
- All blood cells derive from **pluripotent long-term hematopoietic stem cells (LT-HSCs)** in bone marrow — multipotent and self-renewing.
- LT-HSC → **ST-HSC** → committed progenitors → **BFUs** / **CFUs** → mature cells (Fig. 18-4).
- Two large branches: **CMP** (common myeloid progenitor, also called CFU-GEMM) and **CLP** (common lymphoid progenitor).
- Lineage cytokines (colony-stimulating factors / interleukins; work of Donald Metcalf):
  - **GM-CSF** — glycoprotein driving CMP → neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes/macrophages. Recombinant = sargramostim (Leukine), used after bone-marrow transplant and in some acute leukemias.
  - **G-CSF** — drives granulocyte development. Recombinant = filgrastim (Neupogen), used for chemotherapy-induced neutropenia.
  - **M-CSF** — drives monocyte / macrophage / dendritic-cell development; also required for osteoclast development (Ch 52).
  - **IL-3** (multi-CSF) — broad lineage effects; constitutive from liver and kidney.
  - **IL-5** — terminal differentiation of eosinophilic precursors.
  - **TPO (thrombopoietin)** — binds c-Mpl receptor (homolog of viral v-mpl); expands megakaryocyte number and size → platelets.
  - **EPO (erythropoietin)** — kidney (and to a lesser extent liver); homologous to TPO; up-regulated by HIF-1α under hypoxia (Ch 5, pp. 93–94); essential for BFU-E → CFU-E (proerythroblast) transition. Recombinant EPO treats anemia.

**Erythropoiesis.**
- Lineage (Fig. 18-5): ST-HSC → CMP → MEP (megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitor) → BFU-E → CFU-E (proerythroblast) → basophilic erythroblast → **polychromatic erythroblast** (hemoglobin first appears) → **orthochromatic erythroblast** (full hemoglobin complement; exocytoses nucleus) → **reticulocyte** (loses mitochondria and ribosomes) → mature erythrocyte.
- Mature RBC lifespan ~120 days. Reticulocytes appear in circulation when erythropoiesis is heavily activated (useful clinical index).

**The erythrocyte itself.**
- Non-nucleated biconcave disc, diameter ~7.5 µm, volume ~90 fL.
- Cytoskeleton anchored to the membrane via **glycophorin** and **band 3 (Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger AE1)** (see Fig. 2-9).
- Biconcave shape maximizes surface-to-volume ratio → maximizes diffusion area, minimizes intracellular diffusion distances. Three tasks: (1) carry O₂ lung → tissues; (2) carry CO₂ tissues → lung; (3) buffer acids and bases.
- **Hemoglobin** content: ~14.0 g/dL (♀), ~15.5 g/dL (♂); intracellular concentration ~5.5 mM; **MCHC** ~35 g/dL RBCs (~5× plasma protein concentration). Hemoglobin synthesis begins in proerythroblast and finishes at the orthochromatic-erythroblast stage. Structure and O₂/CO₂ carriage = Ch 29.
- No nucleus or organelles → no protein synthesis; no oxidative metabolism. Energy from **glycolysis** (90% of glucose) and **pentose-phosphate shunt** (10%). ATP is exclusively glycolytic.
- **2,3-DPG**: cytosolic ~4–5 mM (about [Hb]); made by DPG mutase from 1,3-DPG; reduces O₂ affinity of hemoglobin (pp. 654–655; right-shifts ODC).
- **Glutathione** ~2 mM (more than any cell except hepatocyte). High GSH/GSSG ratio protects against oxidant damage; glutathione reductase regenerates GSH using NADPH from the pentose-shunt pathway.
- **Carbonic anhydrase** isoforms **CA I** and **CA II** rapidly interconvert CO₂ ⇌ HCO₃⁻; CA II has one of the fastest known turnover numbers.
- **AE1 (band 3)**: most abundant RBC membrane protein (~10⁶ copies/cell); transports up to 50,000 ions/sec; blocked by **DIDS**; supports the Hamburger / chloride shift critical for CO₂ carriage (pp. 124–125, 656).
- **AQP1**: second most abundant (~2 × 10⁵ copies/cell); contributes more than half of the RBC membrane's CO₂ permeability.

**Leukocytes.**
- Two broad classes: **granulocytes** (cytoplasmic granules visible on Wright/Giemsa stain) and **agranulocytes** (lymphocytes, monocytes). Granulocytes = neutrophils + eosinophils + basophils; the umbrella term **polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs)** often refers specifically to neutrophils.
- Diameters: lymphocyte 6–10 µm < basophil ~11 µm < neutrophil ~12 µm < eosinophil ~13 µm < monocyte 14–20 µm < tissue macrophage 20–40 µm.
- Granulocytes have a brief intravascular life (< 12 h) and on activation migrate into tissues.

  - **Neutrophils** (most abundant WBC). Identified by nuclear shape: **segmented** (mature; ≥ 2 lobes connected by a thin filament; ~56% of WBCs) vs. **band** (immature; uniform nuclear thickness; ~3%). Two granule types: **specific** and **azurophilic** — lysosomal enzymes, peroxidase, collagenase. Chemotaxis → phagocytic vacuole → **degranulation** → kill via H₂O₂ and superoxide O₂•⁻ (p. 1238).
  - **Eosinophils**. Granules contain **major basic protein (MBP)** and other enzymes; toxic to parasites and viruses; mediators of allergic reactions.
  - **Basophils** (least common). Granules nearly obscure the nucleus and store **histamine, heparin, peroxidase**. Major source of **IL-4** that stimulates B cells to make IgE. Allergic-reaction mediators.
  - **Lymphocytes**. Agranular. Lineage commitment in bone marrow.
    - **T cells** (70–80% of peripheral lymphocytes): thymic maturation; cell-mediated immunity.
    - **B cells** (10–15%): mature in bone marrow + peripheral lymphoid tissue; with antigen + T-cell + macrophage help → plasma cells secreting antigen-specific antibodies; humoral immunity.
    - Remainder includes **natural killer (NK) cells**.
  - **Monocytes**. Spend most of their long life in tissues as **macrophages** (20–40 µm). Two jobs: phagocytosis of pathogens / debris and antigen presentation to lymphocytes.

**Platelets (thrombocytes).**
- Anucleate disc-shaped cytoplasmic fragments, 2–3 µm; bud from megakaryocytes under TPO + IL-3 control. Each megakaryocyte produces up to a few thousand platelets.
- Normal count 150,000–450,000 / µL.
- **TPO feedback loop**: platelets carry TPO receptors and clear plasma TPO. Hypoplastic marrow → thrombocytopenia → less TPO clearance → TPO rises → megakaryopoiesis up. Hyperplastic marrow → thrombocytosis → high TPO clearance → megakaryopoiesis down.
- Lifespan ~10 days.
- Internal structure (Fig. 18-6): circumferential **microtubule** band (tubulin) as inner skeleton; actin/myosin filaments; mitochondria, lysosomes, peroxisomes; an open **canalicular system**; two special granule types:
  - **α granules** — vWF, **platelet fibrinogen** (endocytosed from plasma, originally liver-made), and **clotting factor V**.
  - **Dense-core granules** — ATP, ADP, **serotonin (5-HT)**, **Ca²⁺**.
- External coat carries platelet receptors (mainly integrin family glycoproteins).

### Clinical box — Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (Box 18-1, p. 430)
- Almost any acute stress (trauma, infection, disease) triggers an **acute-phase response**: within hours, inflammatory cytokines drive the liver to synthesize fibrinogen and other acute-phase proteins.
- High fibrinogen → RBCs **rouleaux** (cluster) → effectively denser RBC stacks → faster gravitational fall in an anticoagulated blood column.
- **ESR** = mm of clear plasma left at top after 1 hour. Normal < 15 mm; > 40 mm in many inflammatory disorders.
- Nonspecific but cheap; widely used in primary care, e.g., to track disease activity in lupus erythematosus.

## Section 2 — Blood viscosity (pp. 436–439)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Whole blood has an anomalous viscosity** (p. 436)
- **Blood viscosity increases with the hematocrit and the fibrinogen plasma concentration** (pp. 436–439)

### Core claims

**Anomalous (non-newtonian) viscosity of whole blood.**
- This section pays the deferred sixth Poiseuille assumption from Ch 17 (constant viscosity, newtonian fluid).
- Water and saline are **newtonian**: shear stress = η · shear rate is a straight line through the origin (Fig. 18-7, blue). Plasma and serum (no cells, no rouleaux) are very nearly newtonian (yellow).
- Whole blood is **non-newtonian**: shear stress vs. shear rate is nonlinear and intercepts the y-axis above the origin (red curve in Fig. 18-7). The intercept is the **yield shear stress** — a threshold below which the fluid does not move at all.
- At high shear rates blood becomes effectively newtonian with slope corresponding to η ≈ **3.2 cP** at typical hematocrit (40%) and fibrinogen (260 mg/dL), 37 °C.
- **Honey vs. mayonnaise analogy:** honey is newtonian (drips off the spoon under gravity); mayonnaise has a finite yield shear stress (sticks to the spoon). Blood behaves like mayonnaise at low driving force.

**Five physiological determinants of effective whole-blood viscosity:** (1) fibrinogen, (2) hematocrit, (3) vessel radius, (4) linear velocity, (5) temperature.

1. **Fibrinogen.** The non-newtonian behavior arises from **fibrinogen-mediated RBC interactions**: plasma alone is newtonian; washed RBCs in saline are newtonian; fibrinogen + RBCs together is non-newtonian. Congenital afibrinogenemia abolishes the yield shear stress; hyperfibrinogenemia raises it (and raises ESR via rouleaux clustering). Fibrinogen levels rise with age and with smoking.
2. **Hematocrit.** Two regimes (Fig. 18-8):
   - **Low–normal Hct (up to ~60%):** viscosity rises with Hct mainly through fibrinogen-mediated and direct RBC-RBC adhesion ("stickiness").
   - **Hct > 60%:** RBCs are so tightly packed that further increases cause **cell deformation**, which raises viscosity steeply. **Polycythemia** produces both high viscosity and a > 4-fold increase in yield shear stress. The combination of high Hct + high fibrinogen produces extreme viscosities.
3. **Vessel radius — Fåhraeus–Lindqvist effect.** For tube radius > ~1 mm, viscosity is independent of radius; below that, viscosity falls steeply as radius decreases (Fig. 18-9). Four causes:
   - **Axial accumulation of RBCs** (Fig. 18-10A). Poiseuille first observed it: RBCs caught between sliding plasma laminae spin and migrate toward the axis (like a struck-off-center billiard ball curving). The wall is lined with a "transparent space" of plasma. Net effect: low-viscosity plasma takes the wall (high shear) and high-viscosity RBC core takes the axis (low shear) → overall viscosity falls.
   - **Plasma skimming**: branch vessels preferentially draw plasma off the wall layer, lowering branch Hct. Some sites resist skimming via an **arterial cushion**.
   - **Breakdown of Poiseuille's infinite-lamina assumption**: a fluid lamina can be no thinner than an erythrocyte, so in small vessels there are too few laminae for the textbook Poiseuille viscosity (Fig. 18-10B).
   - **Tank treading and bolus flow** (Fig. 18-10C): in capillaries of erythrocyte diameter, the RBC membrane rolls around the cytoplasm like a bulldozer track, and two tank-treading cells spin a trapped plasma bolus between them.
   - **Cell deformation in sub-erythrocyte capillaries**: bullet-shaped RBCs squeeze through, separated from the wall by a thin plasma film, "focusing" to the centerline (Fig. 18-10D). Effective viscosity drops further.
4. **Velocity of flow.** Plasma's pressure-flow curve is linear (Fig. 18-11, gold). Whole blood deviates from linearity near zero (Fig. 18-11, red), worse for polycythemia (green). Two reasons: (a) at low shear rate the non-newtonian regime applies and apparent viscosity is high; (b) RBC axial accumulation requires some flow to develop, so at low flow the cell-poor plasma layer at the wall hasn't formed and viscosity is high.
5. **Temperature.** Cooling whole blood from 37 °C to 0 °C raises viscosity ~2.5-fold. Negligible in humans except during intense cooling of the extremities. **Cryoglobulins** (immunoglobulins that precipitate below 37 °C; associated with hepatitis C, autoimmune and lymphoproliferative disease) can raise viscosity catastrophically on modest cooling → distal vessel obstruction and thrombosis.

### Equations

- **Newton's definition of viscosity** (re-stated from Ch 17 Eq. 17-12):

$$\eta = \frac{\text{shear stress}}{\text{shear rate}} = \frac{F/A}{\Delta v / \Delta x}$$

- **Whole-blood (Bingham-fluid) constitutive relation** (schematized from Fig. 18-7):

$$\tau = \tau_y + \eta_\infty \cdot \dot\gamma \qquad (\dot\gamma > \dot\gamma_{\text{yield}})$$

  where $\tau$ = shear stress, $\tau_y$ = yield shear stress, $\eta_\infty$ ≈ 3.2 cP, $\dot\gamma$ = shear rate. Below $\dot\gamma_{\text{yield}}$, flow is effectively zero.

- **Apparent viscosity at typical conditions:** $\eta_{\text{whole blood}} \approx 3.2$ cP (Hct 40%, fibrinogen 260 mg/dL, 37 °C); $\eta_{\text{plasma}} \approx 1.8$ cP; $\eta_{\text{water}} \approx 1.0$ cP.

- **Polycythemia / cooling scaling factors:** Hct → 60–70% can raise effective η by an order of magnitude relative to Hct 40%; 37 °C → 0 °C cooling raises η ~2.5×.

#### Figure 18-7 — Anomalous viscosity of blood *(listed)*

A square plot of √(shear stress) (vertical axis) vs. √(shear rate) (horizontal axis) — the square roots are used to flatten the curves and make the deviation from linearity easy to read. Three curves: **plasma** (yellow line, straight through origin); **normal whole blood** (red curve, hits the y-axis at a positive **yield shear stress** intercept, curves upward at low shear, then merges into a straight high-flow segment of slope ~ 3.2 cP); **polycythemia** (red dashed, even higher intercept and steeper). A vertical zone labeled "Low flow (non-newtonian)" sits at left and a "High flow (newtonian)" zone sits at right.

## Section 3 — Hemostasis and fibrinolysis (pp. 439–446)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Platelets can plug holes in small vessels** (pp. 439–440)
- **A controlled cascade of proteolysis creates a blood clot** (pp. 440–444)
- **Anticoagulants keep the clotting network in check** (pp. 444–445)
- **Fibrinolysis breaks up clots** (pp. 445–446)

### Core claims

**Why blood stays liquid in vessels.**
- Blood remains liquid intravascularly because (i) it does not contact negatively charged surfaces (e.g., collagen beneath endothelium) that would activate the intrinsic coagulation pathway, (ii) it does not contact tissue factor that would activate the extrinsic pathway, and (iii) **thrombolytic pathways** (plasmin from plasminogen) continuously trim any low-grade fibrin formation.

**The four arms of hemostasis (Greek *hemos* + *stasis* = "blood standing"):**
1. **Vasoconstriction.** Raises critical closing pressure (p. 454) → vessels with intravascular pressure below this collapse. Triggered by platelet-released **TXA₂** and **5-HT**, and by thrombin-driven endothelial **endothelin-1** (the most powerful physiological vasoconstrictor).
2. **Increased tissue pressure.** Lowers transmural pressure → vessel radius shrinks → flow drops with the $r^4$ Poiseuille scaling. Halving radius cuts flow 16-fold. The physical principle of pressing a finger on a cut, of a tourniquet, and of a surgical hemostatic clamp.
3. **Platelet plug formation.**
4. **Coagulation (clot formation).**

The next three subsections develop arms 3, 4, and their counter-regulation.

**Platelet plug formation: three sequential steps.**

- **Adhesion.** Quiescent platelets do not adhere to each other, to other blood cells, or to endothelium — partly because both carry negative surface charges (endothelium via **heparan sulfate** proteoglycans). Adhesion is triggered by elevated shear or by vessel injury / humoral signals.
  - Adhesion is integrin-mediated. **von Willebrand factor (vWF)** — made by endothelial cells (stored in **Weibel-Palade bodies**) and megakaryocytes (stored in α granules) — is released under high shear, certain cytokines, or hypoxia. vWF binds platelet **Gp Ib/Ia** (a Gp Ib + Gp Ia heterodimer).
  - When the endothelium is breached, exposed subendothelial matrix ligands engage platelet integrins: **collagen** binds **Gp Ia/IIa**; **fibronectin** and **laminin** bind **Gp Ic/IIa**.
- **Activation (release reaction).** Ligand binding triggers a conformational change in receptors → intracellular signaling via **PLC** (Ch 3) → Ca²⁺ influx → exocytosis of:
  - **Dense-core granule contents:** ATP, ADP, 5-HT, Ca²⁺.
  - **α-granule contents:** growth factors plus three hemostatic proteins — vWF, factor V, fibrinogen.
  - Cyclooxygenase-driven release of **thromboxane A₂** (TXA₂) from arachidonic acid.
  - Cytoskeletal remodeling — broad **lamellipodium** then finger-like **filopodia**.
- **Aggregation.** Released ADP (on platelet **P2Y12**), 5-HT, and TXA₂ recruit more platelets — positive feedback. **Aspirin** blocks COX → less TXA₂ → reduced aggregation. **Clopidogrel** blocks P2Y12. Activation also induces a conformational change in **Gp IIb/IIIa** so that it binds fibrinogen; fibrinogen now bridges between platelets to form the plug. Thrombin, when present, cleaves fibrinogen → fibrin and stabilizes the plug.

**Anti- vs. pro-thrombotic balance.** The intact endothelium is antithrombotic; vascular damage shifts the balance toward prothrombotic by (i) loss of antithrombotic factor production and (ii) exposure of blood to subendothelial thrombogenic factors. Mechanical heart valves can activate platelets purely by shear.

**The classical two-branch coagulation cascade with a common pathway (Fig. 18-12).**
- **Zymogen-to-protease** logic: most clotting factors are inactive serine-protease precursors; each cleavage activates the next in line; **controlled proteolysis** amplifies a small initiating signal.
- Reactions do not run in the fluid phase. The intrinsic cascade runs on the membrane of activated platelets; the extrinsic cascade runs at membrane-bound tissue factor.
- Roman-numeral factor names: I (fibrinogen), II (prothrombin), III (tissue factor), IV (Ca²⁺), V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, XII, XIII, plus HMWK, prekallikrein, vWF (see Table 18-4).
- Common protein domain architecture: signal peptide → propeptide (Gla domain, γ-carboxyglutamate-rich, Ca²⁺-binding, requires **vitamin K**) → EGF-like domain → kringle domain → catalytic (serine-protease) domain.

**Intrinsic pathway (surface-contact activation, left branch of Fig. 18-12).**
- Contact with a negatively charged surface (glass *in vitro*; activated-platelet membrane *in vivo*) converts factor **XII** → **XIIa**.
- **HMWK** (high-molecular-weight kininogen) anchors factor XII to the charged surface as cofactor; the HMWK-mediated XII → XIIa conversion is slow.
- A trickle of XIIa converts **prekallikrein** → **kallikrein** (also HMWK-anchored); kallikrein then accelerates more XII → XIIa (**positive feedback**). Kallikrein cleaving HMWK also releases vasodilatory **kinins** (pp. 553–554).
- XIIa cleaves factor **XI** → **XIa**; XIa cleaves factor **IX** (Christmas factor) → **IXa**.
- Factors IXa, Xa, and especially thrombin cleave factor **VIII** → **VIIIa** (a cofactor, not a protease).
- Factor IXa + VIIIa + Ca²⁺ + negatively charged phospholipid (mostly from activated platelets) assemble into a trimolecular complex called **tenase**.
- **Tenase converts X → Xa.**

**Extrinsic pathway (tissue-factor activation, right branch of Fig. 18-12).**
- Vascular injury allows plasma factor **VII** to contact **tissue factor (TF / thromboplastin / factor III)** — a constitutively expressed integral-membrane protein on nonvascular cells. TF non-proteolytically converts VII → **VIIa**.
- The trimolecular complex **TF + VIIa + Ca²⁺** is analogous to tenase and proteolyzes **X → Xa**.
- Once factor X binds the TF + VIIa + Ca²⁺ complex, factor VIIa cannot dissociate (allosteric trapping).

**Common pathway (center of Fig. 18-12).**
- Whether Xa came from intrinsic or extrinsic origin, the cascade continues identically.
- Thrombin clips factor **V** → **Va** (cofactor). V and VIII are homologous; both go from a single chain into two-peptide active forms held together non-covalently.
- **Prothrombinase** = factor Xa + Va + Ca²⁺ + phospholipid.
- Prothrombinase cleaves **prothrombin (II) → thrombin (IIa)**.

**Thrombin — the central protease.** Three classes of action:
1. **Downstream activation.** Cleaves Aα and Bβ chains of **fibrinogen**, releasing **fibrinopeptides A and B** and yielding soluble **fibrin monomers** (α, β, γ chains). Monomers polymerize spontaneously into a fibrin gel that traps blood cells. Thrombin then activates factor **XIII → XIIIa**, a transglutaminase that covalently cross-links α and γ chains across fibrin polymers → **stable fibrin**.
2. **Upstream positive feedback.** Thrombin catalyzes more prothrombin → thrombin and converts V → Va, VIII → VIIIa.
3. **Paracrine actions.** Triggers endothelial release of NO, PGI₂, ADP, vWF, and **t-PA** (a fibrinolytic). Activates platelets through **PAR-1** (a G-protein–coupled protease-activated receptor; pp. 51–52). Thrombin is the molecular bridge between platelet plug formation and clotting.

**The "connected diagram" view.** The intrinsic/extrinsic dichotomy is increasingly viewed as obsolete: (TF + VIIa + Ca²⁺) of the extrinsic cascade also activates IX and XII of the intrinsic; intrinsic IXa and Xa can activate factor VII of the extrinsic. Clinically, coagulation *in vivo* depends largely on the **extrinsic** pathway. Tissue factor is normally absent from intravascular cells, but inflammation can drive monocytes and endothelium to express TF — the molecular basis of disseminated intravascular coagulation in **sepsis**.

**Endogenous anticoagulants (Fig. 18-13; Table 18-4).** Five molecules from healthy endothelium keep clotting in check:
1. **Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI)** — plasma protein, GPI-linked to endothelium; binds and inactivates the TF + VIIa + Ca²⁺ complex; primary brake on the extrinsic pathway.
2. **Antithrombin III (AT III)** — serpin family serine-protease inhibitor; inhibits Xa and thrombin (and probably XIIa, XIa, IXa). **Heparan sulfate** (on the external surface of most cells, including endothelium) and **heparin** (released by mast cells and basophils) enhance AT III binding to its targets — the molecular basis of heparin's clinical anticoagulant action.
3. **Thrombomodulin** — endothelial glycosaminoglycan; binds and removes thrombin from circulation; the bound thrombin loses its procoagulant activity and acquires the ability to activate protein C.
4. **Protein C** → activated by the thrombomodulin-thrombin complex to **protein Ca (APC)**. With cofactor **protein S**, APC proteolytically inactivates Va and VIIIa, throttling the common and intrinsic pathways.
5. **Protein S** — cofactor for protein C. Both C and S require vitamin K for hepatic synthesis (basis of warfarin anticoagulation; congenital deficiency → hypercoagulable state).
- Additional brake: clearance of activated factors by hepatic **Kupffer cells**.

**Fibrinolysis (Fig. 18-14; Table 18-5).** Once a stable-fibrin clot has formed and the breach has been sealed, the clot must be dissolved before it embolizes or chronically occludes the vessel. The process is **thrombolysis**, mediated by the protease **plasmin**.

- **Plasminogen** (liver-made; large single-chain glycoprotein with five kringles on the heavy chain and a serine-protease domain on the light chain) is converted to **plasmin** by one of two activators: **t-PA** or **u-PA**.
- **Tissue-type plasminogen activator (t-PA)** — serine protease made by endothelial cells; single chain with two N-terminal kringles + C-terminal protease motif. Catecholamines and bradykinin elevate circulating t-PA. Fibrin itself **greatly accelerates** the t-PA-driven cleavage of plasminogen to plasmin (substrate-targeting via the kringles). Recombinant t-PA (alteplase) is the clinical thrombolytic for acute ischemic stroke and STEMI.
- **Urokinase-type plasminogen activator (u-PA)** — serine protease in plasma as single- or two-chain forms. To work, u-PA must bind its receptor **u-PAR** on the cell surface.
- **Plasmin** — kringles anchor to lysine residues on fibrin → close proximity to the protease domain → hydrolysis of stable fibrin into **fibrin breakdown products** (D-dimers; clinical marker of active fibrinolysis). Plasmin can also cleave t-PA.
- **Inhibitory serpins** counter-regulate fibrinolysis:
  - **PAI-1** — endothelial; binds and inhibits both single- and two-chain t-PA, and u-PA. 1:1 complex with t-PA in blood.
  - **PAI-2** — placental in origin; mainly inhibits u-PA. Elevated in pregnancy and may contribute to pregnancy-associated hypercoagulability.
  - **α₂-antiplasmin (α₂-AP)** — liver/kidney; the only serpin that targets free plasmin. Bound plasmin (i.e., fixed to fibrin via its kringles) is *protected* from α₂-AP — so the clot itself shields the protease responsible for its dissolution. **Implication: the very presence of a clot promotes its own breakdown.**
- Activated protein C also inhibits PAI-1 and PAI-2, so the same molecule that brakes coagulation accelerates fibrinolysis.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Hematocrit (Hct)** — fraction of blood column occupied by RBCs (normal ~40% ♀ / ~45% ♂).

- **Oncotic / colloid osmotic pressure** — ~25 mm Hg in plasma; mostly albumin.
- **Albumin / globulins / fibrinogen** — three main plasma protein groups.
- **Acute-phase response / ESR** — inflammation-driven fibrinogen rise → RBC rouleaux → faster sedimentation.
- **Hematopoiesis** — formation of all blood-cell lineages from LT-HSCs in bone marrow.
- **LT-HSC / ST-HSC / CMP / CLP / MEP / GMP** — hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells.
- **BFU / CFU** — burst-forming unit / colony-forming unit (committed progenitors).
- **Lineage cytokines** — GM-CSF, G-CSF, M-CSF, IL-3, IL-5, TPO, EPO.
- **EPO** — kidney-made; HIF-1α-driven; required for BFU-E → CFU-E.
- **TPO** — c-Mpl receptor agonist; expands megakaryocytes.
- **Erythropoiesis stages** — proerythroblast (CFU-E) → basophilic → polychromatic → orthochromatic erythroblast → reticulocyte → erythrocyte.
- **MCV / MCH / MCHC / RDW** — RBC indices on a CBC.
- **2,3-DPG** — RBC glycolytic product that right-shifts the hemoglobin O₂ dissociation curve.

- **CA I, CA II** — RBC carbonic anhydrases; central to CO₂ carriage.
- **Glutathione (GSH/GSSG)** — RBC antioxidant system; GSH regenerated using NADPH from the pentose-phosphate shunt.
- **Granulocytes / agranulocytes** — granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) vs. lymphocytes + monocytes.
- **Neutrophils — band vs. segmented; left shift** — clinical marker of acute infection.
- **Eosinophils** — major basic protein; parasites and allergy.
- **Basophils / mast cells** — histamine, heparin; IL-4 → IgE.
- **T / B / NK lymphocytes**; **plasma cells**; cell-mediated vs. humoral immunity.
- **Monocyte → macrophage** transition in tissue; antigen presentation.
- **Platelet (thrombocyte)** — anucleate megakaryocyte fragment; 150–450 × 10³ / µL.
- **α granules vs. dense-core granules** — vWF/fibrinogen/V vs. ATP/ADP/5-HT/Ca²⁺.
- **vWF / Gp Ib/Ia / Gp IIb/IIIa / Gp Ia/IIa / Gp Ic/IIa** — platelet integrins and their ligands.
- **TXA₂ / cyclooxygenase / aspirin** — platelet activation amplifier; COX inhibition therapy.
- **ADP / P2Y12 / clopidogrel** — platelet activation amplifier; P2Y12 inhibition therapy.
- **PAR-1** — thrombin's protease-activated receptor on platelets.
- **Newtonian vs. non-newtonian fluid** — linear vs. nonlinear shear stress / shear rate.
- **Yield shear stress** — minimum shear stress needed to make whole blood flow.
- **Fåhraeus–Lindqvist effect** — apparent viscosity falls in vessels with radius < ~1 mm.
- **Axial accumulation / plasma skimming / arterial cushion** — small-vessel hematocrit redistribution.
- **Tank treading / bolus flow** — capillary-scale RBC dynamics.
- **Cryoglobulinemia** — pathological cold-induced rise in blood viscosity.
- **Hemostasis** — four pillars: vasoconstriction, tissue pressure, platelet plug, coagulation.
- **Platelet adhesion / activation / aggregation / release reaction**.
- **Endothelin-1** — strongest physiological vasoconstrictor; thrombin-stimulated.
- **Intrinsic pathway** — XII → XI → IX → (with VIIIa, Ca²⁺, phospholipid) = tenase → X.
- **Extrinsic pathway** — VII + tissue factor + Ca²⁺ → X.
- **Common pathway** — X → (with Va, Ca²⁺, phospholipid) = prothrombinase → thrombin → fibrin → (XIIIa) → stable fibrin.

- **Thrombin (IIa)** — central serine protease; downstream activation + positive feedback + paracrine signaling.
- **Vitamin K** — required for γ-carboxylation of II, VII, IX, X, protein C, protein S; warfarin target.
- **Gla domain / EGF-like domain / kringle domain / catalytic (serine-protease) domain** — modular architecture.
- **PT / aPTT / TT** — lab tests of the extrinsic + common, intrinsic + common, and thrombin → fibrin steps respectively.
- **Hemophilia A (VIII)**, **Hemophilia B / Christmas disease (IX)**, **von Willebrand disease**.
- **Anticoagulants** — TFPI, antithrombin III (heparin-potentiated), thrombomodulin, protein C / Ca, protein S.
- **Heparin / heparan sulfate** — endogenous and pharmacologic potentiators of AT III.
- **Warfarin** — vitamin K antagonist.
- **DOACs** — direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, rivaroxaban target Xa; dabigatran targets thrombin).
- **Fibrinolysis / thrombolysis** — plasmin-driven clot dissolution.
- **Plasminogen / plasmin** — fibrin-anchored serine protease.
- **t-PA / u-PA / u-PAR** — plasminogen activators; recombinant t-PA = alteplase.
- **PAI-1 / PAI-2 / α₂-antiplasmin** — serpin brakes on the fibrinolytic cascade.
- **Fibrin breakdown products / D-dimer** — clinical marker of active fibrinolysis.
- **DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation)** — sepsis-driven monocyte/endothelial tissue-factor expression triggering uncontrolled intravascular thrombosis and consumption coagulopathy.
