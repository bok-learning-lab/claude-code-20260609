# Chapter 16 — Circuits of the Central Nervous System

> Section III · The Nervous System · pp. 390–407 · Author: Barry W. Connors

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Elements of neural circuits** (pp. 390–392) — levels of organization (systems, local circuits, microcircuits); the three canonical components of a local circuit (input axons, interneurons, projection neurons); examples from spinal ventral horn and neocortex.
2. **Simple, stereotyped responses: spinal reflex circuits** (pp. 392–396) — the monosynaptic stretch (myotatic) reflex; Golgi tendon organ (Ib) reflex; flexion-withdrawal and crossed-extensor reflexes; brain-stem and cortical modulation of spinal reflexes.
3. **Rhythmic activity: central pattern generators** (pp. 396–399) — motor programs; spinal CPGs for locomotion; half-center model; lamprey CPG with sensory feedback and intersegmental coordination.
4. **Spatial representations: sensory and motor maps in the brain** (pp. 399–405) — retinotopy and V1 magnification; ocular dominance columns, blobs, V2 stripes; somatotopy (Penfield homunculus) and motor map; polysensory maps in the superior colliculus; map plasticity after deafferentation.
5. **Temporal representations: time-measuring circuits** (pp. 405–407) — sound localization; interaural intensity differences (high frequency) vs. interaural delay (low frequency); the Jeffress delay-line / coincidence-detection model in the medial superior olive (MSO).

## Section 1 — Elements of neural circuits (pp. 390–392)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Neural circuits process sensory information, generate motor output, and create spontaneous activity** (p. 390)
- **Nervous systems have several levels of organization** (pp. 390–391)
- **Most local circuits have three elements: input axons, interneurons, and projection (output) neurons** (pp. 391–392)

### Core claims
- A **neural circuit** is any synaptically interconnected network of neurons; even primitive hydrozoan ring networks coordinate rhythmic swimming and switch defensive behaviors on shadow detection.
- **Three levels of organization** are useful: (i) **systems / pathways** — gross subsystems visible in brain anatomy (e.g., retina → LGN → V1 → higher cortex); (ii) **local circuits** — the input, output, and interneurons of a single brain region, often arranged as stereotyped modules; (iii) **microcircuits** — finer dendritic / synaptic arrangements within a local circuit that transform information over small regions of dendrites.
- Two prototype local circuits in this chapter (Figs. 16-1, 16-2):
  - **Spinal ventral horn**: input = primary afferent axons in dorsal roots + descending axons from brain + interspinal axons; output = α-motor neurons (and γ-motor neurons) projecting through ventral roots to skeletal muscle; interneurons include excitatory and inhibitory cells coordinating reflex and locomotor patterns.
  - **Neocortex**: input = thalamic relay axons (especially into layer IV) plus cortico-cortical axons from other cortical areas (including the contralateral hemisphere via corpus callosum); output = layer V pyramidal axons projecting to brainstem and spinal cord, and layer VI pyramidal axons projecting back to the thalamus; interneurons include both excitatory and inhibitory classes.
- **Two universal principles**: most local circuits receive **multiple types of input** and produce **multiple types of output**; rarely does a circuit have only input and output cells — most local processing depends on **interneurons (intrinsic neurons)**, which in many regions vastly outnumber the projection neurons. The cerebellum is the extreme case: ~$10^{11}$ granule cells (a type of excitatory interneuron) — more than all other neurons in the brain combined.
- **Variations** on the canonical scheme: a projection cell's axon can branch and stay within the local circuit (true of cortical pyramidal cells and spinal α-motor neurons); some interneurons lack a true axon and signal through short neurites or dendrites; some "inputs" are chemical (CO₂-sensitive medullary neurons) or physical (temperature-sensitive hypothalamic neurons) rather than synaptic.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "A neuron never works alone." (p. 390)
- > "Neuronal circuits that produce rhythmic motor output are sometimes called central pattern generators." (p. 390)
- > "Within a local brain region, the arrangement of neurons and their synaptic connections is called a local circuit." (p. 390)
- > "All local circuits have some form of input, which is usually a set of axons that originate elsewhere and terminate in synapses within the local circuit." (p. 391)
- > "Local processing is achieved by additional neurons whose axonal connections remain within the local circuit. These neurons are usually called interneurons or intrinsic neurons." (p. 391)

## Section 2 — Spinal reflex circuits (pp. 392–396)

### Subsection headings
- **Passive stretching of a skeletal muscle causes a reflexive contraction of that same muscle and relaxation of the antagonist muscles** (pp. 392–394)
- **Force applied to the Golgi tendon organ regulates muscle contractile strength** (pp. 394–395)
- **Noxious stimuli can evoke complex reflexive movements** (p. 394)
- **Spinal reflexes are strongly influenced by control centers within the brain** (pp. 394–396)

### Core claims — anatomy of the motor unit
- A **motor unit** = one α-motor neuron + all the extrafusal muscle fibers it innervates (see Ch 9). Each muscle fiber belongs to only one motor unit.
- Motor unit size is matched to function: **small** (a few fibers) in fine-control muscles (extraocular); **large** (up to several thousand fibers) in force-generating muscles (gastrocnemius).
- Two classes of motor neuron (see Table 12-1):
  - **α-motor neurons** — extrafusal force-generating fibers.
  - **γ-motor neurons** — intrafusal fibers of muscle spindles.
- The **motor neuron pool** = all motor neurons innervating one muscle.

### Core claims — stretch (myotatic) reflex
- Sherrington (1890s) defined the basic anatomical/physiological circuitry of spinal reflexes; the precision of his reflex timing measurements first led him to coin the term **synapse**.
- The stretch reflex is **monosynaptic** between sensory and motor neuron:
  - Stimulus: rapid muscle stretch (e.g., patellar-tendon tap pulls quadriceps).
  - Sensor: muscle-spindle primary endings → **group Ia axons** (large, fast; see p. 388).
  - Central synapse: a Ia axon makes a **direct excitatory synapse** on α-motor neurons of the homonymous muscle.
  - Effector: same (homonymous) muscle contracts → opposes the stretch.
- Cutting dorsal roots abolishes the reflex, proving its dependence on the sensory afferent.
- A parallel **disynaptic** branch achieves **reciprocal inhibition**: Ia collaterals excite Ia inhibitory interneurons that inhibit α-motor neurons of the antagonist muscle (e.g., relaxing semitendinosus when quadriceps contracts). This minimizes opposing force during the reflex.

### Core claims — Golgi tendon organ (Ib) reflex
- The **Golgi tendon organ** lies in series with the muscle at the muscle–tendon junction; it senses **tension** (active force) rather than length.
- Afferent: **group Ib axons** project polysynaptically onto inhibitory and excitatory interneurons in the cord.
- Classic effect: **inhibition** of α-motor neurons to the contracting muscle and **excitation** of antagonists — roughly the opposite of the stretch reflex; useful for force regulation and joint stability.
- The Ib reflex is context-dependent: during rapid locomotion, Ib input can **excite** the same-muscle motor neurons. The Ib interneurons receive convergent input from other sensory afferents, skin, and descending pathways, so the sign of the reflex is gated by behavior.

### Core claims — flexion-withdrawal and crossed-extensor reflexes
- Noxious cutaneous input (group **Aδ** fast pain afferents) triggers a **flexion-withdrawal reflex**: ipsilateral flexors excited, ipsilateral extensors inhibited, withdrawing the wounded limb.
- A **crossed-extensor** component on the opposite side does the reverse — extensors excited, flexors inhibited — to provide postural support while the wounded limb is lifted (Fig. 16-5).
- Both responses are mediated by sets of excitatory and inhibitory interneurons that project ipsilaterally and contralaterally; flexor reflex magnitude scales with stimulus intensity, and motor output spreads up and down the cord to orchestrate multi-segmental responses.

### Core claims — descending modulation
- Brainstem and cortical axons descend onto spinal interneurons (mostly) and motor neurons. They can **augment** or **suppress** reflexes depending on context:
  - **Jendrassik maneuver** — patient clasps and pulls hands; distraction releases descending suppression and exaggerates leg stretch reflexes (clinical exam trick).
  - **Anticipated catch** of a falling ball — co-contraction of stretched and antagonist muscles for ~60 ms around impact stiffens the arm (programmed override of the simple stretch reflex).
  - Reflex strength of leg stretch varies stride-by-stride during walking.
- Reflex circuits and locomotor circuits **share** interneurons; rhythmic walking can be conceptualized as alternating flexor-withdrawal / crossed-extensor patterns coupled to a pattern generator (see Section 3).

### Clinical anchor — Box 16-1 (Motor System Injury)
- **Lower motor neuron lesion** (motor neuron or its axon damaged): localized **paresis** (weakness) or **paralysis**, **areflexia**, loss of resting tone (**atonia / flaccidity**), and eventual **atrophy** from loss of trophic input.
- **Upper motor neuron lesion** (descending tracts damaged, e.g., spinal-cord transection, stroke, demyelinating disease):
  - Acute: **spinal shock** — flaccid areflexia below the lesion for days–weeks.
  - Chronic (after recovery from spinal shock): **spasticity** = **hypertonia** + **hyperreflexia**, attributed to chronically hyperexcitable spinal neurons driving tonically overactive stretch-reflex circuitry.
- Lesion topography by location: **paraplegia** (both legs), **hemiplegia** (one side), **quadriplegia** (legs + trunk + arms).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "A motor reflex is a rapid, stereotyped motor response to a particular sensory stimulus." (p. 392)
- > "Understanding of this simplest of reflexes, the stretch reflex or myotatic reflex, first requires knowledge of some anatomy." (p. 392)
- > "Stretching a muscle causes rapid feedback excitation of the same muscle through the minimum possible circuit: one sensory neuron, one central synapse, and one motor neuron." (p. 393)
- > "Branches of the group Ia sensory axons excite specific interneurons that inhibit the α-motor neurons of the antagonists. This reciprocal innervation increases the effectiveness of the stretch reflex." (p. 394)
- > "Tendon organs are aligned in series with the muscle; they are exquisitely sensitive to the tension within a tendon and thus respond to the force generated by the muscle rather than to muscle length." (p. 394)
- > "Reflexes do not simply correct for external perturbations of the body; in addition, they play a key role in the control of all movements." (p. 396)

## Section 3 — Central pattern generators (pp. 396–399)

### Subsection headings
- **Central pattern generators in the spinal cord can create a complex motor program even without sensory feedback** (pp. 396–397)
- **Pacemaker cells and synaptic interconnections both contribute to central pattern generation** (pp. 397–398)
- **Central pattern generators in the spinal cord take advantage of sensory feedback, interconnections among spinal segments, and interactions with brainstem control centers** (pp. 398–399)

### Core claims
- A **motor program** is a structured sequence of muscle commands set up by the CNS *before* movement begins; the strongest evidence is that walking, breathing (see pp. 706–709), chewing, swimming, etc., can be produced **without sensory feedback**, although feedback always shapes the final pattern.
- **Central pattern generators (CPGs)** are circuits that produce **rhythmic, coordinated motor output**. Common examples: walking, running, swimming, breathing, chewing, certain eye movements, shivering, scratching.
- Locomotor CPGs **reside in the spinal cord**: cats with the spinal cord transected at the thoracic level can produce well-coordinated stepping on a treadmill. Each limb has **at least one CPG**; if one leg is held still the other continues normally, demonstrating limb independence under loose coupling.
- **Gait** = a particular pattern and coupling of CPGs across limbs. In four-footed animals, walk / trot / pace / gallop differ both in the temporal pattern within a single limb and in the coordination across limbs (Fig. 16-6B). Stimulation of sensory afferents or descending tracts can switch gait abruptly.
- **Two mechanisms** for rhythm generation:
  - **Pacemaker neurons** — single neurons with intrinsic conductances (see Fig. 12-4) that oscillate even when isolated; analogous to cardiac and smooth muscle pacemakers. In vertebrates these contribute to CPGs but are rarely solely responsible.
  - **Network rhythms via reciprocal inhibition** — Graham Brown's 1911 **half-center model** (Fig. 16-7): two excitatory interneurons (one driving flexor motor neurons, one driving extensor motor neurons) reciprocally inhibit each other through dedicated inhibitory interneurons. A tonic drive plus a built-in mechanism to terminate each half-center's activity (e.g., adaptation) produces alternating bursts. Sensory feedback is **not required** for the oscillation.
- **Lamprey spinal CPG** (Grillner) improves on the half-center model in three ways:
  1. **Sensory feedback** — intraspinal stretch receptors detect body bending; one type excites ipsilateral pattern generators, the other inhibits the contralateral generator. Their net effect is to terminate activity on the contracted side and initiate it on the relaxed side.
  2. **Intersegmental coordination** — each of the ~100 segmental CPGs fires with a fixed phase lag (~1% of the cycle) relative to the next, producing the smooth rostrocaudal wave of swimming muscle activity. Reversing the lag swims the animal backward.
  3. **Reciprocal communication with the brainstem** — descending pathways modulate CPG speed and pattern; the CPGs in turn inform the brainstem of their state.
- These principles (CPG + sensory modulation + intersegmental coupling + brainstem control) generalize across vertebrates — including cats and humans walking — only the details differ.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "A common feature of motor control is the motor program, a set of structured muscle commands that are determined by the nervous system before a movement begins." (p. 396)
- > "Central pattern generators driving each of these activities share certain basic properties." (p. 396)
- > "Surprisingly, [the pattern generators] responsible for locomotion reside in the spinal cord itself." (p. 397)
- > "Rhythm generation can continue in the absence of sensory information." (p. 398)
- > "Pacemakers are embedded within interconnected circuits, and it is the combination of intrinsic pacemaker properties and synaptic interconnections that generates rhythms." (pp. 397–398)

#### Figure 16-7 — Half-center model *(listed)*

Two parallel chains, each consisting of one **excitatory interneuron** driving a **motor neuron** to either the flexor or the extensor muscle. The excitatory interneuron of each chain also drives an **inhibitory interneuron** that projects across to silence the opposite chain. A **tonic descending or afferent drive** keeps the system energized. Activity alternates because each active half-center suppresses the other; a built-in mechanism (e.g., adaptation, fatigue, or another inhibitory mechanism not drawn) limits the duration of each burst so that excitability can switch sides.

## Section 4 — Sensory and motor maps (pp. 399–405)

### Subsection headings
- **The nervous system contains maps of sensory and motor information** (p. 399)
- **The cerebral cortex has multiple visuotopic maps** (pp. 399–401)
- **Maps of somatic sensory information magnify some parts of the body more than others** (pp. 401–402)
- **The cerebral cortex has a motor map that is adjacent to and well aligned with the somatosensory map** (pp. 402–403)
- **Sensory and motor maps are fuzzy and plastic** (pp. 403–405)

### Core claims — general
- Sensory receptors are arranged in **planar sheets** that the brain re-maps centrally one or more times. The mapped quantity is not always space:
  - **Skin** → topographic map of body surface (somatotopy).
  - **Retina** → topographic map of visual space (retinotopy).
  - **Cochlea** → topographic map of **sound frequency** (tonotopy), not source location.
  - **Olfactory / taste epithelia** → topographic **chemical-quality** maps.
- Some maps are **computed** rather than directly inherited (sound-source location; see Section 5).
- **Magnification factor** (mm of cortex per unit of sensory space) is **not constant** — high-acuity / behaviorally important regions are over-represented.

### Core claims — visual maps
- Retina → **LGN** of thalamus → primary visual cortex **(V1)**. Left visual hemifield ⇒ both nasal retinae ⇒ axons cross the optic chiasm appropriately so that the left hemifield is represented in the right cortex; upper visual field ⇒ lower V1.
- **Fovea** has hugely magnified V1 representation (~½ of V1 is devoted to the fovea + surround), reflecting the high density of foveal photoreceptors and ganglion cells (see p. 363).
- Within V1, **two-eye information is segregated** at the layer-IV level into **ocular dominance columns** (~0.25–0.5 mm wide), discovered by Hubel and Wiesel (Nobel Prize 1981; also see N16-2).
- Superimposed but distinct: **blobs** (visible with cytochrome-oxidase stain) in layers II/III of V1 — ~0.2 mm round patches that channel color information.
- Adjacent **V2** has its own pattern: **thick stripes, thin stripes, and pale interstripes**. Color-tuned V1 blobs project to **thin stripes** of V2; motion-tuned cells project to **thick stripes** — beginnings of parallel processing streams.
- **>25 visual cortical areas** have been identified in monkey, mostly near V1; humans dedicate **~½ of neocortex** primarily to vision. The "**binding problem**" — combining motion, form, color, location, and context into a single percept — depends on dense reciprocal interconnection between these maps.

### Core claims — somatosensory and motor maps
- Penfield mapped **S1 (postcentral gyrus)** by stimulating the cortical surface of awake patients during neurosurgery — the **sensory homunculus**: legs medial (draped over the top of the gyrus into the interhemispheric fissure), trunk and limbs lateral, mouth and face most lateral.
- Two key features: (i) the map is **discontinuous** in places (hand separates head from rest of body); (ii) **magnification factor varies** — fingertips, lips, and tongue are vastly over-represented. Map area correlates with **density of innervation** and **behavioral importance** (e.g., tongue/lips for speech and food discrimination).
- Species differences reflect behavioral specialization: rodent S1 has a huge whisker-barrel field; primate S1 has huge digits.
- Adjacent **M1 (precentral gyrus)** has a closely-aligned motor homunculus, again with face and hand magnification.
- **fMRI nuance**: at fine scale, the motor map of the hand is **not** a tidy somatotopic strip — individual finger and wrist movements activate widely distributed, overlapping cortical regions. Penfield's gross map is still correct in coarse layout but local organization is **distributed and cooperative**.
- **Polysensory alignment in the superior colliculus** (Fig. 16-11): the SC contains a retinotopic map of visual space, an auditory map, and a somatosensory map, all in approximate spatial register, plus a motor map for orienting eye and head movements in nearly perfect register with the visual map.

### Core claims — why maps?
- Several functional advantages are proposed:
  - **Wiring economy** — neurons that must communicate sit near each other; total axon length is minimized.
  - **Hebbian reinforcement** — neighboring neurons tend to be co-active, so their synapses are reinforced (see pp. 328–333; STDP / LTP).
  - **Easier development** — short axon trajectories are easier to specify.
  - **Edge detection by lateral inhibition** — adjacent map sites are obvious inhibitory targets.

### Core claims — fuzziness and plasticity
- Maps are **abstract, distorted, and method-dependent** ("The map is not the territory" — Korzybski).
- A stimulus is encoded by **distributed activity across many neurons**, not by a single map element — providing redundancy and graceful degradation.
- Maps are **plastic** at all ages. After **median nerve transection** in an owl monkey, the cortical territory deprived of palmar input remaps over months so that responses to the dorsal (hairy) skin of D1–D3 expand into the deprived zone; other regions disappear or shift. In rat M1, **facial nerve transection** is followed by remapping such that the deprived whisker territory comes to evoke eye, eyelid, and forelimb movements. The same plasticity mechanisms are thought to underlie motor learning and post-stroke recovery.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The brain also builds maps, most of which represent very selected aspects of our sensory information about the environment or the motor systems controlling our body." (p. 399)
- > "About half of the primary visual cortex is devoted to input from the relatively small fovea and the retinal area just surrounding it." (p. 399)
- > "Humans devote almost half of their neocortex primarily to the processing of visual information." (p. 399)
- > "The magnification factor for the body surface is not a constant but varies for different parts of the body." (p. 401)
- > "All sensory and motor maps are clearly dynamic and can be reorganized rapidly and substantially as a function of development, behavioral state, training, or damage to the brain or periphery. Such changes are referred to as plasticity." (p. 404)

## Section 5 — Time-measuring circuits: sound localization (pp. 405–407)

### Subsection headings
- **To localize sound, the brain compares the timing and intensity of input to the ears** (pp. 405–406)
- **The brain measures interaural timing by a combination of neural delay lines and coincidence detectors** (pp. 406–407)

### Core claims — strategies for sound localization
- **Vertical-plane (elevation) localization** uses a single ear and exploits the geometry of the **pinna and tragus** (Fig. 16-13): the relative path lengths of direct vs. pinna-reflected sound differ with elevation, producing elevation-specific spectral interference at the eardrum.
- **Horizontal-plane (azimuth)** localization requires **two ears** and uses two different cues, by frequency band:
  - **High frequency (~2–20 kHz)** → **interaural intensity difference (IID)**: the far ear sits in the **acoustic head shadow** of the near ear, hearing a quieter sound. Maximal at ±90°, zero at 0° or 180° (front/back ambiguity).
  - **Low frequency (<~2 kHz)** → **interaural time delay (ITD)**: sound wavelengths longer than head width diffract around the head, so IID vanishes; instead, the brain detects **microsecond-scale arrival-time differences**.
- **Quantitative anchor** for ITD: 200-Hz wave (λ = 172 cm) on a 20-cm-wide head from straight right → ~0.6 ms delay to left ear; 45° → ~0.3 ms; 0° → 0 ms. These sub-millisecond delays are within the temporal resolution of brainstem auditory neurons.

### Core claims — the Jeffress model and the MSO
- **Cochlear nucleus** neurons fire **phase-locked** action potentials to low-frequency sound waves on the same side only. The first binaural comparison happens at the **medial superior olivary (MSO) nucleus**: each MSO neuron receives axons from both cochlear nuclei (Fig. 16-15A).
- MSO neurons are **exquisitely tuned to interaural delay**; their preferred delay varies systematically across the MSO, producing a **spatial map of interaural delay**. The MSO simultaneously carries a tonotopic map of sound frequency, so it maps two stimulus qualities at once.
- **Jeffress (1948) delay-line / coincidence-detection model** (Fig. 16-15B):
  - **Delay lines** are the axons from the two cochlear nuclei. Different MSO target cells receive axons of different lengths/conduction velocities and so different conduction delays.
  - **Coincidence detectors** are the MSO neurons themselves: each fires only when action potentials from the left- and right-ear axons arrive **simultaneously**.
  - A given MSO cell's preferred ITD is set by the mismatch between its left- and right-ear axonal delays. The orderly arrangement of delay-line lengths across the MSO produces the orderly map of azimuth.
- **Mammalian caveat**: in gerbils (and likely other mammals), **synaptic inhibition** also helps tune MSO ITD sensitivity. Pure Jeffress delay-line geometry may be more typical of birds; mammals likely combine delay lines, inhibition, and other mechanisms.
- The MSO's ITD map is a **computed** map (no map for sound location exists at the cochlea). A separate computed map of IID handles high-frequency localization. These two computed sub-maps are combined downstream in the **inferior colliculus** to form a unified map of sound direction. Hierarchical + parallel processing of this kind is a general CNS strategy.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Neural circuits are very good at resolving time intervals, in some cases down to microseconds or less." (p. 405)
- > "For frequencies that are relatively high in frequency (~2 to 20 kHz), the important measure is the interaural intensity difference." (p. 405)
- > "At low frequencies, the nervous system uses another strategy — it measures interaural delay." (p. 405)

#### Figure 16-13 — Detection of sound in the vertical plane *(listed)*

A head in profile with a sound source above; three pathways from the source to the auditory canal are drawn: a **direct** path and two **reflected** paths off the pinna and tragus with slightly longer travel times. #### Figure 16-14 — Sound detection in a horizontal plane *(listed)*

- **Panel A — High-frequency sound.** A head with sound coming from the right; the left ear sits in the **acoustic head shadow** so the wave amplitude reaching it is attenuated. The CNS uses the resulting **interaural intensity difference (IID)** to compute azimuth.
- **Panel B — Low-frequency sound.** Same geometry with a 200-Hz wave (λ = 172 cm) and a 20-cm head; the wave peak reaches the left ear ~**0.6 ms** after the right. The CNS uses this **interaural time delay (ITD)** to compute azimuth.

#### Figure 16-15 — CNS processing of sounds *(viewed)*

- **Panel A — Major auditory pathways.** A cross-section of the mammalian medulla. From each **cochlea**, the **auditory (cochlear) nerve** projects to the **ipsilateral cochlear nucleus**, which sends axons to higher centers including the **MSO nucleus**. Crucially, each MSO neuron is shown receiving **bilateral** input — one axon from each cochlear nucleus — while cochlear-nucleus neurons themselves are monaural. The figure visually establishes that the MSO is the first binaural integration stage.
- **Panel B — Calculation of interaural delay by coincidence detection.** A schematic of the Jeffress model. Sound is shown arriving at the **left ear first** (acoustic delay favors the left). Axons from each cochlear nucleus run across a row of MSO neurons labeled A–E. The left-ear axon takes a **long** intra-axonal path to neuron D; the right-ear axon takes a **short** intra-axonal path to neuron D. Because the long acoustic delay on the right is compensated by the short neuronal delay on the right, the two action potentials arrive **coincidentally at neuron D**, which fires. Neurons A–C and E receive non-coincident input and stay silent. The MSO thereby converts an interaural time delay into a discrete spatial code (which neuron fires) for sound azimuth.

> Vision note: The textbook's canonical "time-into-space" illustration. Anchor for the Jeffress delay-line / coincidence-detection scheme, the role of the MSO, and the more general principle that the brain computes maps when receptor-sheet maps are unavailable.

## Equations summary

This chapter is mostly qualitative. The one quantitative relationship developed is the **interaural time delay (ITD)**:

| Quantity | Expression | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Interaural time delay | $\Delta t \approx \dfrac{w \sin\theta}{c}$ | $w$ = head width (~20 cm), $\theta$ = source azimuth from straight ahead, $c$ = speed of sound (~343 m/s) |
| Maximum ITD (lateral source) | $\Delta t_{\max} \approx w/c \approx 600\ \mu\text{s}$ | for a 20-cm-wide head |
| Frequency cutoff for IID/ITD switch | $\lambda \approx w$ when $f \approx c/w \approx 1.7\ \text{kHz}$ | below this, head-shadow IID vanishes and ITD dominates |
| Phase-lag intersegmental coupling (lamprey CPG) | $\Delta \phi \approx 0.01\,T$ per segment | $T$ = cycle period; ~100 segments distribute one wavelength along the body |

(Cable-theory and AP-conduction quantities are inherited from Chs 7, 12; this chapter applies them only qualitatively.)

## Glossary

- **Local circuit / microcircuit / system** — three nested levels of neural organization.
- **Projection (principal) neuron / interneuron (intrinsic neuron)** — output cells vs. local-processing cells.
- **Motor unit / motor neuron pool** — one α-motor neuron + its fibers; all α-motor neurons to one muscle.
- **α-motor neuron / γ-motor neuron** — extrafusal vs. intrafusal innervation.
- **Group Ia / Ib / Aδ axons** — muscle spindle primary / Golgi tendon organ / fast pain afferents.
- **Muscle spindle / Golgi tendon organ** — length vs. tension mechanoreceptors.
- **Stretch (myotatic) reflex** — monosynaptic Ia → α-motor neuron arc.
- **Reciprocal innervation / reciprocal inhibition** — Ia inhibitory interneuron disynaptically relaxes antagonist.
- **Flexion-withdrawal reflex / crossed-extensor reflex** — multisynaptic noxious-stimulus reflexes with bilateral output.
- **Jendrassik maneuver** — clinical augmentation of stretch reflex by distraction.
- **Spinal shock / spasticity / hypertonia / hyperreflexia / paresis / paralysis / areflexia / atonia / atrophy** — Box 16-1 vocabulary.
- **Paraplegia / hemiplegia / quadriplegia** — lesion-topography terms.
- **Motor program** — preplanned, sensory-independent muscle command sequence.
- **Central pattern generator (CPG)** — circuit producing rhythmic motor output.
- **Half-center model** — reciprocal-inhibition CPG (Graham Brown 1911).
- **Pacemaker neuron** — intrinsically oscillating cell.
- **Lamprey CPG** — Grillner's model: sensory feedback + intersegmental coupling + brainstem control.
- **Retinotopy / tonotopy / somatotopy** — receptor-sheet preserving maps.
- **Magnification factor** — non-uniform scaling of map area.
- **V1 / V2 / LGN / fovea / ocular dominance column / blob / thick stripe / thin stripe / pale interstripe** — visual-pathway anatomy and microarchitecture.
- **Hubel and Wiesel** — discovered ocular dominance columns and orientation tuning (Nobel 1981).
- **Sensory homunculus / motor homunculus** — Penfield's S1 / M1 maps.
- **Superior colliculus** — site of aligned visual, auditory, somatosensory, and motor maps.
- **Map plasticity** — peripheral deafferentation triggers cortical remapping.
- **Interaural intensity difference (IID) / interaural time delay (ITD)** — azimuth cues by frequency band.
- **Acoustic head shadow** — basis for high-frequency IID.
- **Pinna spectral cues** — basis for monoaural vertical localization.
- **Cochlear nucleus / medial superior olive (MSO) / inferior colliculus** — central auditory stations.
- **Phase locking** — synchrony of cochlear-nucleus AP firing with low-frequency sound waves.
- **Jeffress delay-line / coincidence-detection model** — circuit transducing ITD into a spatial code in the MSO.
- **Computed map** — central map of a quality (e.g., ITD) that has no spatial representation at the receptor sheet.
