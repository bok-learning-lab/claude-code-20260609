## Section 1 — Heat and temperature: advantages of homeothermy (pp. 1193–1194)

### Subsection headings
- **Homeotherms maintain their activities over a wide range of environmental temperatures** (p. 1193)
- **Body core temperature depends on time of day, physical activity, time in the menstrual cycle, and age** (pp. 1193–1194)

### Core claims
- **Poikilotherms** do not regulate internal temperature (activity tracks ambient temperature); **homeotherms** do, gaining independence from the environment. Reaction rates depend on temperature, so most physiological functions are temperature-sensitive.
- Stable homeotherm temperature arises from neural networks combining **anticipatory (feed-forward)** and **negative-feedback** control, keeping reaction rates high/optimal and avoiding the pathologies of wide swings (Table 59-1).
- **The fundamental thermoregulatory system has five parts**: (1) thermal sensors; (2) thermosensory afferent pathways; (3) a CNS integration system; (4) efferent pathways; (5) thermal effectors — heat generators (brown adipose tissue, shivering skeletal muscle) and heat-transfer modulators (skin circulation, sweat glands).
- **Normal adult core temperature** ~37 °C (98.6 °F), ranging 36–37.5 °C in healthy active people. "Body temperature" = core temperature, measured sublingually, in the ear canal, or rectally; rectal is most reliable (least affected by ambient air). An infrared thermometer over the temporal artery is the least invasive.
- **Circadian rhythm**: all homeotherms vary ~1 °C over ~24 h; in humans, lowest 3:00–6:00 AM, peak 3:00–6:00 PM. Governed by the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** (anterior hypothalamus), entrained by light–dark cues, independent of the sleep–wake cycle.
- **Menstrual cycle**: temperature rises ~0.5 °C in the postovulatory (luteal) phase; an abrupt 0.3–0.5 °C rise accompanies ovulation (a fertility guide).
- **Age**: infants (high surface-to-mass ratio, poor shivering/sweating, but large BAT depots) and older people (declining heat sensation, lower muscle mass/metabolic rate, reduced cardiovascular reserve, sweat-gland atrophy) maintain core temperature less well.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The ability to regulate internal body temperature has provided higher organisms independence from the environment." (p. 1193)
- > "The stable body temperature of homeotherms is the consequence of designated neural networks that incorporate both anticipatory and negative-feedback controls." (p. 1193)
- > "The 'normal' body temperature of an adult human is ~37°C (98.6°F) but it may be as low as 36°C or as high as 37.5°C in active, healthy people." (p. 1193)
- > "All homeotherms maintain a circadian rhythm (~24-hour cycle) of body temperature, with variations of ~1°C." (p. 1193)
- > "In many women, body temperature increases ~0.5°C during the postovulatory phase of the menstrual cycle." (p. 1193)

## Section 2 — Modes of heat transfer (pp. 1194–1198)

### Subsection headings
- **The body's rate of heat production can vary from ~70 kcal/hr at rest to 600 kcal/hr during exercise** (pp. 1194–1195)
- **Maintaining a relatively constant body temperature requires a fine balance between heat production and heat losses** (p. 1195)
- **Heat moves from the body core to the skin, primarily by convection** (pp. 1195–1196)
- **Heat moves from the skin to the environment by radiation, conduction, convection, and evaporation** (pp. 1196–1197)
- **When heat gain exceeds heat loss, body core temperature rises** (pp. 1197–1198)
- **Clothing insulates the body from the environment and limits heat transfer** (p. 1198)

### Core claims — heat production
- Cellular reactions are inefficient, so metabolism produces heat; heat-production rate tracks O₂ consumption (V̇O₂) because nearly all food substrates are oxidized.
- **Resting metabolic rate (RMR)** at RQ 0.8 corresponds to V̇O₂ ≈ 250 mL/min ≈ **72 kcal/hr (~85 W)**. At RMR the body does no external work, so all energy is dissipated as heat — "like a zero-efficiency, 85-watt incandescent light bulb."
- Heat production is not constant: raised by muscular activity (postural, shivering), digestion, the **van't Hoff relation** (a 10 °C tissue-temperature rise more than doubles metabolic rate), and hormones (thyroxine, epinephrine).
- Comfortable sustained exercise ≈ 400–600 kcal/hr; **maximal endurance exercise**: M ≈ 1300 kcal/hr, of which ~500 kcal/hr may be useful work, leaving ~800 kcal/hr (~960 W) as heat — "changing from an 85-watt light bulb to a 1000-watt space heater."
- Without dissipation, an exercise thermal load would raise core temperature ~1.0 °C every 8–10 min, limiting activity to 25–30 min before hyperthermia (>40 °C) impairs function.

### Equations — the heat-balance framework

- **Whole-body heat-balance equation (Eq. 59-1)** — at constant body mass:

  $$ \underbrace{(M - W)}_{\text{heat production } H} - \underbrace{(R + C + E)}_{\text{heat losses}} = S $$

  where $M$ = metabolism, $W$ = work done on the environment, $R$ = radiative loss, $C$ = convective loss, $E$ = evaporative loss, $S$ = rate of heat storage; all terms in **kcal/hr**. $S>0$ when $(M-W) > (R+C+E)$ → core temperature rises (e.g., exercise); $S<0$ → it falls (e.g., entering cold water). Since cellular processes are inefficient, $H = M - W$ and most metabolic energy ends up as heat.

- **Core→skin transfer (Eq. 59-2)** — heat leaves the core by two parallel routes, passive conduction across tissue plus convection by blood:

  $$ \text{(core→skin transfer)} = \text{(passive conduction core→skin)} + \text{(convection by blood core→skin)} $$

  Both terms are proportional to the gradient $(T_{core} - T_{skin})$. The conduction coefficient (across subcutaneous fat, the body's insulation) is **relatively fixed**; the blood-convection coefficient is **variable**, set by skin blood flow under autonomic control. Skin blood flow is therefore the primary determinant of core→skin heat flow.

- **Radiative heat loss (Eq. 59-3)**:

  $$ R = h_{radiative}\,(T_{skin} - T_{radiant})\,A_{radiative} $$

  with $h_{radiative}$ **constant**, $T_{radiant}$ the mean radiant temperature of surrounding objects, and $A$ the effective surface area. $R>0$ = loss, $R<0$ = gain. Indoors $T_{radiant} = T_{ambient}$; outdoors radiating bodies differ widely (solar load can exceed RMR; loss to a cloudless night sky can also exceed RMR). Radiation ≈ **60% of heat loss** at rest in a neutral thermal indoor environment.

- **Convective heat loss (Eq. 59-4)**:

  $$ C = h_{convective}\,(T_{skin} - T_{ambient})\,A_{convective} $$

  $h_{convective}$ is **variable** — up to ~5× higher at high air velocity (forced convection → **wind chill**); natural convection occurs when skin-warmed air rises and is replaced by cooler air. In water, $h_{convective}$ is ~100× that in air (high conductivity and heat capacity), making immersion the dominant cause of rapid heat loss (e.g., Titanic deaths by hypothermia).

- **Evaporative heat loss (Eq. 59-5)** — independent of temperature gradient; proportional to the **water-vapor-pressure gradient**:

  $$ E = h_{evaporative}\,(P^{H_2O}_{skin} - P^{H_2O}_{ambient})\,A_{evaporative} $$

  $E>0$ = loss by evaporation, $E<0$ = gain by condensation. Evaporating 1 g water removes **~0.58 kcal**; sweat glands deliver up to 30 g/min (1.8 L/hr), so evaporation can remove ~1000 kcal/hr — theoretically nearly all heavy-exercise heat under low humidity. High humidity collapses the vapor-pressure gradient and impairs cooling (the basis of the heat index); high air velocity raises the effective gradient.

- **Rate of mean-body-temperature change (Eq. 59-6)**:

  $$ \frac{\Delta T_{body}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\text{rate of heat storage}}{0.83\;[\text{kcal}/(\text{kg}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C})] \times BW\;[\text{kg}]} $$

  where 0.83 is the specific heat of body tissues and $BW$ is body weight. Knowing the transfer coefficients, the temperature/vapor gradients, $M$ (from V̇O₂ by indirect calorimetry), and $W$, one computes $R$, $C$, $E$, then $S$, then the predicted rate of temperature change — verifiable against direct thermometry of a weighted mean of $T_{core}$ and $T_{skin}$.

### Core claims — internal vs. external heat loads
- $(M-W)$ is an **internal heat load**. The $(R+C+E)$ term is normally a net loss but can become a net gain — an **external heat load** — when $R$ or $C$ reverse sign (standing in the sun with $T_{radiant} > T_{skin}$; a hot sauna with $T_{ambient} > T_{skin}$). Both load types drive net storage and a temperature rise.
- A small amount of core heat also leaves via evaporation of water from the **respiratory tract**, scaling with ventilation (and thus metabolic rate).
- **Clothing** inserts an insulating layer; heat then leaves the clothing surface by the same four routes. Insulation is quantified in **clo units**: 1 clo keeps a resting person comfortable at 21 °C with minimal air movement.

- > "Virtually all heat leaving the body must exit through the skin surface." (p. 1195)

## Section 3 — Active regulation of body temperature by the CNS (pp. 1198–1201)

### Subsection headings
- **Thermoreceptors in the skin and temperature-sensitive neurons in the hypothalamus respond to changes in their local temperature** (pp. 1198–1200)
- **The CNS thermoregulatory network integrates thermal information and directs changes in efferent activity to modify rates of heat transfer and production** (pp. 1200–1201)
- **Thermal effectors include behavior, cutaneous circulation, sweat glands, and skeletal muscles responsible for shivering** (pp. 1200–1201)

### Core claims — the network
- The central thermoregulatory network comprises: (1) afferent skin/visceral thermoreceptors; (2) thermal afferent pathways in the CNS; (3) the integration center in the **preoptic anterior hypothalamus**; (4) efferent autonomic and somatomotor pathways; (5) thermal effectors. It is an **active** system superimposed on the passive poikilothermic system of Section 2.
- **Division of labor**: skin thermoreceptors sense the *environment* well but core poorly (so used as a fast, **feed-forward/anticipatory** signal); hypothalamic neurons sense *core* temperature well but the environment too slowly (used in **negative-feedback** control). The body needs both because thermal inertia makes central receptors alone too slow.

### Core claims — receptors and TRP channels
- **Skin thermoreceptors** are free nerve endings with cation channels: **TRPV1–TRPV4** open as temperature **rises**, **TRPM8** opens as temperature **falls** (see p. 386). Two anatomically distinct classes — **warmth receptors** and **cold receptors** — innervate distinct warm/cold spots; **cold receptors predominate**. Discrimination is coarsest on trunk/limbs, finest on face/lips/fingers.
- **Firing-rate ranges** (Fig. 59-3): warmth receptors increase steady firing as skin warms from ~32 °C to ~45 °C; cold receptors increase steady firing as skin cools from ~40 °C down to 26 °C. Both give a **phasic (dynamic)** response to a step change followed by a **tonic (static)** plateau.
- Skin signals also reach the cortex via thalamus → **conscious thermal perception** and **behavioral thermoregulation** (moving from sun to shade).
- **Hypothalamic temperature-sensitive neurons**: in the preoptic area of the anterior hypothalamus, **~10% are warmth-sensitive** (discharge rises with local warming, via TRPV channels). These neurons are the principal sensor of deep-body (core) temperature — critical during exercise, hot-fluid intake, and the resolution of fever.

### Core claims — integration and efferents
- Warmth/cold receptor cell bodies sit in **dorsal root ganglia**; axons synapse in the dorsal horn on second-order neurons projecting to two sites: the **thalamus** (→ cortex, conscious localization) and the **parabrachial nucleus of the pons** (→ preoptic hypothalamus, autonomic integration). Fourth-order preoptic neurons integrate skin-temperature input with core temperature encoded in preoptic warmth-sensitive-neuron discharge.
- **Sign of integration**: cutaneous **warmth** input *increases* preoptic warmth-sensitive-neuron discharge; cutaneous **cold** input *inhibits* it — shifting the discharge away from the level set by core temperature alone.
- **Efferent logic**: preoptic warmth-sensitive neurons provide **variable inhibition** to descending pathways that otherwise excite (1) sympathetic preganglionic neurons driving cutaneous **vasoconstriction** and **BAT thermogenesis**, and (2) **α-motor neurons** driving **shivering**. A fall in skin temperature **disinhibits** these cold-defense effectors, raising core temperature.
- **Nonthermal modulation of specific efferents**: **dehydration** raises plasma osmolality → CNS osmoreceptors **inhibit sweating** (conserving water at the cost of cooling); **hypoxia** (altitude, emphysema) → arterial chemoreceptor reflex **inhibits thermogenesis** in BAT and muscle (sparing O₂).

### Core claims — the effectors
- **Behavior** is usually the first response to an environmental change sensed by skin receptors (moving out of the sun, building a fire, dressing for a snowstorm); it is a key anticipatory mechanism.
- **Cutaneous circulation** (sympathetic): adjusting cutaneous arteriolar tone and **arteriovenous anastomoses (shunts)** sets core→skin heat flow. **Active vasodilation** can raise skin blood flow up to **10-fold**; cold defense combines withdrawal of active vasodilation with active **vasoconstriction** (which also lowers skin temperature, reinforcing the cold signal). Even maximal vasoconstriction leaves a residual loss (small fixed tissue conductance). **Piloerection** (vestigial in hairless humans) retains a warm air layer.
- **Sweat glands**: with a large heat load, preoptic neurons drive **eccrine sweat glands** to secrete sweat → raises skin water-vapor pressure → evaporative cooling. Innervation is sympathetic but **cholinergic (acetylcholine)** — unusual. Sweat losses can exceed 1 L/hr, so hydration is essential to avoid circulatory collapse and fatal hyperthermia.
- **Thermogenesis**: as cold stress rises, vasoconstriction is supplemented by heat production — first in **brown adipose tissue (BAT)**, then by **shivering**. Sympathetic nerves release **norepinephrine onto β₃-adrenergic receptors** on brown adipocytes; fatty-acid oxidation yields **heat, not ATP**, via the uncoupling protein **UCP1 (thermogenin)** in abundant mitochondria. BAT output scales with depot size and sympathetic drive; adult-human BAT exists and is relatively reduced in obesity. **Shivering** (involuntary clonic skeletal-muscle contractions) can **triple–quadruple** metabolic rate briefly and **double** it for hours before fatigue.

## Section 4 — Hypothermia, hyperthermia, and fever (pp. 1201–1203)

### Subsection headings
- **Hypothermia or hyperthermia occurs when heat transfer to or from the environment overwhelms the body's thermoregulatory capacity** (pp. 1201–1202)
- **Exercise raises heat production, which is followed by a matching rise in heat loss, but at the cost of a steady-state hyperthermia of exercise** (p. 1202)
- **Fever is a regulated hyperthermia** (pp. 1202–1203)

### Core claims — hypothermia
- The most common cause of excessive hypothermia is **prolonged cold-water immersion**. Water's specific heat per volume is ~4000× that of air and its thermal conductivity ~25× that of air, giving $h_{convective}$ ~100× higher than in air — **~200 kcal/(hr·°C·m²)** at rest in still water, **~500** while swimming.
- Defenses (peripheral vasoconstriction + shivering) cannot prevent hypothermia in prolonged immersion. A thick **insulating fat layer** retards loss (Melville's whale-blubber passage); swimmers historically used grease, now wetsuits. Clothing adds insulation by trapping air; **wetting clothing worsens loss** (water conducts better than air and evaporates from the surface).

### Core claims — hyperthermia
- The most common cause of excessive hyperthermia is **prolonged heat + high humidity + physical activity**. As radiant and ambient temperatures rise, $R$ and $C$ fail; above the mid-30s °C, **evaporation is the only effective route** — and **high humidity** then collapses the skin→air vapor-pressure gradient (Eq. 59-5), driving progressive hyperthermia.
- Under **low humidity**, radiative/convective gain rarely causes hyperthermia because evaporation compensates. Protections against radiative hyperthermia: avoid radiant sources (shade) and wear **loose clothing** (screens radiation while permitting evaporative/convective loss underneath).
- **Heat exhaustion** (the most common temperature-related abnormality in athletes): core rises to ~39 °C; failure stems mainly from **dehydration** (reduces sweating) and **hypovolemia** (reduces muscle→core→skin blood flow). More severe cases progress to **heat stroke** (core ≥41 °C; Box 59-1).

### Core claims — the steady-state hyperthermia of exercise
- At exercise onset, heat production exceeds dissipation → storage → core rises. The warmer preoptic environment raises warmth-sensitive-neuron discharge → activates heat dissipation (skin blood flow, sweating). Dissipation rises to match production, but **not enough to also clear the heat already stored**, so a **mildly elevated steady-state core temperature persists** while exercise continues.
- The elevated core temperature during exercise may be part of "central command," needed to sustain warmth-sensitive-neuron drive to the dissipation effectors. After exercise, core temperature returns to baseline.

### Core claims — fever as regulated hyperthermia
- **Key distinction**: exercise/environmental hyperthermia = *incomplete compensation* for an imposed load; **fever = a regulated elevation** induced by the thermoregulatory system itself (a raised set-point).
- **Cascade**: infectious/inflammatory stimuli → macrophages (and some lymphocytes) release **cytokines** → the **acute-phase response** (fever, lethargy, hyperalgesia). Cytokines such as **IL-1β** act as **endogenous pyrogens** (Table 59-3), inducing peripheral and CNS production of **prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂)**. IL-1 acts on endothelial cells of the leaky **OVLT** (organum vasculosum laminae terminalis, third-ventricle wall above the optic chiasm) → release PGE₂ → diffuses to the adjacent preoptic hypothalamus.
- **Mechanism of the set-point shift**: **PGE₂ inhibits preoptic warmth-sensitive neurons** — mimicking cold-receptor input — disinhibiting cold-defense effectors: cutaneous **vasoconstriction** (heat retention) plus **BAT thermogenesis** and **shivering ("chills")** → core rises. This is the opposite logic to exercise hyperthermia, where elevated core *directly* drives heat-loss effectors.
- **Breaking the fever**: when PGE₂ falls (e.g., an antipyretic inhibiting PGE₂ synthesis), the now-elevated core strongly stimulates warmth-sensitive neurons → inhibits heat production and activates heat loss, and the fever "breaks."
- **Behavioral coupling**: in exercise one feels warm (sheds clothing); at fever onset one feels cold (adds blankets, "chills"). Because fever uses the same cold-defense effectors, it is hard to mount a febrile rise in a cold environment. The protective value of fever is debated; in vitro T-lymphocyte proliferation to interleukins is many-fold higher at 39 °C than 37 °C.
- **Malignant hyperthermia** (per chapter focus): a pharmacogenetic, non-pyrogen-mediated hyperthermia — uncontrolled skeletal-muscle heat production triggered by certain anesthetics (distinct from fever's regulated set-point shift; the chapter treats it among thermoregulatory-capacity failures rather than as a raised set-point).

- > "Water has a specific heat per unit volume that is ~4000 times that of air and a thermal conductivity that is ~25 times that of air." (p. 1201)
- > "Heat exhaustion is the most common temperature-related abnormality in athletes." (p. 1198)
- > "Fever is a regulated elevation of core temperature induced by the central thermoregulatory system itself." (p. 1202)

### Reference — modes of heat transfer between body and environment

| Mode | Driving gradient | Coefficient behavior | Direction / notes | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Radiation (R)** | $T_{skin} - T_{radiant}$ (infrared exchange with surrounding solid bodies) | $h_{radiative}$ **constant** | ~60% of resting indoor loss; can reverse to a gain (sun, fire, hot surfaces) | 59-3 |
| **Conduction** | $T_{skin} - T_{object}$ (direct contact) | fixed (tissue/fat insulation) | Usually minimal (shoes/clothing); large lying on hot sand or with an ice pack | — |
| **Convection (C)** | $T_{skin} - T_{ambient}$ (medium = air or water) | $h_{convective}$ **variable**: ~5× higher with wind; ~100× higher in water | Natural vs. forced (wind chill); dominant route in water immersion | 59-4 |
| **Evaporation (E)** | $P^{H_2O}_{skin} - P^{H_2O}_{ambient}$ (water-vapor pressure, **not** temperature) | $h_{evaporative}$; raised by air velocity | ~0.58 kcal per g water; up to ~1000 kcal/hr; the only route when $T_{ambient}>T_{skin}$; defeated by high humidity | 59-5 |

## Glossary

- **Homeotherm / poikilotherm** — regulates / does not regulate internal temperature.
- **Core vs. shell (skin) temperature** — regulated ~37 °C core vs. variable skin; rectal most reliable.
- **Circadian variation** — ~1 °C, low 3–6 AM / high 3–6 PM; suprachiasmatic nucleus.
- **Heat-balance equation** — $(M-W) - (R+C+E) = S$ (Eq. 59-1), kcal/hr.
- **RMR** — resting metabolic rate ~70–72 kcal/hr (~85 W); van't Hoff Q₁₀ ~2.
- **Radiation / conduction / convection / evaporation** — the four heat-transfer modes (Eqs. 59-3 to 59-5).
- **clo unit** — clothing insulation; 1 clo = comfort at 21 °C at rest.
- **Wind chill / heat index** — forced convection (variable $h_{convective}$) / humidity-limited evaporation.
- **TRPV1–4 / TRPM8** — warmth- / cold-gated cation channels in thermoreceptors and preoptic neurons.
- **Warmth vs. cold receptors** — skin thermoreceptor classes; cold predominates; phasic + tonic responses.
- **Preoptic anterior hypothalamus** — central integrator; warmth-sensitive neurons sense core temperature (the "set-point").
- **Arteriovenous anastomoses (AV shunts)** — sympathetic control of cutaneous blood flow.
- **Eccrine sweat glands** — sympathetic **cholinergic** evaporative effector.
- **BAT / UCP1 (thermogenin)** — brown adipose nonshivering thermogenesis; NE → β₃ → uncoupled oxidation.
- **Shivering** — α-motor-driven heat production, 2–4× RMR.
- **Fever** — regulated hyperthermia; pyrogens → PGE₂ (via OVLT) → raised set-point.
- **Endogenous pyrogens** — IL-1α/β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α/β, MIP-1α/β, IFN-α/β/γ (Table 59-3).
- **OVLT** — organum vasculosum laminae terminalis; leaky-BBB site of PGE₂ entry to preoptic area.
- **Heat exhaustion / heat stroke** — ~39 °C (dehydration/hypovolemia) / ≥41 °C with organ failure (Box 59-1).
- **Hypothermia** — cold-water immersion dominant cause; $h_{convective}$ ~100× air.
- **Malignant hyperthermia** — anesthetic-triggered uncontrolled muscle thermogenesis (not a set-point shift).
