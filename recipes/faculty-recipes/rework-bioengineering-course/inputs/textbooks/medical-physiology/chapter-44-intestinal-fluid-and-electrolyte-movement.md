## Section 1 — Functional anatomy (pp. 899–901)

### Subsection headings
- **The small intestine has a villus-crypt organization, whereas the colon has surface epithelial cells with interspersed crypts** (pp. 899–900)
- **The surface area of the small intestine is amplified by folds, villi, and microvilli; amplification is less marked in the colon** (pp. 900–901)

### Core claims
- **Division of labor.** The small intestine absorbs nutrients (after luminal + brush-border digestion, see Ch 45) and absorbs/secretes fluid and electrolytes; the colon absorbs/secretes fluid and electrolytes but does **not** absorb nutrients (except neonatally).
- **Villus vs. crypt.** Small-intestinal villi (finger-like projections) surround the openings of **crypts of Lieberkühn**. Villous cells are the primary **absorptive** cells; crypt cells primarily **secrete**. The colon lacks villi: **surface epithelial cells** absorb, **colonic gland (crypt) cells** secrete.
- **Cell renewal.** The proliferative zone is at the crypt base in both organs; stem cells differentiate into vacuolated, goblet, and Paneth cells. Vacuolated cells migrate up the crypt-villus axis, become absorptive cells, reach the villus tip, undergo apoptosis, and slough. Turnover ≈ 48–96 hr; faster with feeding/lactation/resection, slower in starvation.
- **Surface-area amplification, three levels.** Small intestine: (1) macroscopic folds of Kerckring, (2) villi + crypts, (3) microvilli — net ~600-fold increase, total ~200 m² ("a doubles tennis court"). Colon amplifies via folds, crypts, and microvilli only (no villi) → only ~25 m².

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The cells lining the villi are considered to be the primary cells responsible for both nutrient and electrolyte absorption, whereas the crypt cells primarily participate in secretion." (p. 899)
- > "The colon does not have villi. Instead, the cells lining the large intestine are surface epithelial cells, and interspersed over the colonic surface are numerous apertures of colonic crypts (or glands)." (p. 899)
- > "The intestinal mucosa is a dynamic organ with continuous cell proliferation and migration." (p. 899)
- > "The total surface area of the human small intestine is ~200 m², or the surface area of a doubles tennis court." (p. 900)

## Section 2 — Overview of fluid and electrolyte movement (pp. 901–903)

### Subsection headings
- **The small intestine absorbs ~6.5 L/day of an ~8.5-L fluid load that is presented to it, and the colon absorbs ~1.9 L/day** (pp. 901–902)
- **The small intestine absorbs net water, Na⁺, Cl⁻, and K⁺ and secretes HCO₃⁻, whereas the colon absorbs net water, Na⁺, and Cl⁻ and secretes both K⁺ and HCO₃⁻** (p. 902)
- **The intestines absorb and secrete solutes by both active and passive mechanisms** (pp. 902–903)
- **Intestinal fluid movement is always coupled to solute movement, and sometimes solute movement is coupled to fluid movement by solvent drag** (p. 903)
- **The resistance of the tight junctions primarily determines the transepithelial resistance of intestinal epithelia** (p. 903)

### Core claims — the daily fluid budget
- **Dietary fluid** is only ~1.5–2.5 L/day, but the load presented to the small intestine is ~8–9 L/day; the difference is endogenous (salivary, gastric, pancreatic, biliary, and small-intestinal secretions).
- **Small intestine:** load ≈ 7.5 L (from other organs) + 1.0 L (its own secretion) = **~8.5 L/day**; ileocecal flow out ≈ 2.0 L → absorbs **~6.5 L/day**. Na⁺ absorption ≈ 600 mmol/day. Estimated maximal absorptive capacity 15–20 L/day.
- **Colon:** absorbs ileocecal 2.0 L minus stool water ~0.1 L = **~1.9 L/day**; maximal colonic capacity 4–5 L/day. This reserve is why a rise in ileocecal flow (e.g., to ~5 L when small-bowel absorption fails) need not produce diarrhea — the colon compensates.
- **Net ion directions:** small intestine is a net absorber of water, Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺ but a net **secretor of HCO₃⁻**; absorption is **isosmotic** (like the renal proximal tubule). Colon nets absorption of water, Na⁺, Cl⁻ but **secretes K⁺ and HCO₃⁻**.

### Core claims — mechanisms and heterogeneity
- **Two axes of heterogeneity:** segmental (longitudinal, e.g., jejunum vs. ileum vs. colon) and crypt-villus/surface (radial). Example: HCO₃⁻-stimulated Na⁺ absorption occurs only proximally; electrogenic (ENaC) Na⁺ absorption is restricted to the rectosigmoid colon.
- **Transcellular vs. paracellular.** Transcellular movement crosses both membranes in series and requires at least one **active** step; paracellular movement is passive through tight junctions.
- **Na-K pump is the engine.** All transcellular Na⁺ absorption is driven by the basolateral Na,K-ATPase, which keeps [Na⁺]ᵢ low (~15 mM) and the cell interior negative, providing the inward electrochemical gradient that powers apical Na⁺ entry (channels or Na⁺-coupled transporters).
- **Solvent drag.** Fluid movement (predominantly **paracellular**) follows active solute transport; conversely, bulk water flow drags solutes — significant for Na⁺ and urea in the high-permeability **jejunum**, negligible in ileum/colon.
- **Tight-junction resistance.** Paracellular resistance < transcellular, so transepithelial resistance ≈ tight-junction properties. Resistance rises in the **aboral** direction (jejunum < ileum < distal colon); crypt junctions are leakier than villus junctions.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The fluid content of the average diet is typically 1.5 to 2.5 L/day. However, the fluid load to the small intestine is considerably greater—8 to 9 L/day." (p. 901)
- > "Of the ~8.5 L/day that are presented to the small intestine, the small intestine removes ~6.5 L/day, delivering ~2 L/day to the colon. The large intestine removes ~1.9 L/day, leaving ~0.1 L/day in the feces." (Fig. 44-2, p. 902)
- > "Fluid absorption is isosmotic in the small intestine, similar to that observed in the renal proximal tubule." (p. 902)
- > "All transcellular Na⁺ absorption is mediated by the Na-K pump (i.e., Na,K-ATPase) located at the basolateral membrane." (p. 902)
- > "Solvent drag accounts for a significant fraction of the Na⁺ and urea absorbed in the human jejunum (but not in the more distal segments of the small intestine or the large intestine)." (p. 903)
- > "Overall mucosal resistance primarily reflects paracellular resistance, which in turn depends primarily on the properties of the tight junctions." (p. 903)

## Section 3 — Cellular mechanisms of Na⁺ absorption (pp. 903–905)

### Subsection headings
- **Na/glucose and Na/amino-acid cotransport in the small intestine is a major mechanism for postprandial Na⁺ absorption** (pp. 903–904)
- **Electroneutral Na-H exchange in the duodenum and jejunum is responsible for Na⁺ absorption that is stimulated by luminal alkalinity** (pp. 904–905)
- **Parallel Na-H and Cl-HCO₃ exchange in the ileum and proximal colon is the primary mechanism of Na⁺ absorption during the interdigestive period** (p. 905)
- **Epithelial Na⁺ channels are the primary mechanism of "electrogenic" Na⁺ absorption in the distal part of the colon** (p. 905)

### Core claims — the four apical Na⁺ entry modes
1. **Nutrient-coupled (SGLT1, Na/amino-acid).** Villous cells, throughout the small intestine; the dominant **postprandial** mechanism. SGLT1 couples 2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose; basolateral exit of glucose via GLUT2. Electrogenic → makes the lumen more negative, driving parallel Cl⁻ absorption. **Not** inhibited by cAMP or [Ca²⁺]ᵢ — the molecular basis of oral rehydration therapy (Box 44-1).
2. **Electroneutral Na-H exchange (NHE3/NHE2).** Duodenum + jejunum; stimulated by luminal alkalinity (HCO₃⁻-rich pancreatic/biliary/duodenal secretions) and by low pHᵢ. Inhibited by **millimolar** amiloride.
3. **Parallel Na-H + Cl-HCO₃ exchange → electroneutral NaCl absorption.** Ileum + proximal colon; the dominant **interdigestive** mechanism. Two separate exchangers coupled through pHᵢ — **not** an Na/Cl cotransporter. Apical Cl-HCO₃ exchanger in human colon is **DRA (SLC26A3)**. Down-regulated by cAMP, cGMP, and Ca²⁺; up-regulated by aldosterone and by low [Ca²⁺]ᵢ.
4. **Electrogenic ENaC.** Distal (rectosigmoid) colon; highly Na⁺-selective; blocked by **micromolar** amiloride; allows Na⁺ absorption against large gradients (Na⁺ conservation). Insensitive to luminal glucose/HCO₃⁻ and to cyclic nucleotides; markedly stimulated by **aldosterone**.

### Equations (transport stoichiometries)

- **Na/glucose cotransport (SGLT1):**

  $$2\,\text{Na}^+_{\text{lumen}} + \text{glucose}_{\text{lumen}} \rightarrow 2\,\text{Na}^+_{\text{cell}} + \text{glucose}_{\text{cell}}$$

- **Apical Na-H exchange (NHE3):**

  $$\text{Na}^+_{\text{lumen}} + \text{H}^+_{\text{cell}} \rightleftharpoons \text{Na}^+_{\text{cell}} + \text{H}^+_{\text{lumen}}$$

- **Electroneutral NaCl absorption** (coupled via pHᵢ; net 1 Na⁺ + 1 Cl⁻ in, 1 H⁺ + 1 HCO₃⁻ out → H₂O + CO₂ in lumen):

  $$\underbrace{\text{Na}^+ / \text{H}^+}_{\text{NHE3}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{Cl}^- / \text{HCO}_3^-}_{\text{DRA}} \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{pH}_i\;}\; \text{net NaCl absorption}$$

- **ENaC** (electrogenic, channel-mediated):

  $$\text{Na}^+_{\text{lumen}} \xrightarrow{\;\Delta\psi,\ \text{ENaC}\;} \text{Na}^+_{\text{cell}} \quad (\text{aldosterone-stimulated})$$

- **Basolateral exit, all modes:** Na,K-ATPase

  $$3\,\text{Na}^+_{\text{cell}} + 2\,\text{K}^+_{\text{blood}} \xrightarrow{\text{ATP}} 3\,\text{Na}^+_{\text{blood}} + 2\,\text{K}^+_{\text{cell}}$$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "This process is the primary mechanism for Na⁺ absorption after a meal, but it makes little contribution during the interdigestive period." (p. 903)
- > "Nutrient-coupled Na⁺ transporters, unlike other Na⁺ absorptive mechanisms in the small intestine, are not inhibited by either cAMP or [Ca²⁺]ᵢ." (p. 903)
- > "Electroneutral NaCl absorption is the result not of an Na/Cl cotransporter but rather of parallel apical membrane Na-H and Cl-HCO₃ exchangers that are closely linked by small changes in pHᵢ." (pp. 905)
- > "In the human colon, DRA (downregulated-in-adenoma, SLC26A3) mediates this Cl-HCO₃ exchange." (p. 905)
- > "Like the Na-H exchanger, these ENaCs are blocked by the diuretic amiloride, but at micromolar rather than millimolar concentrations." (p. 905)
- > "Na⁺ absorption in the distal part of the colon is highly efficient … it plays an important role in Na⁺ conservation." (p. 905)

## Section 4 — Cellular mechanisms of Cl⁻ absorption and secretion (pp. 905–908)

### Subsection headings
- **Voltage-dependent Cl⁻ absorption represents coupling of Cl⁻ absorption to electrogenic Na⁺ absorption in both the small and the large intestine** (pp. 905–906)
- **Electroneutral Cl-HCO₃ exchange results in Cl⁻ absorption and HCO₃⁻ secretion in the ileum and colon** (p. 906)
- **Parallel Na-H and Cl-HCO₃ exchange in the ileum and proximal colon mediates Cl⁻ absorption during the interdigestive period** (p. 906)
- **Electrogenic Cl⁻ secretion occurs in crypts of both the small and the large intestine** (pp. 906–908)

### Core claims — Cl⁻ absorption (three modes)
1. **Voltage-dependent (passive) Cl⁻ absorption.** Cl⁻ follows the lumen-negative voltage set up by electrogenic Na⁺ absorption — nutrient-coupled (small intestine) or ENaC (distal colon). Route is mostly **paracellular**; not an active process.
2. **Electroneutral Cl-HCO₃ exchange.** In ileal villus + colonic surface cells, an apical 1:1 Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchange (**DRA**) without a parallel Na-H exchanger → net **Cl⁻ absorption + HCO₃⁻ secretion**. Basolateral Cl⁻ exit possibly via a ClC-2 channel. Its congenital absence causes **congenital chloridorrhea** (Box 44-2).
3. **Coupled with electroneutral NaCl absorption** (parallel NHE3 + DRA, pHᵢ-linked) — the interdigestive route in ileum and proximal colon.

### Core claims — crypt-cell Cl⁻ secretion (the secretory engine)
- **Secretion is a crypt-cell function**, normally masked by the three absorptive processes; it becomes dominant in secretory diarrhea (Box 44-3).
- **Basolateral loading:** the Na/K/Cl cotransporter **NKCC1 (SLC12A2)** brings Cl⁻ into the cell, energized by the low [Na⁺]ᵢ set by the Na-K pump; basolateral K⁺ channels (**IK1, BK**) recycle K⁺.
- **Apical exit:** **CFTR** (and possibly Ca²⁺-activated CaCC) conducts Cl⁻ out into the lumen, raising lumen-negativity → drives **paracellular Na⁺ secretion** → net NaCl + fluid secretion.
- **Activation:** in the resting state apical Cl⁻ channels are closed/absent. **Secretagogues** raise cAMP (VIP, cholera/heat-labile *E. coli* toxin), cGMP (heat-stable *E. coli* toxin, guanylin), or Ca²⁺ (ACh, serotonin), activating protein kinases that open or insert apical Cl⁻ channels.

### Equations

- **NKCC1 (basolateral loading), 1 Na⁺ : 1 K⁺ : 2 Cl⁻:**

  $$\text{Na}^+_{\text{blood}} + \text{K}^+_{\text{blood}} + 2\,\text{Cl}^-_{\text{blood}} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+_{\text{cell}} + \text{K}^+_{\text{cell}} + 2\,\text{Cl}^-_{\text{cell}}$$

- **Apical Cl⁻ secretion (CFTR) + paracellular Na⁺:**

  $$\text{Cl}^-_{\text{cell}} \xrightarrow{\text{CFTR}} \text{Cl}^-_{\text{lumen}}; \qquad \text{Na}^+_{\text{blood}} \xrightarrow{\text{paracellular},\ \Delta\psi} \text{Na}^+_{\text{lumen}}$$

- **Apical Cl-HCO₃ exchange (DRA), 1:1:**

  $$\text{Cl}^-_{\text{lumen}} + \text{HCO}_3^-_{\text{cell}} \rightleftharpoons \text{Cl}^-_{\text{cell}} + \text{HCO}_3^-_{\text{lumen}}$$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The small intestine and the large intestine are also capable of active Cl⁻ secretion, although Cl⁻ secretion is believed to occur mainly in the crypts rather than in either the villi or surface cells." (p. 906)
- > "A Cl⁻ channel (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator [CFTR]) is present on the apical membrane." (p. 907)
- > "As a result, [Cl⁻]ᵢ is raised sufficiently that the Cl⁻ electrochemical gradient favors the passive efflux of Cl⁻ across the apical membrane." (p. 907)
- > "Normally (i.e., in the unstimulated state) the crypts secrete little Cl⁻ because the apical membrane Cl⁻ channels are either closed or not present." (p. 907)
- > "The induction of apical membrane Cl⁻ channels is extremely important in the pathophysiology of many diarrheal disorders." (p. 908)

## Section 5 — Cellular mechanisms of K⁺ absorption and secretion (pp. 908–910)

### Subsection headings
- **Overall net transepithelial K⁺ movement is absorptive in the small intestine and secretory in the colon** (pp. 908–909)
- **K⁺ absorption in the small intestine probably occurs via solvent drag** (p. 909)
- **Passive K⁺ secretion is the primary mechanism for net colonic secretion** (p. 909)
- **Active K⁺ secretion is also present throughout the large intestine and is induced both by aldosterone and by cAMP** (p. 909)
- **Active K⁺ absorption takes place only in the distal portion of the colon and is energized by an apical H-K pump** (pp. 909–910)

### Core claims
- **GI K⁺ budget:** dietary intake 80–120 mmol/day; stool output only ~10 mmol/day (kidney handles the rest). Stool [K⁺] is often >100 mM — from colonic secretion plus water absorption. Pattern parallels the kidney: absorb proximally, secrete distally.
- **Small-intestinal K⁺ absorption** (jejunum, ileum) is passive — most likely **solvent drag**; no evidence for active or carrier-mediated transport.
- **Colonic passive K⁺ secretion** (the main net-secretory route) is **paracellular**, driven by the lumen-negative Vₜₑ (15–25 mV); greatest in the distal colon (most negative Vₜₑ); enhanced by aldosterone-driven rises in lumen-negativity during dehydration.
- **Colonic active K⁺ secretion** uses a **pump-leak** model paralleling active Cl⁻ secretion and renal distal-nephron K⁺ secretion: basolateral uptake by Na-K pump + NKCC1; apical exit via **BK K⁺ channels** (rate-limiting). Aldosterone stimulates it in surface cells; cAMP/Ca²⁺ (VIP, cholera toxin, serotonin) stimulate it in crypt cells — contributing to fecal K⁺ loss in diarrhea.
- **Distal-colon active K⁺ absorption** is transcellular via an apical **H-K pump** (~60% identical to the Na-K and gastric H-K pumps), exchanging luminal K⁺ for cellular H⁺; basolateral exit possibly via K/Cl cotransport. Up-regulated by **dietary K⁺ depletion**.

### Equations

- **Apical colonic H-K pump (active K⁺ absorption):**

  $$\text{K}^+_{\text{lumen}} + \text{H}^+_{\text{cell}} \xrightarrow{\text{ATP}} \text{K}^+_{\text{cell}} + \text{H}^+_{\text{lumen}}$$

- **Passive colonic K⁺ secretion (paracellular, voltage-driven):**

  $$\text{K}^+_{\text{blood}} \xrightarrow{\;V_{te}\ (-15\ \text{to}\ -25\ \text{mV})\;} \text{K}^+_{\text{lumen}}$$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The intestines absorb K⁺ in the proximal segments but secrete it in the distal segments." (p. 908)
- > "Dietary K⁺ furnishes 80 to 120 mmol/day, whereas stool K⁺ output is only ~10 mmol/day." (p. 908)
- > "K⁺ absorption in the small intestine is probably passive, most likely a result of solvent drag." (p. 909)
- > "Passive K⁺ secretion … is driven by the lumen-negative Vₜₑ of 15 to 25 mV. The route of passive K⁺ secretion is predominantly paracellular." (p. 909)
- > "The colonic H-K pump is ~60% identical at the amino-acid level to both the Na-K pump and the gastric parietal-cell H-K pump." (p. 910)

## Section 6 — Regulation of intestinal ion transport (pp. 910–913)

### Subsection headings
- **Chemical mediators from the enteric nervous system, endocrine cells, and immune cells in the lamina propria may be either secretagogues or absorptagogues** (pp. 910–911)
- **Secretagogues can be classified by their type and by the intracellular second-messenger system that they stimulate** (pp. 911–912)
- **Mineralocorticoids, glucocorticoids, and somatostatin are absorptagogues** (pp. 912–913)

### Core claims
- **Four modes of regulation:** neural, endocrine, paracrine, autocrine. **Secretagogues** promote secretion; **absorptagogues** promote absorption.
- **Enteric nervous system (ENS).** Secretomotor neurons release **ACh** (+ VIP, 5-HT, histamine) → active Cl⁻ secretion. A secretory stimulus often triggers a coupled propulsive motor response.
- **Endocrine — renin-angiotensin-aldosterone.** Dehydration/volume contraction → ↑ angiotensin + aldosterone. **Angiotensin** stimulates electroneutral NaCl absorption (↑ apical Na-H exchange) in the **small intestine**; **aldosterone** stimulates electrogenic ENaC absorption in the **colon** — a classic endocrine feedback loop restoring total-body Na⁺/fluid (parallel to the renal action, pp. 765–766).
- **Paracrine — immune cells of the lamina propria** (Table 44-3): mast cells (histamine, O₂ radicals), macrophages (prostaglandins), neutrophils (eicosanoids, PAF), fibroblasts (eicosanoids, bradykinin). A single agonist (e.g., histamine) acts at multiple sites — epithelium directly, plus ENS neurons → amplified, multiplexed effects (Fig. 44-7).
- **Secretagogue classification (two axes):** by type — (1) bacterial enterotoxins, (2) hormones/neurotransmitters, (3) immune-cell products, (4) laxatives; and by second messenger — **cAMP, cGMP, Ca²⁺**.
  - **cAMP path:** heat-labile *E. coli* toxin, cholera toxin, VIP → adenylyl cyclase → PKA.
  - **cGMP path:** heat-stable *E. coli* toxin, **guanylin** (15-aa peptide) → receptor guanylyl cyclase (ANP-receptor-like) → PKG.
  - **Ca²⁺ path:** serotonin/5-HT, ACh, bradykinin → PLC → IP₃ → Ca²⁺ release → PKC / CaM kinase.
- **Common end effect (Table 44-4):** all three second messengers **stimulate Cl⁻ secretion** and **inhibit electroneutral NaCl absorption** → net fluid + electrolyte secretion. cAMP and Ca²⁺ are near-equal and strong on both; cGMP is somewhat weaker on Cl⁻ secretion but comparable on inhibiting NaCl absorption.
- **Absorptagogues.** Corticosteroids are the main ones. **Mineralocorticoids** (aldosterone) → ↑ ENaC + Na-K pumps → electrogenic Na⁺ absorption + K⁺ secretion in distal colon (no small-intestinal effect). **Glucocorticoids** → ↑ electroneutral NaCl absorption throughout (own receptor, genomic). **Somatostatin, enkephalins, norepinephrine** → ↑ absorption / ↓ HCO₃⁻ secretion by lowering [Ca²⁺]ᵢ (blocking Ca²⁺ channels).

- > "Most of these agonists (i.e., secretagogues) promote secretion, whereas some others (i.e., absorptagogues) enhance absorption." (p. 910)
- > "Both angiotensin and aldosterone regulate total-body Na⁺ homeostasis by stimulating Na⁺ absorption, angiotensin in the small intestine, and aldosterone in the colon." (p. 911)
- > "An enterotoxin is an exotoxin that induces changes in intestinal fluid and electrolyte movement." (p. 911)

## Glossary

- **Crypt of Lieberkühn** — glandular invagination; site of secretory (crypt) cells.
- **Villus vs. surface cell** — absorptive cell of small intestine vs. colon.
- **Folds of Kerckring / villi / microvilli** — the three levels of small-intestinal surface amplification (~600×, ~200 m²).
- **Solvent drag** — passive solute carriage by bulk water flow; jejunum (Na⁺, urea, K⁺).
- **SGLT1** — apical 2 Na⁺/glucose cotransporter; postprandial Na⁺ absorption; basis of ORS.
- **NHE3 (SLC9A3)** — apical Na-H exchanger; amiloride (mM)-sensitive; duodenum/jejunum and (with DRA) ileum/proximal colon.
- **DRA (SLC26A3)** — apical Cl-HCO₃ exchanger; electroneutral NaCl absorption + HCO₃⁻ secretion; mutated in congenital chloridorrhea.
- **ENaC** — apical epithelial Na⁺ channel; electrogenic Na⁺ absorption in distal colon; amiloride (µM)-sensitive; aldosterone-stimulated.
- **NKCC1 (SLC12A2)** — basolateral 1 Na⁺:1 K⁺:2 Cl⁻ cotransporter; loads Cl⁻ for crypt secretion.
- **CFTR** — apical cAMP-activated Cl⁻ channel; crypt Cl⁻ secretion; secretory diarrhea + cystic fibrosis.
- **CaCC** — Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channel; alternative apical secretory route.
- **Colonic H-K pump** — apical active K⁺ absorption in distal colon; K⁺ depletion-induced.
- **BK / IK1 K⁺ channels** — apical (BK, rate-limiting for active K⁺ secretion) and basolateral K⁺ channels.
- **Secretagogue / absorptagogue** — agents that increase secretion / absorption.
- **Enterotoxin** — bacterial exotoxin altering intestinal fluid/electrolyte movement (cholera, *E. coli*).
- **Guanylin** — endogenous 15-aa cGMP-pathway secretagogue (heat-stable-toxin mimic).
- **Oral rehydration solution (ORS)** — glucose + Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻; exploits cAMP-insensitive SGLT1 absorption.
- **Congenital chloridorrhea** — autosomal-recessive DRA loss → high-Cl⁻ diarrhea + metabolic alkalosis.
