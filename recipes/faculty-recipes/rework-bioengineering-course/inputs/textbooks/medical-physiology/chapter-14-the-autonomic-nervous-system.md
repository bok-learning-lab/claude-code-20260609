# Chapter 14 — The Autonomic Nervous System

> Section III · The Nervous System · pp. 334–352 · Author: George B. Richerson

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Organization of the visceral control system** (pp. 334–340) — three ANS divisions (sympathetic, parasympathetic, enteric); preganglionic vs. postganglionic anatomy; sympathetic chain and prevertebral plexus; parasympathetic cranial and sacral outflow; visceral afferents; enteric plexuses.

## Section 1 — Organization of the visceral control system (pp. 334–340)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The ANS has sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric divisions** (p. 334)
- **Sympathetic preganglionic neurons originate from spinal segments T1 to L3 and synapse with postganglionic neurons in paravertebral or prevertebral ganglia** (pp. 335–336)
- **Parasympathetic preganglionic neurons originate from the brainstem and sacral spinal cord and synapse with postganglionic neurons in ganglia located near target organs** (pp. 338–339)
- **The visceral control system also has an important afferent limb** (p. 339)
- **The enteric division is a self-contained nervous system of the GI tract and receives sympathetic and parasympathetic input** (pp. 339–340)

### Core claims
- **Three divisions.** Output from the CNS to non-skeletal-muscle targets travels along two efferent pathways (sympathetic, parasympathetic); a third division, the **enteric nervous system (ENS)**, is a quasi-independent network of plexuses around the gut. Both efferent divisions use a **two-neuron chain**: preganglionic cell body in the CNS → peripheral ganglion → postganglionic axon → target.
- **Sympathetic = thoracolumbar.** Preganglionic cell bodies sit in the **intermediolateral cell column (lateral horn)** of spinal segments **T1–L3**. Axons exit via ventral roots, branch into **white rami communicantes** (myelinated), and enter the **sympathetic chain (paravertebral) ganglia** running from upper neck to coccyx. Three rostral fates: synapse in the segmental paravertebral ganglion, ascend/descend in the chain, or pass through (greater/lesser splanchnic nerves) to **prevertebral ganglia** (celiac, superior mesenteric, aorticorenal, inferior mesenteric). Postganglionic axons (mostly unmyelinated) rejoin spinal nerves via **gray rami communicantes**.
- **Divergence.** One preganglionic sympathetic axon can synapse on as many as **~200 postganglionic neurons**, supporting the broad, simultaneous output characteristic of the sympathetic division.
- **Rostral ganglia.** The **superior cervical ganglion** (fusion of C1–C4) supplies head and neck; the **middle cervical** + **stellate** (inferior cervical fused with T1) ganglia supply heart, lungs, bronchi.
- **Parasympathetic = craniosacral.** Preganglionic cell bodies sit in brainstem nuclei (with **CN III, VII, IX, X**) and in the **sacral cord (S2–S4)**. Postganglionic neurons sit in **terminal ganglia** in or near target organs, so postganglionic axons are short. Specifically:
  - **Edinger–Westphal nucleus (midbrain) → CN III → ciliary ganglion** → pupillary constrictor + ciliary muscle.
  - **Superior salivatory nucleus (pons/medulla) → CN VII** → pterygopalatine (lacrimal) and submandibular (submandibular/sublingual saliva) ganglia.
  - **Inferior salivatory nucleus / rostral nucleus ambiguus → CN IX → otic ganglion** → parotid.
  - **Nucleus ambiguus + dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus → CN X** → terminal ganglia in thoracic and abdominal viscera (down to the distal colon).
  - **Sacral S2–S4 → pelvic splanchnic nerves** → descending colon, rectum, bladder, reproductive organs.
- **Visceral afferents.** Cell bodies in **dorsal root ganglia** or cranial-nerve ganglia (e.g., **nodose**, **petrosal**); ~90% are unmyelinated. Most non-nociceptive afferents (mechanoreceptor, chemoreceptor, baroreceptor) travel with the **vagus** — which is mostly *afferent*. Most visceral *nociceptive* afferents travel with sympathetic nerves and enter the cord segmentally; viscerotopic mapping exists in cord and brainstem but **not** in cortex, hence **referred pain** to a dermatome (e.g., left-ventricular ischemia → left T1–T5; diaphragm → C3–C5 shoulder pain).
- **Enteric nervous system.** >10⁸ neurons in two plexuses — **myenteric (Auerbach)** between outer longitudinal and inner circular muscle (motility) and **submucosal (Meissner)** (ion/fluid transport). Receives parasympathetic input from CN X (sacral for distal colon) and modulatory sympathetic postganglionic input; the ENS can sustain peristalsis, secretion, and biliary contractions autonomously. Functionally analogous to a vast terminal parasympathetic ganglion.

- > "Preganglionic neurons are found in columns of cells in the brainstem and spinal cord and send axons out of the CNS to make synapses with postganglionic neurons in peripheral ganglia interposed between the CNS and their target cells." (p. 334)
- > "The cell bodies of preganglionic sympathetic motor neurons are located in the thoracic and upper lumbar spinal cord between levels T1 and L3." (p. 335)
- > "It has been estimated that each preganglionic sympathetic neuron branches and synapses on as many as 200 postganglionic neurons." (p. 336)
- > "Unlike the sympathetic — or thoracolumbar — division … the parasympathetic — or craniosacral — division's preganglionic cell bodies are cranial and sacral." (p. 338)
- > "Most fibers in the vagus nerve are afferents, even though all parasympathetic preganglionic output … to the abdominal and thoracic viscera also travels in the vagus nerve." (p. 339)
- > "The enteric nervous system (ENS) is a collection of nerve plexuses that surround the GI tract … The ENS is estimated to contain >100 million neurons." (pp. 339–340)

### Box anchor — PHOX2B (Box 14-1, p. 340)
**PHOX2B** is a transcription factor required for development of nearly all visceral-control neurons (pre/postganglionic parasympathetic, postganglionic sympathetic, enteric, all visceral afferents, NTS) and almost no other neurons (exceptions: locus coeruleus, several cranial-nerve nuclei). Heterozygous mutations cause **congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS / "Ondine curse")**; co-occurrence with **Hirschsprung disease** (aganglionic colon) defines **Haddad syndrome**; some patients develop **neuroblastoma**. PHOX2B is the "master gene" of the visceral control system.

## Section 2 — Synaptic physiology of the ANS (pp. 340–347)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions have opposite effects on most visceral targets** (p. 340)
- **All preganglionic neurons — both sympathetic and parasympathetic — release acetylcholine and stimulate N₂ nicotinic receptors on postganglionic neurons** (p. 341)
- **All postganglionic parasympathetic neurons release ACh and stimulate muscarinic receptors on visceral targets** (pp. 341–342)
- **Most postganglionic sympathetic neurons release norepinephrine onto visceral targets** (pp. 342–343)
- **Postganglionic sympathetic and parasympathetic neurons often have muscarinic as well as nicotinic receptors** (pp. 343–344)
- **Nonclassic transmitters can be released at each level of the ANS** (pp. 344–345)
- **Two of the most unusual nonclassic neurotransmitters, ATP and nitric oxide, were first identified in the ANS** (pp. 345–347)

### Core claims
- **Antagonism is the rule, not the law.** Sympathetic is excitatory and parasympathetic inhibitory in organs activated during exertion (e.g., heart rate); the reverse is true in "vegetative" organs (gut peristalsis). Exceptions: salivary glands receive *both* divisions excitatorily (with different secretion profiles); sweat glands, piloerector muscles, and most peripheral vessels receive *only* sympathetic input.
- **Varicosities and en passant synapses.** Postganglionic autonomic axons bear **bulbous varicosities** along their length rather than terminal boutons; many varicosities form discrete ~50-nm synaptic clefts onto smooth muscle, gland, or cardiac cells, allowing a single axon to influence many targets.
- **Ganglionic transmission (universal).** Every preganglionic neuron — sympathetic *and* parasympathetic — releases **ACh** acting on a **nicotinic** receptor on the postganglionic neuron. The ganglionic isoform is **N₂** (vs. **N₁** at the NMJ): N₂ is stimulated by **tetramethylammonium**, blocked by **hexamethonium**, and is resistant to **d-tubocurarine** (which preferentially blocks N₁). Both are pentameric ligand-gated cation channels (Na⁺/K⁺); reversal ~0 mV; produce **fast EPSPs**.
- **Postganglionic parasympathetic = ACh + muscarinic.** Five M-subtype GPCRs (M₁–M₅) all activated by ACh and muscarine and all blocked by **atropine**. Signal transduction:
  - **M₁, M₃, M₅** → Gαq → PLC → IP₃/DAG → ↑[Ca²⁺]ᵢ and PKC activation.
  - **M₂, M₄** → Gαi/Gαo → ↓adenylyl cyclase → ↓cAMP; can also gate K⁺ channels directly via Gβγ.
  - Responses are slow and prolonged compared with nicotinic EPSPs.
- **Postganglionic sympathetic = norepinephrine + adrenergic.** Two families of GPCRs (α, β), each with multiple subtypes (α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, β₃):
  - **α₁** (blood vessels) → Gαq → PLC → IP₃/DAG, ↑Ca²⁺ (vasoconstriction).
  - **α₂** (presynaptic terminals) → Gαi → ↓cAMP (autoinhibition of release).
  - **β₁** (heart) → Gαs → ↑cAMP (↑rate, ↑contractility).
  - **β₂** (bronchial smooth muscle, vessels in skeletal muscle) → Gαs → ↑cAMP (relaxation).
  - **β₃** (fat) → Gαs → ↑cAMP (lipolysis).
  - In general, **α receptors have higher affinity for NE**; **β receptors have higher affinity for epinephrine**.
- **Exception.** Sympathetic innervation of **sweat glands** is *cholinergic* and acts through *muscarinic* receptors.
- **Adrenal medulla.** A specialized "sympathetic ganglion": preganglionic sympathetic axons release ACh onto **chromaffin cells** bearing N₂ receptors; chromaffin cells release predominantly **epinephrine** directly into the bloodstream rather than synapsing on a specific target — a neuroendocrine broadcast channel.
- **Dual fast/slow ganglionic responses and the M current.** Ganglion-cell intracellular records show a **fast nicotinic EPSP** followed by a **slow muscarinic EPSP** (or IPSP, depending on subtype). A key muscarinic action is suppression of a hyperpolarizing K⁺ current — the **M current** — widely expressed in ganglia and CNS. With the M current active, current injection elicits a single AP; muscarinic block of the M current produces a steady-state depolarization and converts the same stimulus into a train of APs — i.e., slow muscarinic modulation toggles cellular excitability.
- **Cotransmission / nonclassic NANC transmitters.** Many ANS neurons co-release **ATP**, **NO**, **VIP**, **NPY**, **substance P**, **CGRP**, **enkephalins**, **5-HT**, **somatostatin**, **TRH**, and others. Co-release ratios are activity-dependent (e.g., medullary raphé neurons release 5-HT alone at low frequency; 5-HT + TRH at intermediate; 5-HT + TRH + substance P at high firing rates — a "chemical coding").
- **ATP** is colocalized with NE in vasoconstrictor sympathetic terminals; it acts on **P2X** ligand-gated cation channels (high Ca²⁺ permeability) and **P2Y/P2U** GPCRs on smooth muscle and produces the fastest phase of vasoconstriction (faster than NE through α₁, which produces the intermediate phase; NPY through Y1 receptors gives the slowest, most prolonged phase). See Fig. 14-10.
- **NO** is produced from L-arginine by **nitric oxide synthase (NOS)**; diffuses to neighboring smooth-muscle cells; activates **guanylyl cyclase** → ↑cGMP → relaxation. Found in preganglionic and postganglionic neurons of both divisions and in vascular endothelium. In Fig. 14-11, a parasympathetic varicosity co-releases NO, ACh, and **VIP**: NO diffuses directly to smooth muscle (rapid relaxation); ACh hits **M₃** on endothelium → endothelial NO synthase → more NO (also rapid relaxation); VIP binds smooth-muscle receptors to produce a delayed, prolonged relaxation via cAMP / decreased Ca²⁺.

- > "Many postganglionic autonomic neurons have bulbous expansions, or varicosities, that are distributed along their axons within their target organ." (p. 341)
- > "Both nicotinic transmission triggered by stimulation of preganglionic neurons leads to rapid depolarization of postganglionic neurons." (p. 341)
- > "All postganglionic parasympathetic neurons act through muscarinic ACh receptors on the postsynaptic target." (p. 341)
- > "Most postganglionic sympathetic neurons release norepinephrine, which acts on target cells through adrenergic receptors. The sympathetic innervation of sweat glands is an exception to this rule." (pp. 342–343)
- > "The adrenal medulla is a special adaptation of the sympathetic division, homologous to a postganglionic sympathetic neuron." (p. 342)
- > "Norepinephrine and epinephrine both activate all five subtypes of adrenergic receptor, but with different affinities. In general, the α receptors have a greater affinity for norepinephrine, whereas the β receptors have a greater affinity for epinephrine." (pp. 342–343)
- > "The slow, modulatory effects of muscarinic responses greatly enhance the ability of the ANS to control visceral activity beyond what could be accomplished with only fast nicotinic EPSPs." (pp. 343–344)
- > "Nitric oxide (NO) and ATP were first identified and studied as neurotransmitters in the ANS, but they are now known to be more widely used throughout the nervous system." (pp. 345–346)

## Section 3 — CNS control of the viscera (pp. 347–352)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Sympathetic output can be massive and nonspecific, as in the fight-or-flight response, or selective for specific target organs** (pp. 347–348)
- **Parasympathetic neurons participate in many simple involuntary reflexes** (p. 348)
- **A variety of brainstem nuclei provide basic control of the ANS** (pp. 348–349)
- **The forebrain can modulate autonomic output, and reciprocally, visceral sensory input integrated in the brainstem can influence or even overwhelm the forebrain** (pp. 349–350)
- **CNS control centers oversee visceral feedback loops and orchestrate a feed-forward response to meet anticipated needs** (pp. 350–351)
- **The ANS has multiple levels of reflex loops** (pp. 351–352)

### Core claims
- **Cannon's mass-discharge hypothesis (1915) is half-right.** The **fight-or-flight response** is real: a coordinated, near-simultaneous sympathetic surge yielding ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑BP, ↑ventilation, bronchodilation, sweating, piloerection, hyperglycemia (with ↓insulin), ↓clotting time, splenic contraction, and ↓GI activity; pathological version = **panic attack**. But sympathetic output can *also* be organ-specific via "chemical coding" — different postganglionic populations release distinct combinations of NE + cotransmitters (e.g., lumbar vasoconstrictors release NE + ATP + NPY; inferior mesenteric neurons release NE + somatostatin to control gut motility).
- **Parasympathetic output is reflex-specific, never mass-discharge.** Examples: **baroreflex** (dorsal motor nucleus of vagus → ↓HR on BP rise), **micturition reflex** (sacral preganglionic → bladder distention sensor → detrusor contraction + internal sphincter relaxation, pp. 736–737), **pupillary light reflex** (Edinger–Westphal → CN III → ciliary ganglion → pupil constrictor, p. 362), **salivation** to food sight/smell, **vagovagal reflexes** (e.g., gastrocolic), **bronchoconstriction** to lung irritant receptors.
- **Brainstem command centers** (Table 14-4):
  - **Nucleus tractus solitarii (NTS)** — second-order sensory hub. Receives all visceral chemoreceptor and baroreceptor input via CN IX (carotid body, carotid sinus) and CN X (aortic body, aortic arch, plus non-nociceptive afferents from every thoracic and abdominal organ), plus CN VII (taste), CN V (teeth/sinuses). Distributes viscerotopically. Output to **intermediolateral cell column** (sympathetic preganglionics), **sacral parasympathetic**, **phrenic motor nucleus**, **dorsal motor nucleus of vagus**, **nucleus ambiguus**, **ventrolateral medulla**, **medullary raphé**, **area postrema**, **parabrachial**, **hypothalamus**, **cortex**. The major lower-brainstem command center.
  - **Rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM)** — sympathetic premotor output to cardiovascular system; stimulation raises sympathetic outflow to the heart and vessels selectively.
  - **Medullary raphé** — serotonergic neurons projecting widely (cardiovascular, respiratory, GI, reticular, pain) — sets *autonomic tone*.
  - **Locus coeruleus, parabrachial nucleus, area postrema, periaqueductal gray** — additional modulators.
- **Hypothalamus** — particularly the **paraventricular nucleus** — is the dominant rostral coordinator. Projects to parabrachial, medullary raphé, NTS, periaqueductal gray, locus coeruleus, dorsal motor nucleus of vagus, nucleus ambiguus, and intermediolateral cell column. Integrates autonomic output with feeding, thermoregulation, circadian rhythms, water balance, emotion, sexual behavior, neuroendocrine pituitary control; can initiate fight-or-flight directly.
- **Cortex → ANS.** Direct neocortical projections to brainstem autonomic centers are sparse; most descending influence goes through limbic structures (amygdala, insula, prefrontal, entorhinal). This sparseness explains why **biofeedback** can produce only modest effects on global ANS state and almost no organ-specific cortical control.
- **ANS → cortex.** Visceral afferents reach the neocortex but without viscerotopic map — hence we can't localize gut sensation precisely. Visceral input can nonetheless dominate cortex (hunger, nausea, dyspnea, visceral pain, bladder/bowel distention, thermal stress). **Vagus nerve stimulation** (a surgically implanted device delivering 30 s every 5 min) is FDA-approved for refractory epilepsy and reduces seizures by ~50% in ~25% of patients (Box 14-2).
- **Feedback + feed-forward.** Most homeostatic loops are feedback (BP set-point via baroreflex). But the ANS also anticipates: HR rises *before* the gun in a sprinter; sympathetic outflow rises at the start of exercise so that alveolar ventilation overshoots and arterial Pco₂ *drops* at exercise onset (the opposite of what pure feedback would predict).
- **Hierarchical reflex loops, phylogenetically nested:**
  - ENS ↔ jellyfish neural net (motility, absorption, local reflexes).
  - Autonomic ganglia ↔ annelid ganglionic nervous system — *not* simple relays: superior cervical ganglion has ~10⁶ neurons + interneurons + a dense neuropil supporting peripheral integration.
  - Spinal cord ↔ amphioxus — segmental autonomic + somatic reflexes; preserved after high-cervical transection (with respiratory support).
  - Medulla — minimum viable visceral controller; destruction = instant death; sufficient (with cord + peripheral ANS) for indefinite homeostasis.
  - Midbrain, hypothalamus, limbic, neocortex — successively more rostral modulators, not essential for survival of basic homeostasis.

### Clinical anchors
- **Vagus nerve stimulation for refractory epilepsy** (Box 14-2, p. 350): implanted device fires 30 s every 5 min; halves seizure frequency in ~25% of patients; side effects = hoarseness, cough, breathlessness.
- **Autonomic crosstalk pathologies** (Box 14-3, p. 351): respiratory sinus arrhythmia (normal physiologic spillover); micturition → cardiorespiratory link → **postmicturition syncope** when an obstructed full bladder is drained too rapidly; full bladder → apnea + hypertension.
- **Autonomic hyper-reflexia** (after high cord transection): caudal cord + ANS still functions, but loss of supraspinal modulation lets a full bladder produce paroxysmal hypertension and sweating.
- **Horner syndrome** (Box 14-4 + Fig. 14-12, pp. 351–352): unilateral **ptosis + miosis + anhidrosis** from loss of sympathetic innervation to ipsilateral face/eye. Localized along a three-neuron pathway: 1st-order hypothalamus → intermediolateral cell column (lesions: Wallenberg / lateral medullary stroke from posterior inferior cerebellar artery occlusion); 2nd-order preganglionic from IML → superior cervical ganglion (lesions: Pancoast tumor at lung apex); 3rd-order postganglionic from superior cervical along the carotid (lesions: **carotid dissection** — a Horner here is a warning of impending stroke). Two bedside tests:
  - **2–10% cocaine** (blocks NE reuptake) — fails to dilate a Horner pupil at any lesion level (insufficient NE in the cleft).
  - **Hydroxyamphetamine (Paredrine)** — releases NE from intact postganglionic terminals; fails to dilate only in 3rd-order (postganglionic) Horner.

## Equations and quantitative anchors

The chapter is largely qualitative; numerical anchors are:

- **Sympathetic divergence:** one preganglionic axon → ≤ ~200 postganglionic neurons.
- **ENS neuron count:** > 10⁸; exceeds spinal cord neuron count.
- **Superior cervical ganglion neuron count:** ~10⁶ (plus interneurons).
- **Synaptic cleft width** at autonomic en passant synapses: ~50 nm.
- **Visceral afferents** that are unmyelinated: ~90%.
- **Vagal afferents:** the vagus is *mostly* afferent (sensory) despite carrying all CN X parasympathetic preganglionic output to the thoracoabdominal viscera.
- **Vagus-nerve stimulation for epilepsy:** ~50% reduction in seizure count in ~25% of patients; duty cycle 30 s on / 5 min off, 24 h/day.

## Glossary

- **Autonomic nervous system (ANS)** — efferent visceral motor system + visceral afferents + central control nuclei; three divisions: sympathetic, parasympathetic, enteric.
- **Preganglionic / postganglionic neuron** — the two-neuron efferent chain.
- **Intermediolateral cell column (lateral horn)** — sympathetic preganglionic cell-body location, T1–L3.
- **Paravertebral (sympathetic chain) / prevertebral (celiac, superior mesenteric, aorticorenal, inferior mesenteric) ganglia.**
- **Superior cervical / middle cervical / stellate ganglion** — rostral sympathetic chain.
- **White ramus communicans** (myelinated preganglionic) / **gray ramus communicans** (unmyelinated postganglionic).
- **Greater / lesser splanchnic nerve** — preganglionic axons to prevertebral ganglia.
- **Edinger–Westphal nucleus / superior salivatory nucleus / inferior salivatory nucleus / nucleus ambiguus / dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus** — cranial parasympathetic preganglionic nuclei.
- **Ciliary / pterygopalatine / submandibular / otic / terminal / pelvic ganglia** — parasympathetic postganglionic locations.
- **Pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2–S4)** — sacral parasympathetic outflow.
- **Vagus nerve (CN X)** — bulk of parasympathetic preganglionic outflow + bulk of non-nociceptive visceral afferents.
- **Nodose / petrosal ganglia** — vagal / glossopharyngeal sensory ganglia.
- **Visceral afferent / referred pain.**
- **Enteric nervous system (ENS); myenteric (Auerbach) plexus / submucosal (Meissner) plexus.**
- **PHOX2B** — master transcription factor of the visceral control system; CCHS / Haddad syndrome.
- **Varicosity / en passant synapse.**
- **Nicotinic N₁ (NMJ, d-tubocurarine-sensitive) vs. N₂ (ganglionic, hexamethonium-sensitive).**
- **Muscarinic M₁–M₅** — atropine-blocked GPCRs; M₁/M₃/M₅ → Gq; M₂/M₄ → Gi/o.
- **Adrenergic α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, β₃** — GPCRs; α₁ → Gq (vasoconstriction), α₂ → Gi (presynaptic autoinhibition), β₁ → Gs (cardiac), β₂ → Gs (bronchodilation), β₃ → Gs (lipolysis).
- **Chromaffin cell / adrenal medulla** — neuroendocrine sympathetic ganglion analogue; secretes epinephrine.
- **M current** — muscarinic-suppressible K⁺ current; gates ganglion-cell excitability.
- **Cotransmission / chemical coding / NANC (nonadrenergic noncholinergic) transmission.**
- **ATP / P2X / P2Y / P2U receptors.**
- **NO / nitric oxide synthase / guanylyl cyclase / cGMP.**
- **VIP / NPY / substance P / CGRP / TRH / 5-HT** — ANS cotransmitters.
- **Nucleus tractus solitarii (NTS)** — major visceral afferent relay.
- **Rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM)** — sympathetic premotor center.
- **Medullary raphé / locus coeruleus / area postrema / parabrachial nucleus / periaqueductal gray** — brainstem autonomic modulators.
- **Paraventricular nucleus of hypothalamus** — dominant rostral autonomic coordinator.
- **Baroreflex / micturition reflex / pupillary light reflex / vagovagal reflex.**
- **Fight-or-flight response / panic attack.**
- **Vasovagal syncope / postmicturition syncope / autonomic hyper-reflexia.**
- **Horner syndrome (ptosis + miosis + anhidrosis)** — three-neuron sympathetic pathway lesion (Wallenberg, Pancoast tumor, carotid dissection).
- **Atropine, hexamethonium, d-tubocurarine, phentolamine, yohimbine, metoprolol, propranolol (β-blockers), phenylephrine, clonidine, dobutamine, isoproterenol, terbutaline, salbutamol, pirenzepine, methoctramine, cocaine (NE-reuptake block), hydroxyamphetamine** — key pharmacology.
- **Pancoast tumor / Wallenberg syndrome / carotid dissection** — neuroanatomical localization anchors.
- **Vagus nerve stimulator** — implanted device for refractory epilepsy.
