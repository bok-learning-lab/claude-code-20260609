## Section 1 — Extracellular space of the CNS (pp. 275–278)

### Subsection headings
- **The brain's extracellular space is small and tightly regulated** (pp. 275–276)
- **The brain has three fluid compartments: interstitial fluid (ISF), CSF, and intravascular blood** (pp. 276–277)
- **CSF and ISF communicate freely across the pia and ependyma** (pp. 277–278)

### Core claims
- **Brain extracellular space (ECS)** is ~15–20% of brain volume — surprisingly small given the metabolic and signaling demands. The narrow extracellular sleeves between cells (~20 nm typical) confine diffusion and demand tight ionic regulation.
- **CSF volume in adults**: total ~140–150 mL; ventricular ~25 mL, subarachnoid ~125 mL.
- **CSF turnover**: 500 mL/day in adults — total CSF replaced ~3–4× per day.
- **ISF and CSF communicate** freely across the pia (subarachnoid space) and ependyma (ventricular surface). Composition is similar but not identical.
- **Composition of CSF vs. plasma** (Table 11-1 territory):

| Solute | CSF | Plasma |
|---|---|---|
| Na⁺ (mM) | 147 | 142 |
| K⁺ (mM) | 2.9 | 4.4 |
| Ca²⁺ (mM) | 1.2 | 2.4 |
| Mg²⁺ (mM) | 1.1 | 0.8 |
| Cl⁻ (mM) | 130 | 102 |
| HCO₃⁻ (mM) | 22 | 24 |
| Glucose (mg/dL) | 60 | 90 |
| Protein (mg/dL) | 15–45 | 6000–8000 |
| pH | 7.33 | 7.40 |

  CSF is **lower in K⁺ and protein**, slightly higher in Cl⁻ and Mg²⁺ — actively regulated, not a simple plasma ultrafiltrate.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The brain's extracellular space is small and tightly regulated." (p. 275)
- > "The composition of CSF is actively regulated by the choroid plexus and differs from that of plasma." (p. 277)
- > "CSF and ISF communicate freely across the pia and ependyma." (p. 277)

## Section 2 — The CSF system (pp. 278–283)

### Subsection headings
- **The choroid plexus secretes CSF into the ventricles** (pp. 278–280)
- **CSF circulates from lateral to third to fourth ventricle and exits into the subarachnoid space** (pp. 280–281)
- **CSF is absorbed at the arachnoid villi into the dural venous sinuses** (pp. 281–282)
- **The brain floats in CSF, which acts as a shock absorber** (p. 282)
- **The Monro–Kellie doctrine: brain + blood + CSF = constant intracranial volume** (pp. 282–283)

### Core claims — choroid plexus secretion
- Choroid plexus is a specialized epithelium covering vascular fronds protruding into the **lateral, third, and fourth ventricles**.
- The choroid epithelial cell has **fenestrated capillaries beneath** and a **tight epithelial layer above** with basolateral (blood-facing) and apical (CSF-facing) membranes.
- Net transport: Na⁺ enters at the basolateral side via NHE3 + NBCe2; K⁺ and Cl⁻ trafficking; the apical Na/K pump faces **opposite the typical epithelium orientation** (i.e., apical, facing CSF) — pumping Na⁺ into CSF. Apical AQP1 mediates water flux. Carbonic anhydrase generates HCO₃⁻ that is co-secreted with Na⁺. Net flux: Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻ + water into the ventricle.
- **Pharmacology**: **acetazolamide** (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) reduces CSF production; clinically used in idiopathic intracranial hypertension.

### Core claims — circulation and absorption
- Path of CSF flow: **lateral ventricles → foramina of Monro → third ventricle → cerebral aqueduct (of Sylvius) → fourth ventricle → exits via the foramina of Luschka (lateral) and Magendie (medial) → subarachnoid space (cisterns and over the convexities) → arachnoid villi → dural sinuses → systemic venous blood.**
- **Arachnoid villi (granulations)** are one-way valves: bulk flow occurs when intracranial CSF pressure exceeds sinus venous pressure by ~5 mm Hg.
- **Newly recognized**: a glymphatic system (perivascular CSF–ISF exchange driven by AQP4-rich astrocyte endfeet) provides additional bulk clearance, especially during sleep. Cited in updated chapter editions and increasingly in clinical literature on Aβ clearance in Alzheimer's.

### Core claims — Monro–Kellie and intracranial pressure
- **Monro–Kellie doctrine**: the skull is a rigid box with three components — brain (~80%), blood (~10%), CSF (~10%). Their **total volume is constant**; an increase in any one must be compensated by a decrease in another, or **intracranial pressure (ICP)** rises.

  $$V_{\text{brain}} + V_{\text{blood}} + V_{\text{CSF}} = V_{\text{cranial vault}}$$

- **Normal ICP** is 5–15 mm Hg in adults supine; >20 mm Hg sustained is pathological.
- **Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP)** = MAP − ICP. Critical for avoiding ischemia:

  $$\text{CPP} = \text{MAP} - \text{ICP}$$

  CPP < 50 mm Hg compromises autoregulation; CPP < 30 mm Hg risks infarction.

### Box 11-1 — Lumbar puncture (clinical anchor)
Indications: diagnostic (meningitis, subarachnoid hemorrhage, MS, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, Guillain–Barré), therapeutic (drainage, intrathecal drug delivery — chemotherapy, baclofen, antibiotics). Technique: needle enters between L3/L4 or L4/L5 (below the conus medullaris in adults at L1–L2) through the dura into the subarachnoid space; CSF drips out under gravity at ~70–180 mm H₂O opening pressure. **Contraindication**: signs of mass effect with raised ICP, because pressure differential across the foramen magnum can cause uncal or tonsillar herniation. CT/MRI before LP when focal findings are present.

## Section 3 — The blood–brain barrier (pp. 283–288)

### Subsection headings
- **The BBB is formed by cerebral endothelial cells with continuous tight junctions** (pp. 283–284)
- **Selective transport across the BBB requires specific carriers and transporters** (pp. 284–286)
- **Circumventricular organs lack a BBB and sample the systemic milieu** (pp. 286–287)
- **The BBB can be disrupted by hypoxia, inflammation, and disease** (pp. 287–288)

### Core claims — BBB architecture
- The CNS capillary endothelium has:
  - **Continuous tight junctions** (claudin-5, occludin, ZO-1) — orders of magnitude tighter than peripheral capillary junctions; trans-endothelial electrical resistance ~1500 Ω·cm².
  - **No fenestrations**, low transcytotic activity.
  - **Pericytes** ensheathing the abluminal endothelium.
  - **Astrocyte endfeet** covering >99% of the capillary surface; secrete factors that induce and maintain the barrier phenotype.
- Functional consequences: passive diffusion only for small, lipid-soluble molecules. Polar molecules (glucose, amino acids, ions) require specific transporters.

### Core claims — BBB transport
- **Glucose**: GLUT1 (high abundance) — bidirectional facilitated diffusion. Brain extracts ~10% of plasma glucose at first pass.
- **Amino acids**: LAT1 (large neutral AAs), CAT1 (cationic), EAAT (acidic) — selective routes; competition matters (e.g., phenylalanine excess in PKU competes with tyrosine and tryptophan uptake → neurotransmitter precursor depletion).
- **Lipid-soluble drugs**: easily cross (e.g., diazepam, propofol, ethanol).
- **Polar drugs and large molecules**: excluded unless they have a carrier or undergo receptor-mediated transcytosis (transferrin–Fe complex; LDL; insulin).
- **Efflux pumps**: P-glycoprotein/MDR1, MRPs, BCRP on luminal endothelial membrane actively pump out many drugs → severely limits CNS drug delivery.

### Circumventricular organs (CVOs)
Specialized brain regions that **lack a BBB**, allowing direct sampling of systemic blood or secretion into it:
- **OVLT, SFO** — osmosensors; trigger thirst and vasopressin release (Ch 38).
- **Area postrema** — chemoreceptor trigger zone for emesis (and target of antiemetic drugs).
- **Median eminence** — releases hypothalamic hormones into hypophyseal portal blood (Ch 47).
- **Posterior pituitary** — releases ADH and oxytocin into systemic blood.
- **Pineal gland** — releases melatonin into systemic blood.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The blood-brain barrier is formed by the continuous tight junctions between brain capillary endothelial cells." (p. 283)
- > "Lipid-soluble compounds can diffuse passively across the blood-brain barrier; polar compounds and ions require specific transporters." (p. 284)
- > "The circumventricular organs lack a blood-brain barrier, which allows them to sense blood-borne signals." (p. 286)
- > "P-glycoprotein and other efflux transporters limit drug penetration into the CNS." (p. 285)
- > "Astrocytes have foot processes that contact the abluminal surface of cerebral capillaries and induce the barrier phenotype of endothelial cells." (p. 283)

## Section 4 — Brain ion homeostasis and astrocyte function (pp. 288–292)

### Subsection headings
- **Extracellular K⁺ is tightly regulated; astrocytes provide spatial buffering** (pp. 288–289)
- **Glutamate is recycled by the glutamate–glutamine cycle** (pp. 289–290)
- **Brain pH is regulated by transmembrane HCO₃⁻ and H⁺ transport** (pp. 290–291)
- **Astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle couples activity to energy substrate supply** (pp. 291–292)

### Core claims
- **K⁺ spatial buffering**: during neuronal activity, $[\mathrm{K^+}]_o$ can rise from ~3 mM to 4–10 mM (and as high as 50–80 mM during spreading depression). Astrocytes take up excess K⁺ via Kir4.1 (and Na/K pump) at the active site and release it at distant sites where $[\mathrm{K^+}]_o$ is lower. The astrocyte syncytium (gap-junction-coupled by Cx43/Cx30) acts as a current sink. Disruption (e.g., Kir4.1 mutations) causes seizure phenotypes.
- **Glutamate–glutamine cycle**: synaptic glutamate is taken up mainly by astrocytic EAAT1 (GLAST) and EAAT2 (GLT-1) — together ~90% of brain glutamate uptake. In astrocytes, **glutamine synthetase** converts glutamate to glutamine; glutamine is exported back to neurons (via SN1/SN2 transporters) where **glutaminase** regenerates glutamate. Termination of glutamatergic transmission, prevention of excitotoxicity, and nitrogen handling are all served by this cycle.
- **Brain pH regulation**: neurons and astrocytes express NHE1, NBCe1 (electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransporter), AE3 (anion exchanger), and MCT (lactate/H⁺). pH transients accompany activity but are clamped within ~0.1 unit at steady state.
- **Astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle (ANLS)**: increased neuronal activity → glutamate uptake by astrocytes → Na⁺ co-entry → activates astrocyte Na/K pump → triggers glycolysis → lactate released via MCT1/4 → neurons take up lactate via MCT2 → enter the TCA cycle as a primary aerobic substrate. The model is contested but explains key features of fMRI BOLD signal and PET fluorodeoxyglucose uptake.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Astrocytes provide spatial buffering of extracellular K⁺ during neuronal activity." (p. 288)
- > "Glutamate released at synapses is mostly taken up by astrocytes and converted to glutamine, which is recycled back to neurons." (p. 289)
- > "Astrocytes and neurons cooperate to regulate brain pH." (p. 290)
- > "The astrocyte–neuron lactate shuttle hypothesis links synaptic activity to glucose utilization." (p. 291)

## Section 5 — Cerebral edema, hypoxia, ischemia (pp. 292–294)

### Subsection headings
- **Cerebral edema is either vasogenic or cytotoxic** (pp. 292–293)
- **Brain hypoxia and ischemia cause excitotoxicity** (pp. 293–294)

### Core claims
- **Vasogenic edema** — BBB breakdown allows protein-rich fluid into the ECS. Causes: tumor, abscess, infarction (after BBB damage), trauma. Treatment: corticosteroids (effective in tumor-related edema by tightening junctions), osmotic diuresis (mannitol, hypertonic saline).
- **Cytotoxic edema** — cellular swelling without BBB disruption. Cause: Na/K pump failure (ischemia, hypoxia) → cells take on water osmotically. Acute: minutes to hours; sustained cytotoxic edema causes irreversible cell death.
- **Interstitial edema** — periventricular accumulation when CSF outflow is obstructed (hydrocephalus); periventricular white-matter signal change on MRI.
- **Excitotoxicity cascade** during ischemia:
  1. Energy failure → Na/K pump fails → membrane depolarizes.
  2. Glutamate is released massively (and reuptake reversed).
  3. **NMDA-receptor activation** → Ca²⁺ floods neurons → activates Ca²⁺-dependent proteases (calpains), phospholipases (PLA₂), and NOS → cellular damage.
  4. Mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload → permeability transition pore opens → cytochrome c release → apoptosis or necrosis.
- **Neuroprotection** approaches (largely unsuccessful in trials but conceptually important): NMDA antagonists, free-radical scavengers, hypothermia, glucose control, blood-pressure management to maintain CPP.
- **Hydrocephalus** — communicating (impaired absorption at arachnoid villi after subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis) vs. obstructive/non-communicating (aqueductal stenosis, Chiari malformation, tumor). Treatment: ventriculoperitoneal shunting, endoscopic third ventriculostomy.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Cerebral edema is classified as vasogenic, cytotoxic, or interstitial." (p. 292)
- > "Cytotoxic edema is caused by failure of the Na/K pump, leading to cellular swelling." (p. 292)
- > "Massive glutamate release during ischemia leads to excitotoxic neuronal injury." (p. 293)
- > "Hydrocephalus is the abnormal accumulation of CSF in the ventricles." (p. 293)

## Equations summary

- **Monro–Kellie**: $V_{\text{brain}} + V_{\text{blood}} + V_{\text{CSF}} = \text{const}$.
- **Cerebral perfusion pressure**: $\text{CPP} = \text{MAP} - \text{ICP}$.
- **CSF production rate**: ~0.35 mL/min (≈500 mL/day).
- **Choroid Na/HCO₃ stoichiometry**: $1\,\text{Na}^+ : 2\,\text{HCO}_3^-$ via NBCe2 (basolateral entry).
- **BBB endothelial TEER**: ≥1500 Ω·cm² (vs. ~3 Ω·cm² for muscle capillary).
- **K⁺ activity-dependent rise**: $\Delta[\mathrm{K^+}]_o \approx 1{-}5\,\text{mM}$ in normal activity; up to ~50 mM in seizure / spreading depression.
- **Brain glucose extraction**: ~10% of plasma glucose per pass; CMRglc ~30 μmol/100 g/min in cortex.
