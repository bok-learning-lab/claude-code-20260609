## Section 1 — The environment (pp. 1223–1226)

### Subsection headings
- **Voluntary feedback control mechanisms can modulate the many layers of our external environment** (pp. 1223–1224)
- **Environmental temperature provides conscious clues for triggering voluntary feedback mechanisms** (p. 1224)
- **Room ventilation should maintain P_O₂, P_CO₂, and levels of toxic substances within acceptable limits** (pp. 1224–1225)
- **Tissues must resist the G force produced by gravity and other mechanisms of acceleration** (pp. 1225–1226)
- **The partial pressures of gases—other than water—inside the body depend on PB** (p. 1226)

### Core claims
- **Milieu extérieur** (Claude Bernard's term extended): the external environment surrounds the organism in layers — skin surface, surrounding air, clothing, housing, natural environment. The chapter studies how PB and gravity at the outermost layers perturb the milieu intérieur.
- Body core temperature is normally **36–38 °C**. A naked person is in a neutral thermal environment at **26–27 °C, 50% RH**; >35 °C risks heat stroke; <20 °C drives vasoconstriction and cold pain. Voluntary actions (clothing, A/C, shelter) form the effector limb of a conscious negative-feedback loop.
- **Room ventilation (V̇_room)** must supply O₂ and remove CO₂ to keep partial pressures acceptable. Dry sea-level air: P_O₂ ≈ 159 mm Hg (20.95%), P_CO₂ ≈ 0.2 mm Hg (0.03%). OSHA acceptable lower limit for O₂ = 19.5% (148 mm Hg); acceptable upper limit for P_CO₂ ≈ 3.8 mm Hg (0.5%).
- **Carbon monoxide** is insidious because symptomless: it is lethal when it occupies ~half of Hb binding sites, occurring at P_CO ≈ 0.13 mm Hg ≈ 170 ppm. The half-time for CO wash-in/wash-out is ~4 hours, so toxicity accrues slowly; ~12.5% saturation after 2 h gives mild headache/nausea, ~25% after 4 h gives impaired cognition and unconsciousness.
- **Threshold limit values (TLVs)** and **biological exposure indices (BEIs, e.g. carboxyhemoglobin)** bound safe occupational exposure.
- **G force** is dimensionless: the experienced force (mass × acceleration) relative to gravitational force. Bone tolerates ~+5.7G axially if the spine is straight, but only ~+3G if curved (the jump-and-land case). Immobilization/space flight demineralize bone — bed rest for 6–7 wk cost 14 g bone calcium, 1.7 kg muscle, 21% gastrocnemius strength, 6% blood volume.
- **Master rule (the foundation for the rest of the chapter):** by Dalton's law PB is the sum of partial pressures of constituent gases, so each gas's partial pressure changes **in proportion to PB** as PB rises (diving) or falls (altitude) — *except water vapor*, whose partial pressure depends only on temperature (P_H₂O ≈ 47 mm Hg at 37 °C). Thus P_H₂O is negligible at depth but dominant at altitude.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Dalton's law (see Box 26-2) states that PB is the sum of the partial pressures of the individual gases in the air mixture." (p. 1226)
- > "As PB increases during diving beneath the water, or as PB decreases during ascent to high altitude, the partial pressure of each constituent gas in dry ambient air changes in proportion to the change in PB." (p. 1226)
- > "When a gas is in equilibrium with liquid water … the partial pressure of water vapor (P_H₂O) depends not on PB but on temperature." (p. 1226)
- > "CO can be lethal when it occupies approximately half of the binding sites on hemoglobin (Hb), which occurs at a P_CO of ~0.13 mm Hg or … 170 parts per million (ppm)." (p. 1225)
- > "More insidious than hypoxia, and less noticeable, is the symptomless encroachment of carbon monoxide (CO) gas on the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve." (p. 1225)

### Equations

- **Gravitational force (weight)** — Eq. 61-4:

  $$F = m \cdot g, \qquad g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m\cdot s^{-2}}$$

- **G force** (dimensionless) — Eq. 61-5:

  $$G = \frac{m \cdot a}{m \cdot g} = \frac{a}{g}$$

  +1G = standing at rest; jumping/landing ≈ +3G; high-performance flight can exceed +8G.

- **Room-ventilation exponential-decay (washout) method** — Eq. 61-2:

  $$\dot V_{room} = \frac{V_{room}}{\Delta t}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{C_{initial}}{C_{final}}\right)$$

  Worked example (Eq. 61-3): a 27,000 L room, CO₂ falling 3.0% → 1.5% over 10 min gives V̇_room ≈ 1871 L/min.

## Section 2 — Diving physiology / hyperbaria (pp. 1226–1230)

### Subsection headings
- **Immersion raises PB, thereby compressing gases in the lungs** (pp. 1226–1227)
- **SCUBA divers breathe compressed air to maintain normal lung expansion** (pp. 1227–1228)
- **Increased alveolar P_N₂ can cause narcosis** (p. 1228)
- **Increased alveolar P_O₂ can lead to O₂ toxicity** (pp. 1228–1229)
- **Using helium to replace inspired N₂ and O₂ avoids nitrogen narcosis and O₂ toxicity** (p. 1229)
- **After an extended dive, one must decompress slowly to avoid decompression illness** (pp. 1229–1230)

### Core claims
- **Pressure with depth:** water is virtually incompressible, so PB rises *linearly* with depth. Seawater (density ~2.5% > fresh water) adds **1 atm per 10 m**. At sea surface PB = 1 atm; at 10 m PB = 2 atm; at 20 m, 3 atm; etc. (Fig. 61-1).
- **Boyle's law and barotrauma:** P and V vary inversely, so a breath-hold descent to 10 m halves lung gas volume. Stiff/non-equilibrating air cavities (middle ear with a blocked eustachian tube, sinuses) are distorted — pain, middle-ear hemorrhage, or tympanic-membrane rupture (**barotrauma**). Compliant cavities (intestines) compress freely.
- **Breath-hold diving:** human chest cannot fully empty (record breath-hold dive >200 m); aquatic mammals' flexible ribs let lungs collapse (whales: ~2 h, to ~900 m / 91 atm). On descent, rising alveolar P_CO₂ (doubling to ~80 mm Hg at 2 atm) reverses CO₂ diffusion so alveolar CO₂ enters blood; on rapid ascent the falling PB drops alveolar P_O₂ and P_CO₂, the falling P_aCO₂ removes ventilatory drive, and falling P_aO₂ → cerebral hypoxia → **deep-water blackout**.
- **SCUBA / surface-supplied air** keeps airway pressure equal to ambient water pressure, so the trans-chest-wall gradient is normal and the lungs stay normally expanded (Fig. 61-3).
- **Nitrogen narcosis:** by Henry's law, the rising inspired P_N₂ at depth dissolves more N₂ in blood and (slowly, over hours) lipid-rich tissues. High P_N₂ reduces membrane ion conductance and neuronal excitability like a gas anesthetic. At ~4–5 atm it produces an alcohol-like intoxication — "Martini's law": each ~15 m ≈ one extra martini. Progresses to lethargy, fatigue, loss of consciousness.
- **O₂ toxicity:** by Henry's law the *dissolved* O₂ rises linearly with P_O₂ even though Hb-bound O₂ barely increases (Fig. 61-4). Pulmonary toxicity (prolonged high P_O₂): epithelial/smooth-muscle inflammation, pulmonary edema, atelectasis, fibrin, consolidation — from inactivated repair enzymes and oxidation. CNS toxicity: ambient P_O₂ ≈ 1500 mm Hg (room air at ~10 atm) for 30–45 min → seizures, coma; early signs are muscle twitching, nausea, disorientation, irritability. Mechanism is **free-radical** (superoxide, peroxide) oxidation of membrane lipids and metabolic enzymes; superoxide dismutase scavenges the few radicals formed at normal sea-level P_O₂.
- **Saturation diving and helium:** at equilibrium ("saturation") the dissolved N₂ volume is proportional to alveolar P_N₂ (Henry's law) — the body holds ~1 L N₂ at 1 atm and ~4 L at 4 atm, equilibrating slowly in underperfused fat. Helium replaces N₂ because it (1) narcotizes far less, (2) dissolves less in tissue, (3) is less dense (lowers airway resistance, but raises convective heat loss → warmer chambers needed), (4) diffuses out faster on decompression. F_IO₂ is also reduced (e.g. 2% O₂ in He at 10 atm matches sea-level inspired P_O₂) to avoid O₂ toxicity.
- **Decompression illness (DCI):** too-rapid ascent drops PB so dissolved N₂ leaves solution and forms bubbles (exactly like opening a carbonated bottle — Henry's law in reverse). Two disorders:
  - **Decompression sickness (DCS):** local bubbles in tissue/venous blood. Type I (mild) — "niggles," pruritus, rash, deep throbbing joint/muscle pain (**the bends**). Type II (serious) — CNS (spinal cord; "the staggers" → paralysis from myelin-sheath bubbles), pulmonary ("the chokes" from venous gas emboli lodging in the lung), and circulatory (obstruction + coagulation cascade + hypovolemic shock).
  - **Arterial gas embolization (AGE):** bubbles entering systemic arteries via alveolar tears or a right-to-left shunt (e.g. patent foramen ovale), lodging in brain/organs — catastrophic without immediate recompression.
- **Prevention/treatment:** ascend slowly (no faster than ~3 m/hr after a saturation dive) so dissolved N₂ moves tissue → blood → alveoli down its gradient; N₂ leaves water fast but fat slowly, giving fast and slow compartments. Divers use **decompression tables** keyed to depth and duration (Fig. 61-5). Best treatment is **recompression** in a hyperbaric chamber, redissolving the gas, then a supervised slow decompression.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Because liquid water is virtually incompressible, PB increases linearly with the height (weight) of the column of water." (p. 1227)
- > "According to Boyle's law, pressure and volume vary inversely with each other." (p. 1227)
- > "According to Henry's law (see Box 26-2), the increased P_N₂ will cause more N₂ to dissolve in pulmonary-capillary blood and, eventually, the body's tissues." (p. 1228)
- > "Diving to increased depths (e.g., 4 to 5 atm) while breathing compressed air causes nitrogen narcosis." (p. 1228)
- > "Too rapid an ascent causes the N₂ in the tissues—previously dissolved under high pressure—to leave solution and form bubbles as PB falls. This process is identical to the formation of gas bubbles when one opens a bottle of a carbonated beverage that had been capped under high pressure." (p. 1229)
- > "The best treatment for DCI is to recompress the diver in a hyperbaric chamber." (p. 1230)
- > "At equilibrium—as required by Henry's law—the volume of N₂ dissolved in the tissues is proportional to alveolar P_N₂." (p. 1229)

### Equations

- **Boyle's law** (gas-space compression with depth):

  $$P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad V \propto \frac{1}{P_B}$$

- **Henry's law** (dissolved gas content of blood/tissue):

  $$[\text{gas}]_{dissolved} = s \cdot P_{gas}$$

  with $s$ the gas-specific solubility coefficient. Governs N₂ loading at depth, O₂ dissolving linearly with P_O₂, and bubble formation on ascent.

- **PB with seawater depth:**

  $$P_B(\text{atm}) = 1 + \frac{\text{depth (m)}}{10}$$

Plot of **O₂ partial pressure in arterial blood (mm Hg, x-axis, 0–2800)**, with parallel secondary axes for **PB (atm, up to ~25)** and **depth (m, up to ~240)**, vs. **O₂ content of blood (mL/dL, y-axis, 0–40)**. ## Section 3 — High-altitude physiology / hypobaria (pp. 1230–1232)

### Subsection headings
- **PB and ambient P_O₂ on top of Mount Everest are approximately one third of their values at sea level** (pp. 1230–1231)
- **Up to modest altitudes, arterial O₂ content falls relatively less than PB due to the shape of the Hb-O₂ dissociation curve** (p. 1231)
- **During the first few days at altitude, compensatory adjustments to hypoxemia include tachycardia and hyperventilation** (p. 1231)
- **Long-term adaptations to altitude include increases in hematocrit, pulmonary diffusing capacity, capillarity, and oxidative enzymes** (pp. 1231–1232)
- **High altitude causes mild symptoms in most people and acute or chronic mountain sickness in susceptible individuals** (p. 1232)

### Core claims
- **Atmosphere is compressible** → density and PB fall *exponentially*, halving every ~5500 m. Half the atmosphere's mass lies below 5500 m. (Contrast: the water column is linear.)
- **Inspired PO₂ at the alveolus** must subtract water vapor (P_H₂O = 47 mm Hg at 37 °C), which becomes a *larger fraction* of PB as PB falls. At the Everest summit: inspired P_O₂ = 0.21 × (255 − 47) ≈ **44 mm Hg**, vs. ~149 mm Hg at sea level. Hypoxia is the dominant stress.
- **Air travel:** pressurized cabins hold ~1800 m equivalent (~79% sea-level pressure, cross-continental) to ~2400 m (~74%, transoceanic); most passengers tolerate the mild desaturation (~89% at 3000 m), but COPD patients may need supplemental O₂ aloft.
- **Hb-curve protection at modest altitude:** up to ~3000 m, arterial P_O₂ (60–70 mm Hg) sits on the *flat* upper part of the Hb-O₂ curve, so O₂ saturation/content fall little. At higher altitudes P_O₂ drops onto the *steep* part and content falls sharply. A further penalty: at extreme altitude (especially in exercise) pulmonary-capillary blood may fail to reach diffusion equilibrium with alveolar gas, lowering arterial P_O₂ below even the reduced alveolar value.
- **Acute response (first days):** low arterial P_O₂ stimulates **peripheral chemoreceptors** → immediate **hyperventilation**, which (a) raises alveolar/arterial P_O₂ toward ambient and (b) blows off CO₂ → **respiratory alkalosis**, which then inhibits the chemoreceptors and blunts the very drive that produced it (so V̇ at 4500 m only ~2× sea level acutely). Sympathetic drive causes **tachycardia** and ↑cardiac output to enhance O₂ delivery.
- **Acclimatization (days–weeks):** ventilation rises further as (1) CSF pH falls, relieving central-chemoreceptor inhibition (and renal compensation lowers plasma pH), and (2) peripheral chemoreceptor hypoxic sensitivity rises. The **kidneys** compensate for the respiratory alkalosis by **decreasing acid secretion**, so plasma pH returns toward normal; unreabsorbed HCO₃⁻ causes an osmotic diuresis and alkaline urine. (At the Everest summit, alveolar P_CO₂ measured ~7–8 mm Hg, implying ≥5-fold hyperventilation.)
- **Long-term adaptations** (many mediated by **HIF-1**, which activates erythropoiesis/angiogenesis genes):
  - **Polycythemia** — renal hypoxia (+ norepinephrine) → **erythropoietin (EPO)** from renal fibroblast-like cells → ↑RBC mass; Hb rises from 14–15 to >18 g/dL, hematocrit from 40–45% to >55%. (Also: ↑2,3-bisphosphoglycerate right-shifts the Hb curve to favor tissue unloading.)
  - **Pulmonary diffusing capacity** 2–3× — ↑pulmonary-capillary blood volume and surface area, deeper inspiration, and right-ventricular hypertrophy raising PA pressure to perfuse the apices (cf. Fig. 31-9).
  - **Capillary density** — hypoxia-driven angiogenesis within days (VEGF, FGF, angiogenin).
  - **Oxidative enzymes** — ↑mitochondrial oxidative enzyme expression, improving tissue O₂ extraction.
- **Mountain sickness spectrum:**
  - Mild symptoms (drowsiness, fatigue, headache, nausea, declining cognition) in some by 2100 m, most above 3500 m — from inadequate hyperventilation and, later, mild **cerebral edema** (cerebral arteriolar dilation → ↑capillary filtration → transudation).
  - **Acute mountain sickness (AMS):** rapid ascent to 3000–3500 m → headache, fatigue, dizziness, dyspnea, sleep disturbance, peripheral edema, nausea, vomiting; onset within day 1, lasting 3–5 days. Two causes: progressive **cerebral edema (HACE)** and **pulmonary edema (HAPE)** — hypoxia → **hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** → ↑pulmonary vascular resistance, ↑capillary pressure, transudation. Both can be fatal; treat with supplemental O₂ and descent.
  - **Susceptibility** is mainly a constitutional (not fitness) difference: those who hyperventilate more keep P_O₂ higher and P_CO₂ lower → less cerebral vasodilation and less pulmonary vasoconstriction, and are protected.
  - **Chronic mountain sickness:** after prolonged residence, exaggerated EPO response → **polycythemia** (hematocrit >60%) → ↑viscosity and vascular resistance, thrombosis risk; combined with hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, this overloads the right heart → right-ventricular congestive heart failure.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "PB falls by half for each ~5500 m of ascent." (p. 1230)
- > "Therefore, inspired P_O₂ = 21% × (255 − 47) = 44 mm Hg, compared with 149 mm Hg at sea level." (p. 1231)
- > "The reason is that at this altitude, arterial P_O₂ is 60 to 70 mm Hg, which corresponds to the relatively flat portion of the O₂-Hb dissociation curve … so that arterial O₂ content is little affected." (p. 1231)
- > "Hyperventilation blows off CO₂, producing a respiratory alkalosis that inhibits the peripheral but especially the central chemoreceptors and thereby decreases ventilatory drive." (p. 1231)
- > "The kidneys respond over a period of several days to the respiratory alkalosis by decreasing their rate of acid secretion … so that blood pH decreases toward normal (i.e., metabolic compensation for respiratory alkalosis)." (p. 1231)
- > "Many of these adaptations are mediated by an increase in hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1), a transcription factor that activates genes involved in erythropoiesis, angiogenesis, and other processes." (p. 1231)
- > "The second cause of the symptoms is pulmonary edema, which occurs as hypoxia leads to hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction … which in turn increases total pulmonary vascular resistance, pulmonary-capillary pressure, and transudation." (p. 1232)

### Equations

- **Alveolar inspired PO₂ at altitude** (humidified air, before gas exchange):

  $$P_{IO_2} = F_{IO_2}\,(P_B - P_{H_2O}) = 0.21\,(P_B - 47\ \text{mm Hg})$$

  At Everest summit ($P_B = 255$): $P_{IO_2} \approx 0.21(255-47) \approx 44$ mm Hg.

- **Alveolar gas equation at altitude** (from Ch 30, reused here to obtain alveolar PO₂):

  $$P_{AO_2} = F_{IO_2}\,(P_B - P_{H_2O}) - \frac{P_{ACO_2}}{R}$$

  with $R \approx 0.8$ at sea level; at altitude, hyperventilation drives $P_{ACO_2}$ very low (≈7–8 mm Hg at the Everest summit), partly defending $P_{AO_2}$.

- **Exponential fall of PB with altitude** (Fig. 61-6):

  $$P_B(z) \approx P_{B,0}\cdot 2^{-z/5500\ \text{m}}$$

## Section 4 — Flight and space physiology (pp. 1232–1234)

### Subsection headings
- **Acceleration in one direction shifts the blood volume in the opposite direction** (pp. 1232–1233)
- **Sustained microgravity causes a cephalad shift of fluid and reflex fluid loss** (p. 1233)
- **Space flight leads to motion sickness and to decreases in muscle and bone mass** (pp. 1233–1234)

### Core claims
- **Launch G forces** (Fig. 61-7): a shuttle ascent ramps to ~+3G during the ~2-min solid-rocket burn, drops to +1G, then builds to ~+4G before main-engine cutoff. Astronauts sit with backs perpendicular to the thrust so the G force acts front-to-back across the chest, sparing the cephalad-caudal axis.
- **G force shifts blood away from the direction of acceleration.** In aircraft, head-to-foot acceleration (>8G in high-performance flight) pools blood away from the head → ↓cerebral blood flow → **gray-out** (loss of peripheral vision and color as the retina is deprived of O₂) → **blackout** (loss of consciousness lasting tens of seconds). **G suits** apply lower-extremity counterpressure to oppose pooling and maintain cardiac filling and cerebral flow.
- **Microgravity cephalad fluid shift:** with gravity removed, blood redistributes from the leg capacitance vessels toward the head, expanding central blood volume, raising preload, and increasing facial transudation (the "bloated face" of astronauts within 24 h). An expected CVP rise is hard to confirm in space; ground head-down-tilt models do show ↑CVP with reflex responses — ↑atrial stretch → **ANP** release, and low-pressure baroreceptor stimulation → ↓**arginine vasopressin (ADH)** — both increasing renal salt/water excretion, explaining the relative under-hydration of astronauts. Mean arterial pressure and cardiac output end up not significantly different from earth values.
- **Motion sickness:** >half of astronauts get nausea/vomiting in early microgravity from conflicting sensory input about body position.
- **Bone and muscle loss:** disuse plus microgravity demineralize bone and waste muscle (cf. the bed-rest data in Section 1).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "G forces tend to shift the blood volume away from the direction of acceleration, thereby adding to the other component forces that determine blood pressure." (p. 1233)
- > "The early warnings of such an event are narrowing of the visual field (i.e., loss of peripheral vision) and loss of color perception as the retina is deprived of O₂, a phenomenon called gray-out." (p. 1233)
- > "This cephalad shift of blood volume—away from the capacitance vessels of the legs—expands the central blood volume, increasing the cardiac preload." (p. 1233)
- > "These two events increase excretion of salt and water by the kidneys, which tends to correct the perceived volume overload and explains the tendency for astronauts to remain relatively underhydrated during space flight." (p. 1233)

### Table B — Gas laws governing environmental physiology

## Glossary

- **Milieu extérieur / milieu intérieur** — the external environment in layers vs. Bernard's internal extracellular fluid.
- **PB (barometric pressure)** — 760 mm Hg at sea level; +1 atm per 10 m seawater; halves per ~5500 m altitude.
- **Dalton's / Boyle's / Henry's laws** — partial-pressure additivity; inverse P–V; dissolved gas ∝ partial pressure.
- **Barotrauma** — pressure-induced damage to non-equilibrating air cavities (middle ear, sinuses).
- **Breath-hold diving / deep-water blackout** — ascent-phase fall in P_aO₂ and P_aCO₂ → cerebral hypoxia, lost drive.
- **Nitrogen narcosis** — anesthetic-like effect of high dissolved N₂ ("Martini's law").
- **O₂ toxicity** — free-radical pulmonary and CNS injury at high dissolved P_O₂.
- **Saturation diving / helium mix** — tissue gas equilibration; He replaces N₂ and dilutes O₂.
- **Decompression illness (DCI)** — DCS (the bends, staggers, chokes) + arterial gas embolization (AGE); from N₂ bubbles on ascent.
- **Recompression / decompression tables** — hyperbaric treatment and slow-ascent prevention.
- **Hypobaric hypoxia** — reduced inspired P_O₂ at altitude.
- **Respiratory alkalosis of altitude / renal compensation** — hyperventilation blows off CO₂; kidney lowers acid secretion.
- **Acclimatization** — EPO/polycythemia, ↑2,3-BPG, ↑diffusing capacity, ↑capillarity, ↑oxidative enzymes; HIF-1–driven.
- **Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV)** — alveolar-hypoxia–driven; basis of HAPE.
- **AMS / HACE / HAPE** — acute mountain sickness; high-altitude cerebral edema; high-altitude pulmonary edema.
- **Chronic mountain sickness** — excess polycythemia → ↑viscosity → right-heart failure.
- **G force** — experienced acceleration relative to gravity; gray-out/blackout; G suits.
- **Cephalad fluid shift** — microgravity blood redistribution → ANP↑, ADH↓, renal fluid loss.
