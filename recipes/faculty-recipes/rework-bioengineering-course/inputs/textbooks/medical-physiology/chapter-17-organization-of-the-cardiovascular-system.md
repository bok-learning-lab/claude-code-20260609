# Chapter 17 — Organization of the Cardiovascular System

> Section IV · The Cardiovascular System · pp. 410–428 · Author: Emile L. Boulpaep
> **First chapter of a new section (Section IV).** This chapter establishes the hemodynamic vocabulary — pressure, flow, resistance, compliance, viscosity, turbulence, kinetic vs. potential energy — that the rest of Section IV (Chs 18–25) elaborates and that Section V (respiration) and Section VI (renal) inherit.

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Elements of the cardiovascular system** (pp. 410–412) — why a circulation is needed; the heart as a dual pump driving two serial circuits (systemic and pulmonary).
2. **Hemodynamics** (pp. 412–415) — Ohm's law for liquids; series and parallel resistances; pressure as a *difference*; driving, transmural, and hydrostatic pressures; cardiac output = HR · SV.
3. **Poiseuille's law and viscosity** (pp. 415–417) — flow ∝ $r^4$, $\Delta P$, $1/\eta$, $1/L$; viscosity as shear stress / shear rate.
4. **How blood flows** (pp. 417–418) — laminar vs. turbulent flow; Reynolds number; pulsatile pressure between systolic and diastolic values; mean arterial pressure.
5. **Origins of pressure in the circulation** (pp. 418–420) — gravity (hydrostatic), compliance, viscous resistance, inertia / Bernoulli.
6. **How to measure blood pressure, blood flow, and cardiac volumes** (pp. 420–428) — direct (catheter + transducer) and indirect (sphygmomanometry) pressure; electromagnetic and ultrasound flowmetry; Fick principle; dye and thermodilution; clearance methods (BSP, PAH, ¹³³Xe, N₂O, ²⁰¹Tl); angiography, M-mode, 2-D, and Doppler echocardiography.

## Section 1 — Elements of the cardiovascular system (pp. 410–412)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The circulation is an evolutionary consequence of body size** (p. 410)
- **The heart is a dual pump that drives the blood in two serial circuits: the systemic and the pulmonary circulations** (pp. 410–412)

### Core claims
- Single cells and small organisms meet metabolic needs by **diffusion alone** (see p. 108 — Fick's law from Ch 5). For large multicellular bodies the diffusion distance becomes prohibitive, so a **convective transport system** is required to maintain steep gradients between epithelial exchange surfaces (lung, gut, kidney) and central cells.
- **Three functional organs** make up the circulation: a **pump** (heart) circulating a **liquid organ** (blood) through a system of **containers** (vessels).
- **Primary role:** distribution of dissolved gases and other molecules for nutrition, growth, and repair. **Secondary roles:** (1) fast chemical signaling via circulating hormones and neurotransmitters, (2) heat dissipation from core to surface, (3) mediation of inflammatory and host-defense responses.
- The mammalian/avian solution is a **dual circulation in series** (Fig. 17-1D):
  - **Left heart** = main pump → **systemic circulation** (high pressure: aorta to systemic capillaries).
  - **Right heart** = boost pump → **pulmonary circulation** (low pressure).
  - The two pumps' outputs must be **delicately equalized** (continuity of flow), since blood does not accumulate in either circuit at steady state.
- Mass and energy budget: the heart weighs ~300 g, delivers ~5 L/min at rest (can rise 5-fold with exercise), and pumps ~400 million L of blood over a 75-year lifespan.
- The system is **self-repairing and self-expanding**: endothelial cells mend damaged vessels and generate new ones (**angiogenesis**, see Ch 20).
- The clinical taxonomy of cardiovascular failure follows from the three-organ schema:
  - **Pump failure** → congestive heart failure (Chs 22, 25).
  - **Blood failure** → thrombosis, embolism (Ch 18).
  - **Vessel failure** → hemorrhage (incompetent container) or atherosclerosis (inefficient distribution; Ch 19).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The circulation is an evolutionary consequence of body size." (p. 410)
- > "The heart really consists of two pumps, the left heart, or main pump, and the right heart, or boost pump." (p. 410)
- > "The primary role of the circulatory system is the distribution of dissolved gases and other molecules for nutrition, growth, and repair." (p. 410)
- > "We could also divide the vascular system into a high-pressure part (extending from the contracting left ventricle to the systemic capillaries) and a low-pressure part." (p. 410)
- > "Endothelial cells lining vessels mend the surfaces of existing blood vessels and generate new vessels (angiogenesis)." (p. 412)
- > "Some of the most important life-threatening human diseases are caused by failure of the heart as a pump … failure of the blood … or failure of the vasculature." (p. 412)

## Section 2 — Hemodynamics (pp. 412–415)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Blood flow is driven by a constant pressure head across variable resistances** (pp. 412–413)
- **Blood pressure is always measured as a pressure difference between two points** (pp. 413–414)
- **Total blood flow, or cardiac output, is the product (heart rate) × (stroke volume)** (pp. 414–415)

### Core claims
- To first approximation, the left ventricle behaves as a **constant pressure-head generator**, not a constant flow generator: the aorta–vena cava $\Delta P$ stays fairly stable over time, while flow and resistance fluctuate.
- **Ohm's law of hydrodynamics** applies at every instant, regardless of circuit complexity or vessel compliance, *provided $R$ is constant in time*.
- **Pathways** from left heart to right heart (Fig. 17-3) include: (i) a single capillary bed (e.g., coronary, skeletal muscle); (ii) **two capillary beds in series** (renal glomerular → peritubular; hepatic portal: splenic + mesenteric → hepatic sinusoid); (iii) parallel beds merging into a series bed. By contrast, right heart → left heart is **one pathway: the pulmonary capillary bed**.
- **Three kinds of pressure differences** in a blood vessel (Fig. 17-4), each with its own spatial axis:
  - **Driving (axial) pressure** $\Delta P_{\text{axial}}$ — along the long axis of the vessel; **governs flow** ($F = \Delta P / R$).
  - **Transmural pressure** $\Delta P_{\text{radial}}$ — across the wall; difference between intravascular and tissue pressure; **governs vessel diameter** (and through diameter, resistance and capacitance).
  - **Hydrostatic pressure** $\Delta P_{\text{hydrostatic}}$ — along the gravitational axis; exists whether or not blood is flowing.
- The reference pressure in cardiovascular physiology is **atmospheric (barometric) pressure** at **the level of the heart**.
- **Continuity of flow** (mass conservation): right and left cardiac outputs must be equal at steady state.

### Equations

- **Ohm's law of hydrodynamics** (Eq. 17-1):

$$\Delta P = F \cdot R$$

- **Series resistance addition** (Eq. 17-2):

$$R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \cdots$$

- **Parallel resistance addition** (Eq. 17-3):

$$\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \cdots$$

- **Pressure as a liquid column** (Eq. 17-4):

$$P = \rho \, g \, h$$

  with $\rho$ the liquid density, $g$ gravitational acceleration, $h$ column height. Physiologists report pressures in mm Hg or cm H₂O.

- **Pressure as force per area** (Eq. 17-5):

$$P = F / A$$

- **Continuity** ($F$ = product of cross-sectional area and linear velocity, Eq. 17-8):

$$F = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta t} = A \cdot v$$

  holds at every point in the circulation regardless of geometry.

- **Cardiac output** (Eq. 17-6):

$$\text{CO} = F = \text{HR} \times \text{SV}$$

  Resting adult CO ≈ 5 L/min in a 70-kg human; **cardiac index** (CO normalized to body surface area) ≈ 3.0 L·min⁻¹·m⁻². CO can rise 5-fold during exercise.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The pressure difference between an upstream point and a downstream site is equal to the product of the flow and the resistance between those two points." (p. 412)
- > "The heart behaves more like a generator of a constant pressure head than like a generator of constant flow, at least within physiological limits." (p. 412)
- > "Pressure is never expressed in absolute terms but as a pressure difference $\Delta P$ relative to some 'reference' pressure." (p. 413)
- > "Driving pressure, transmural pressure, and hydrostatic pressure are three different kinds of pressure differences in the circulation." (p. 414, paraphrase of section heading sequence)
- > "The flow of the right and left hearts (i.e., right and left cardiac outputs) must be equal in the steady state." (p. 414)
- > "Cardiac output depends on body size and is best normalized to body surface area." (p. 414)

## Section 3 — Poiseuille's law and viscosity (pp. 415–417)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Flow in an idealized vessel increases with the fourth power of radius (Poiseuille equation)** (pp. 415–416)
- **Viscous resistance to flow is proportional to the viscosity of blood but does not depend on properties of the blood vessel walls** (p. 415)
- **The viscosity of blood is a measure of the internal slipperiness between layers of fluid** (pp. 415–416)

### Core claims
- The **Hagen–Poiseuille equation** describes the steady, laminar flow of a newtonian fluid through a straight, rigid, cylindrical tube. Three implications:
  1. $F \propto \Delta P$ (proportionality constant = $1/R$).
  2. $F \propto r^4$ — small changes in radius cause **enormous** changes in flow. (Arterioles are the principal site of resistance control in the systemic circulation for exactly this reason; see Chs 19, 23.)
  3. $F \propto 1/L$ and $F \propto 1/\eta$.
- **Viscosity** ($\eta$) is an intensive property of the fluid alone, independent of vessel-wall properties. The unit is the **poise (P)**. Whole blood ≈ **3 centipoise (cP)**; plasma ≈ 1.8 cP; water ≈ 1 cP.
- In a cylindrical vessel, the no-slip condition at the wall plus Newton's definition of viscosity give a **parabolic velocity profile**: $v = 0$ at the wall, $v = v_{\max}$ on the axis. The lower the viscosity, the **sharper the bullet** of the profile.
- The Poiseuille equation requires **six assumptions** (see below). Blood approximately satisfies the first three; conditions 4 (laminar) and 5 (steady) are addressed by the next two sections; assumption 6 (newtonian viscosity) is violated in small vessels and discussed in Ch 18 ("anomalous viscosity," Fåhraeus–Lindqvist effect, plasma skimming).
- **Peripheral resistance units (PRU):** mm Hg per (mL/s). Total peripheral resistance (TPR) is the canonical clinical use.

### Equations

- **Hagen–Poiseuille law** (Eq. 17-9):

$$F = \frac{\pi r^4 \, \Delta P}{8 \, \eta \, L}$$

- **Resistance from Ohm's law** (Eq. 17-10):

$$R = \frac{\Delta P}{F}$$

- **Viscous resistance** of a Poiseuille tube (Eq. 17-11), obtained by combining Eqs 17-9 and 17-10:

$$R = \frac{8 \, \eta \, L}{\pi \, r^4}$$

- **Newton's definition of viscosity** (Eq. 17-12):

$$\eta = \frac{\text{shear stress}}{\text{shear rate}} = \frac{F/A}{\Delta v / \Delta x}$$

  Units: dyne·s/cm² = poise.

### Six assumptions behind Poiseuille's law (verbatim from p. 416)

1. Fluid is incompressible.
2. Tube is straight, rigid, cylindrical, unbranched, of constant radius.
3. Velocity of the thin fluid layer at the wall is zero (no slippage).
4. Flow is laminar (concentric undisturbed shells; no gross radial exchange).
5. Flow is steady (not pulsatile).
6. Viscosity is constant — both spatially across the section and in the newtonian sense (independent of shear stress and shear rate).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Flow is directly proportional to the fourth power of vessel radius." (p. 415)
- > "Unlike Ohm's law of hydrodynamics, which applies to all vessels, no matter how complicated, the Poiseuille equation applies only to rigid, cylindrical tubes." (p. 415)
- > "Viscous resistance is proportional to the viscosity of the fluid and the length of the tube but inversely proportional to the fourth power of the radius of the blood vessel." (p. 415)
- > "Viscosity expresses the degree of lack of slipperiness between two layers of fluid." (p. 415)
- > "Whole blood has a viscosity of ~3 centipoise (cP)." (p. 415)

### Figures

#### Figure 17-5 — Viscosity *(viewed)*

**Panel A — Definition of viscosity (Newton's flat-plate construction).** Two parallel rectangular fluid sheets of area $A$ are drawn one above the other. A hand applies a force $F$ to the lower sheet, sliding it past the stationary upper sheet at velocity $v$ relative to a faster-moving second sheet at velocity $v + \Delta v$. The vertical separation between the sheets is $\Delta x$; the velocity gradient $\Delta v / \Delta x$ across the gap is the **shear rate**; the force per unit area $F/A$ is the **shear stress**; their ratio is the viscosity $\eta$. **Panel B — Viscous flow in a cylinder.** A horizontal blood vessel with pressure head $\Delta P$ on the left. Each layer has its own arrow whose length encodes its axial velocity: the wall layer (#0) has zero velocity ("At the vessel wall, velocity is zero"), velocities rise concentrically, and the central axis carries the maximum velocity $v_{\max}$ ("Along the central axis, velocity is maximal"). The envelope of arrow tips traces a **parabolic velocity profile** — the geometric content of Poiseuille's law.

## Section 4 — How blood flows: turbulence and pulsatility (pp. 417–418)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Blood flow is laminar** (p. 417)
- **Pressure and flow oscillate with each heartbeat between maximum systolic and minimum diastolic values** (p. 418)

### Core claims
- **Laminar vs. turbulent** flow is determined by the dimensionless **Reynolds number** Re. Blood flow is **laminar** when $\text{Re} \lesssim 2000$ and **turbulent** when $\text{Re} \gtrsim 3000$. In the turbulent regime the parabolic velocity profile is **blunted** and $F$ scales like $\sqrt{\Delta P}$ rather than $\Delta P$ (effective resistance rises).
- **Predictors of turbulence:**
  - High radius $r$ (e.g., aorta).
  - High velocity $v$ (e.g., high cardiac output, exercise, stenosis-induced jet).
  - Low viscosity $\eta$ (e.g., anemia — low hematocrit).
- **Clinical correlate:** laminar flow is **silent**; turbulence produces **Korotkoff sounds** (basis of auscultatory sphygmomanometry), **cardiac murmurs** across diseased valves, and **arterial bruits** across stenotic vessels. Intense turbulence is palpable as **thrills**.
- **Pulsatility.** Both pressure and flow oscillate with each heartbeat (Fig. 17-7). Definitions:
  - **Systolic arterial pressure (SAP)** ≈ 120 mm Hg in a healthy adult; corresponds to ventricular contraction.
  - **Diastolic arterial pressure (DAP)** ≈ 80 mm Hg; corresponds to ventricular relaxation.
  - **Pulse pressure** = SAP − DAP ≈ 40 mm Hg.
  - **Mean arterial pressure (MAP)** ≈ 95 mm Hg. *Not* the arithmetic mean of SAP and DAP; it is the time average (area under the $P(t)$ curve divided by cycle length). A common diastole-weighted clinical approximation: $\text{MAP} \approx \text{DAP} + \tfrac{1}{3}(\text{SAP} - \text{DAP})$.
- Because pressure and flow are pulsatile and phase-shifted, the steady-state DC Ohm-law description must be augmented by an **AC-circuit analogy** with impedance components (Note N17-5).

### Equations

- **Reynolds number** (Eq. 17-13):

$$\text{Re} = \frac{2 \, r \, v \, \rho}{\eta}$$

  Dimensionless. Numerator = inertial (disruptive) forces; denominator = viscous (cohesive) forces.

- **Pulse pressure**:

$$\text{PP} = \text{SAP} - \text{DAP}$$

- **Mean arterial pressure** (definition):

$$\text{MAP} = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T P(t) \, dt$$

  with $T$ = one cardiac cycle. Clinical approximation:

$$\text{MAP} \approx \text{DAP} + \tfrac{1}{3} \, \text{PP}$$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Blood flow is laminar when Re is below ~2000 and is mostly turbulent when Re exceeds ~3000." (p. 417)
- > "Turbulent blood flow occurs when r is large … or when v is large … Turbulent flow can also occur when a local decrease in vessel diameter causes a local increase in v." (p. 417)
- > "Laminar flow is silent, whereas vortex formation during turbulence sets up murmurs." (p. 417)
- > "The difference between the systolic pressure and the diastolic pressure is the pulse pressure." (p. 418)
- > "The mean arterial pressure is not the arithmetic mean of systolic and diastolic values … rather, it is the area beneath the curve … divided by the duration of the cycle." (p. 418)

### Clinical box — Heart Murmurs and Arterial Bruits (Box 17-1, p. 417)
- **Cardiac murmurs** arise from turbulence across diseased valves (stenosis or regurgitation). Their grade (1–6) and timing (systolic vs. diastolic) localize the lesion. The Reynolds-number framework predicts when murmurs appear: anything that raises $r$, raises $v$, or lowers $\eta$ (anemia, high-output states, exercise, thin chest wall) makes turbulence audible.
- **Arterial bruits** are murmurs heard over diseased peripheral arteries (typically atherosclerotic — carotid, femoral). They reflect post-stenotic jet turbulence.

## Section 5 — Origins of pressure in the circulation (pp. 418–420)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Gravity causes a hydrostatic pressure difference when there is a difference in height** (pp. 418–419)
- **Low compliance of a vessel causes the transmural pressure to increase when the vessel blood volume is increased** (p. 419)
- **The viscous resistance of blood causes an axial pressure difference when there is flow** (pp. 419–420)
- **The inertia of the blood and vessels causes pressure to decrease when the velocity of blood flow increases** (p. 420)

### Core claims
**Four origins of pressure** in the circulation: **gravity, compliance, viscous resistance, inertia.**

- **Gravity (hydrostatic).** A column of blood of height $\Delta h$ produces $\Delta P_{\text{hydrostatic}} = -\rho g \Delta h$. The reference height for cardiovascular pressures is the **level of the heart**.
  - Worked example (180-cm-tall adult, Fig. 17-8):
    - **Recumbent (horizontal):** Mean aortic ≈ 95 mm Hg; mean large arteries to head and foot ≈ 90 mm Hg; mean large veins from head and foot ≈ 5 mm Hg; right-atrial ≈ 2 mm Hg. Driving pressure $\Delta P$ across every regional bed ≈ 85 mm Hg.
    - **Upright (erect):** A 130-cm column of blood from heart to foot adds ≈ 95 mm Hg → foot artery ≈ 185 mm Hg; foot vein ≈ 100 mm Hg. A 50-cm column from heart to head subtracts ≈ 37 mm Hg → head artery ≈ 53 mm Hg; head vein ≈ −32 mm Hg (i.e., 32 mm Hg below atmospheric). **Driving $\Delta P$ across every bed is still 85 mm Hg** — gravity does not change driving pressure, only the absolute level.
  - **Venous valves** (one-way, in the limbs) plus skeletal-muscle pumping (the "muscle-vein pump") prevent the full 95 mm Hg column from being expressed inside dependent veins at rest (see Ch 19).
- **Compliance.** Vessels are distensible (not rigid). Compliance is defined as:

$$C = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta P}$$

  - **Zero compliance** (rigid tube, Fig. 17-9A): $\Delta V \to 0$ for any finite $\Delta P$; injecting volume drives pressure to infinity.
  - **Infinite compliance** (no wall resistance, Fig. 17-9B): $\Delta P = 0$ for any $\Delta V$.
  - **Finite compliance** (real vessels, Fig. 17-9C): injecting $\Delta V$ raises pressure by $\Delta V / C$.
  - The **arterial tree** has low compliance and high pressure (≈ 1.5 mL/mm Hg total for the systemic arteries); the **venous system** has high compliance and low pressure (≈ 30–50 mL/mm Hg). Veins therefore hold ~65–70% of total blood volume — the **capacitance reservoir** of the circulation (developed in Chs 19, 23, 25).
  - Compliance is a **static** property of the vessel wall and applies even when there is no flow (e.g., during cardiac arrest the pressure distribution still depends on compliance — this is the basis for the mean systemic filling pressure construct of Ch 23).
- **Viscous resistance.** During steady flow, $\Delta P_{\text{axial}} = F \cdot R$ — this is the only one of the four pressure components that appears in the Poiseuille equation.
- **Inertia and the Bernoulli effect.** Fluids flow from high **total energy** (potential + kinetic), not strictly from high pressure:

$$E = P + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2$$

  - In a tube that **narrows** (e.g., a stenosis), continuity ($F = A v$) forces $v$ to rise where $A$ shrinks; if $v$ rises by a factor of 4 (cross-section narrowed by 4×), kinetic energy rises by 16×, and **pressure paradoxically falls** in the narrow region even though total energy is monotonically decreasing along the flow direction (Fig. 17-10 worked example: $P$ falls 100 → 60 → 80; total energy 102 → 92 → 82).
  - The Bernoulli construction is the physical basis for measuring **pulmonary artery wedge pressure** and explains why **catheter-tip pressure** depends on tip orientation: facing upstream over-reads by $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2$; facing downstream under-reads by the same amount; only a side-port reads the true static intravascular pressure (Fig. 17-11).

### Equations

- **Hydrostatic pressure difference** (Eq. 17-4 applied to a vertical column):

$$\Delta P_{\text{hydrostatic}} = -\rho \, g \, (h_2 - h_1)$$

- **Compliance**:

$$C = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta P}$$

  (Inverse: **elastance** $E = \Delta P / \Delta V$.) Both compliance and elastance reappear in Chs 19, 22, 27 (lung mechanics).

- **Bernoulli equation** (incompressible, inviscid, along a streamline):

$$P + \tfrac{1}{2} \rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}$$

- **Continuity-driven velocity change** at a stenosis:

$$A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \;\; \Rightarrow \;\; v_2 = v_1 \cdot \frac{A_1}{A_2}$$

  This is the geometric reason cardiac murmur loudness scales steeply with the degree of stenosis (because Re scales with $v$).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Four factors help generate pressure in the circulation: gravity, compliance of the vessels, viscous resistance, and inertia." (p. 418)
- > "Whether the body is recumbent or upright has a tremendous effect on the intravascular pressure." (p. 418)
- > "Although the absolute arterial and venous pressures are much higher in the foot than in the head, the $\Delta P$ that drives blood flow is the same in the vascular beds of the foot and head." (p. 419)
- > "If we were to infuse blood into a patient's blood vessels, the intravascular pressure would rise throughout the circulation, even if the heart were stopped." (p. 419)
- > "It is more accurate to say that fluids flow from a higher to a lower total energy. This energy is made up of both the pressure or potential energy and the kinetic energy." (p. 420)

## Section 6 — Measuring blood pressure, blood flow, and cardiac volumes (pp. 420–428)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Blood pressure can be measured directly by puncturing the vessel** (pp. 420–421)
- **Blood pressure can be measured indirectly by use of a sphygmomanometer** (pp. 421–422)
- **Blood flow can be measured directly by electromagnetic and ultrasound flowmeters** (pp. 422–423)
- **Cardiac output can be measured indirectly by the Fick method, which is based on the conservation of mass** (pp. 423–424)
- **Cardiac output can be measured indirectly by dilution methods** (pp. 425–426)
- **Regional blood flow can be measured indirectly by "clearance" methods** (pp. 426–427)
- **Ventricular dimensions, ventricular volumes, and volume changes can be measured by angiography and echocardiography** (pp. 427–428)

### Core claims — direct pressure measurement
- The modern setup (Fig. 17-12) is a **fluid-filled catheter** transmitting pressure to an external **strain-gauge transducer** bonded to a stiff diaphragm; output is amplified and recorded. The opposite face of the diaphragm is open to atmosphere — i.e., the reading is gauge pressure (relative to PB).
- **System frequency response** is degraded by air bubbles in the catheter, by length, and by narrow bore (these increase the dead-volume that must be displaced to transmit a pressure transient). High-fidelity recordings use **solid-state tip transducers**.
- **Right-heart catheterization** (Swan–Ganz): catheter introduced into an antecubital vein, advanced through the SVC, RA, RV, pulmonary valve, into a small pulmonary artery branch where its tip wedges and reads the **pulmonary wedge pressure** ≈ left atrial pressure (the static column between catheter tip and left atrium reveals downstream pressure through the pulmonary capillary bed). See p. 519.
- **Left-heart catheterization:** catheter introduced into brachial or femoral artery, advanced retrograde through the aorta to the LV and (across the mitral valve) the LA.
- **Capillary pressure** is measured in the research laboratory with a servo-nulled **micropipette** pressurized to the point where no fluid moves in or out (Wiedemann technique).

### Core claims — indirect pressure (sphygmomanometry)
- **Sphygmomanometer cuff** placed around the upper arm, inflated above expected SAP to occlude the brachial artery, then slowly deflated (Fig. 17-13).
- **Palpatory method** (radial pulse): pressure at which the pulse first returns = systolic (slightly underestimated).
- **Auscultatory method** (stethoscope over brachial artery): five **Korotkoff phases**:
  - **Phase I** — sharp tapping → SAP.
  - **Phase II** — blowing/swishing murmur.
  - **Phase III** — louder thumping.
  - **Phase IV** — muffling.
  - **Phase V** — silence.
  - The point of muffling (phase IV) is preferred for diastolic in most contemporary practice, though disappearance (phase V) is also used.
- **Cuff sizing.** AHA recommendation: bag width ≥ 20% larger than limb diameter, encircling at least halfway around the limb, centered over the artery. **Undersized cuffs over-read; oversized cuffs under-read.** Critical for obese, pediatric, or thigh measurements.

### Core claims — direct flow measurement (research)
- **Electromagnetic flowmeter** (Fig. 17-14): a magnetic field $B$ is applied across a vessel; moving blood (an electrolyte conductor) generates a transverse EMF perpendicular to both $B$ and $\vec{v}$ (Faraday induction):

$$E = B \cdot v \cdot D$$

  Eq. 17-14. ($D$ = vessel diameter.)
- **Ultrasound flowmeters** use either the **Doppler shift** (frequency change of reflected ultrasound proportional to RBC velocity) or **transit-time** differences between paired probes. Both measure **linear velocity**, from which flow is obtained as $F = A \cdot v$.
- **Plethysmography** (noninvasive, classical): occluding venous outflow with a cuff while leaving arterial inflow intact, then measuring the rate of limb-volume rise. Gives organ blood flow.
- **Transcutaneous Doppler ultrasound** of surface vessels is the most widely used noninvasive clinical method (carotid duplex, peripheral arterial study, fetal heart, etc.).

### Core claims — Fick principle for cardiac output
- The **Fick principle** is the conservation of mass applied to a marker substance $X$ that is added to (or removed from) the blood between two checkpoints. The rate of substance flow at a checkpoint is $\dot Q_X = F \cdot [X]$.
- Applied to the **lungs**, with O₂ as the marker:
  - The lungs **add** O₂ at the rate $\dot V_{O_2}$ (measured by O₂ consumption — e.g., closed-circuit spirometry).
  - Upstream checkpoint = **pulmonary artery** (mixed venous blood); $[O_2]_{\text{a, venous}} \approx 15 \text{ mL O}_2/\text{dL}$.
  - Downstream checkpoint = **pulmonary vein / systemic artery**; $[O_2]_{\text{b, arterial}} \approx 20 \text{ mL O}_2/\text{dL}$.
  - Cardiac output:

$$\text{CO} = \frac{\dot V_{O_2}}{[O_2]_{\text{arterial}} - [O_2]_{\text{mixed venous}}}$$

  - Worked example (Eq. 17-19): CO = (250 mL/min) / (20 − 15 mL/dL) = 5 L/min.
- The Fick method is the **gold standard** for cardiac output measurement but requires both an arterial sample and a mixed-venous sample (right-heart catheter).

### Core claims — dye / thermodilution
- **Stewart–Hamilton dye dilution** (Fig. 17-16): inject a known bolus $Q_X$ (typically **indocyanine green** = Cardiogreen, cleared by the liver so it doesn't accumulate) upstream, monitor downstream concentration $[X](t)$.

$$\text{CO} = \frac{Q_X}{\int_0^\infty [X](t) \, dt} = \frac{Q_X}{[\bar X] \cdot \Delta t} = \frac{Q_X}{\text{Area}}$$

  (Eq. 17-22.) Worked example: 5 mg dye, area = 2 mg/L × 0.5 min = 1 mg·min/L → CO = 5 L/min.
- **Practical pitfalls:** (1) **dispersion** flattens the downstream curve more if the bolus crosses more or longer paths; (2) **recirculation** contaminates the tail of the first wave (corrected by exponential extrapolation of the down-slope).
- **Thermodilution** (the standard Swan–Ganz technique): inject **cold saline** into the right atrium, measure temperature transient downstream with a pulmonary-artery thermistor. Advantages: (1) cold can be re-injected repeatedly without buildup; (2) single venous-only puncture; (3) less dispersion (no capillary bed crossed between injection and detection); (4) less recirculation (cold equilibrates in pulmonary and systemic capillary beds).

### Core claims — clearance methods for regional flow
- The same Fick principle applied to a single organ:

$$F_{\text{organ}} = \frac{\dot Q_X}{[X]_a - [X]_v}$$

  (Eq. 17-24.) Specific applications:
  - **BSP (bromsulphthalein) clearance** → hepatic blood flow (almost completely cleared into bile).
  - **PAH (para-aminohippurate) clearance** → renal blood flow (almost completely secreted into urine; see Ch 34).
  - **Inert-gas clearance (¹³³Xe, ⁸⁵Kr)** → tissue blood flow in coronary and skeletal muscle beds.
  - **N₂O uptake** (Kety–Schmidt method) → cerebral blood flow.
- **²⁰¹Tl scintigraphy** (Box 17-2) — a qualitative measure of regional **coronary** blood flow using a K⁺-analog that is taken up by metabolically active myocardium in proportion to perfusion; "cold spots" mark stenosis; widely used in exercise stress testing.

### Core claims — chamber imaging
- **Gated radionuclide ventriculography (MUGA, ⁹⁹ᵐTc-RBC):** ECG-gated camera images of the blood pool over many cycles; gives end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes (in arbitrary units), from which **ejection fraction** = (EDV − ESV) / EDV is computed. EF is the canonical clinical index of LV systolic function.
- **Contrast angiography:** intracavitary injection of an x-ray-opaque contrast and cine fluoroscopy. Provides a 2-D projection of LV outline frame-by-frame; can estimate absolute volumes assuming a geometric model (typically a **prolate ellipsoid** with axes $L$, $D_1$, $D_2$, often simplified to $D_1 = D_2$ and $D_1 = L/2$, Fig. 17-17B).
- **Echocardiography** is now the dominant noninvasive modality:
  - **M-mode** — a single fixed ultrasound beam transects RV wall, RV cavity, septum, LV cavity, mitral valve leaflets, posterior LV wall; the temporal trace shows wall and leaflet motion over the cardiac cycle in one dimension.
  - **Two-dimensional (B-mode)** — the probe sweeps rapidly through an arc, producing a real anatomical cross-section; superior to angiography for volumes.
  - **Doppler echocardiography** — overlays color-encoded velocity on the B-mode image. Flow toward the transducer is rendered above baseline (often red); flow away appears below baseline (often blue). The spectrum at each pixel encodes the distribution of RBC velocities; broadband signals indicate turbulence (regurgitant jets, stenotic jets).
  - **Color flow Doppler echocardiography** (Fig. 17-18) is the standard test for valvular regurgitation and intracardiac shunts.
- **Cardiac MR (magnetic resonance angiography, phase-contrast mapping)** provides quantitative, three-dimensional flow imaging without contrast or ionizing radiation; the temporal resolution is lower than for echocardiography.

### Equations (consolidated for Section 6)

- **Faraday EMF in an electromagnetic flowmeter** (Eq. 17-14):

$$E = B \cdot v \cdot D$$

- **Fick principle for substance flow at a checkpoint** (Eq. 17-15):

$$\dot Q_X = F \cdot [X]$$

- **Mass balance between two checkpoints** (Eq. 17-16):

$$\dot Q_{\text{added}/\text{subtracted}} = \dot Q_b - \dot Q_a$$

- **Fick equation for flow** (Eq. 17-17/17-18):

$$F = \frac{\dot Q_{\text{added}/\text{subtracted}}}{[X]_b - [X]_a}$$

- **Cardiac output by Fick (O₂ uptake)** (Eq. 17-19):

$$\text{CO} = \frac{\dot V_{O_2}}{[O_2]_{\text{art}} - [O_2]_{\text{mv}}}$$

- **Stewart–Hamilton dye dilution** (Eq. 17-22):

$$\text{CO} = \frac{Q_X}{\int [X](t) \, dt} = \frac{Q_X}{\text{Area under } [X](t)}$$

- **Ejection fraction:**

$$\text{EF} = \frac{\text{EDV} - \text{ESV}}{\text{EDV}} = \frac{\text{SV}}{\text{EDV}}$$

### Clinical box — Thallium scanning for assessment of coronary blood flow (Box 17-2, p. 426)
- ²⁰¹Tl is a K⁺ analog taken up by active cardiomyocytes via the same channels and transporters as K⁺ (chiefly the Na/K pump and inwardly rectifying K⁺ channels — see Chs 5, 7). Uptake is proportional to coronary perfusion.
- Imaged by 2-D gamma scintigraphy or SPECT; stenotic segments appear as **cold spots** ("perfusion defects").
- Sensitized by **exercise stress** (or pharmacologic stress with dipyridamole/adenosine, which dilates normal coronaries but unmasks stenotic ones via the **coronary steal** phenomenon — see Ch 24).

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Systemic circulation / pulmonary circulation** — two serial circuits driven by the left and right hearts respectively.
- **Cardiac output (CO)** — volume of blood pumped per minute by one ventricle; $\text{CO} = \text{HR} \cdot \text{SV}$.
- **Cardiac index** — CO / body surface area.
- **Stroke volume (SV) / heart rate (HR) / ejection fraction (EF)** — chamber output indices.
- **Driving pressure / transmural pressure / hydrostatic pressure** — three pressure differences with three axes (axial, radial, vertical).
- **Mean arterial pressure (MAP)** — time average of arterial pressure; clinical approx. $\text{DAP} + \tfrac{1}{3}\text{PP}$.
- **Systolic / diastolic arterial pressure (SAP / DAP)** — peak and trough of pulsatile arterial pressure.
- **Pulse pressure (PP)** — SAP − DAP.
- **Ohm's law of hydrodynamics** — $\Delta P = F \cdot R$.
- **Series and parallel resistance addition** — same algebra as electrical circuits.
- **Hagen–Poiseuille law** — $F = \pi r^4 \Delta P / (8 \eta L)$; valid for steady laminar newtonian flow in rigid cylinders.
- **Viscosity ($\eta$)** — shear stress / shear rate; units of poise; whole blood ≈ 3 cP.
- **Peripheral resistance unit (PRU)** — mm Hg per mL/s; **total peripheral resistance (TPR)** ≈ MAP / CO.
- **Reynolds number (Re)** — $2 r v \rho / \eta$; laminar < ~2000, turbulent > ~3000.
- **Laminar vs. turbulent flow** — parabolic vs. blunted velocity profiles; silent vs. murmur-producing.
- **Bruit / murmur / thrill** — clinical correlates of turbulence.
- **Compliance ($C = \Delta V / \Delta P$)** vs. **elastance** ($E = 1/C$).
- **Windkessel / capacitance vessels** — high-compliance arterial elastic / venous reservoir.
- **Bernoulli equation** — $P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{const}$ along a streamline.
- **Continuity equation** — $F = A \cdot v$.
- **Hydrostatic pressure** — $\Delta P = \rho g \Delta h$.
- **Sphygmomanometry / Korotkoff sounds (phases I–V) / palpatory method / auscultatory method**.
- **Right-heart catheterization / Swan–Ganz / pulmonary wedge pressure**.
- **Electromagnetic flowmeter (Faraday induction)**.
- **Ultrasound flowmeter / Doppler / transit-time**.
- **Fick principle** — $\dot Q_X = F \cdot [X]$; conservation of mass.
- **Stewart–Hamilton dye dilution / indocyanine green (Cardiogreen)**.
- **Thermodilution** — cold-saline analog of dye dilution; the standard Swan–Ganz CO measurement.
- **Clearance methods** — BSP (hepatic), PAH (renal), ¹³³Xe/⁸⁵Kr (coronary, muscle), N₂O Kety–Schmidt (cerebral), ²⁰¹Tl scintigraphy (coronary).
- **Angiography / gated radionuclide ventriculography (MUGA) / M-mode / 2-D / Doppler / color flow Doppler echocardiography / cardiac MR**.
- **End-diastolic volume (EDV) / end-systolic volume (ESV)** → EF = (EDV − ESV)/EDV.
- **Mean systemic filling pressure** — preview of Ch 23: the pressure throughout the circulation when the heart is stopped, determined by compliance and blood volume.
- **Series vs. parallel vascular beds** — anatomical motif of Fig. 17-3.
