# Chapter 35 — Transport of Sodium and Chloride

> Section VI · The Urinary System · pp. 754–769 · Authors: Gerhard Giebisch, Erich E. Windhager, Peter S. Aronson

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Segmental Na⁺ and Cl⁻ handling along the nephron** (pp. 754–756) — fractions reabsorbed per segment (~67% PT, ~25% TAL, ~5% DCT/CCT, ~3% MCD); transcellular vs. paracellular routes; the two-membrane model; the basolateral Na-K pump as the engine.
2. **Cellular and molecular transporters** (pp. 756–761) — apical entry pathways segment by segment: NHE3 + Na/solute cotransport (PT), NKCC2 + ROMK + lumen-positive Vte (TAL), NCC (DCT), ENaC (principal cell). Cl⁻ handling: paracellular in early PT, Cl-base exchange in late PT, NKCC2 and basolateral ClC channels in TAL, pendrin in β-intercalated cells.
3. **Water reabsorption and renal O₂ cost** (pp. 761–763) — passive, secondary to solute; isosmotic Windhager microperfusion result; AQP1; O₂ consumption ∝ Na⁺ transport.
4. **Regulation of Na⁺/Cl⁻ transport** (pp. 763–769) — glomerulotubular balance (peritubular Starling + luminal flow sensing); distal load dependence; the four effectors of effective circulating volume (RAAS, sympathetic nerves, AVP, ANP); plus dopamine, Ca²⁺/CaSR, ouabain-like steroid, prostaglandins, bradykinin.

## Section 1 — Segmental Na⁺ and Cl⁻ handling along the nephron (pp. 754–756)

### Subsection headings
- **Na⁺ and Cl⁻ reabsorption decreases from proximal tubules to Henle's loops to classic distal tubules to collecting tubules and ducts** (pp. 754–755)
- **The tubule reabsorbs Na⁺ via both the transcellular and the paracellular pathways** (pp. 755–756)

### Core claims
- **Filtered load of Na⁺** = GFR × plasma [Na⁺] = ~180 L/day × ~142 mM ≈ **25,500 mmol/day** — about nine times the total Na⁺ in body fluids, equivalent to the Na⁺ in ~1.5 kg of table salt. On a typical Western diet (~120 mmol/day intake), the kidneys reabsorb **~99.6%** of the filtered Na⁺, so even tiny changes in fractional reabsorption shift total-body Na⁺, ECF volume, body weight, and blood pressure.
- **Segmental fractions of filtered Na⁺ reabsorbed** (Fig. 35-2):
  - **Proximal tubule ~67%** — TF_Na/P_Na stays ≈1.0 throughout (reabsorbate is iso-natremic), so PT reabsorption is **nearly isosmotic**.
  - **Loop of Henle / TAL ~25%** — low water permeability of the TAL means Na⁺ is reabsorbed faster than water, so TF_Na/P_Na entering the DCT falls to ~0.45.
  - **DCT through cortical collecting tubule ~5%** of filtered Na⁺.
  - **Medullary collecting duct ~3%** of filtered Na⁺.
  - The distal nephron reabsorbs small amounts but can build a **steep transepithelial gradient** and responds to mineralocorticoids and AVP.
- **Two pathways** (Fig. 35-3A): the **transcellular** path (sequential apical then basolateral crossing; governed by membrane channels/transporters and electrochemical gradients) and the **paracellular** path (entirely extracellular, through tight junctions; governed by transepithelial driving forces and tight-junction permeability set by claudins).
- **Transcellular mechanism** is a variation of the classic two-membrane model: (1) passive apical Na⁺ entry down the electrochemical gradient — via cotransporters/exchangers in PT, TAL, DCT, but via ENaC in collecting ducts; (2) active basolateral extrusion by the **Na-K pump**, which keeps [Na⁺]ᵢ ~15 mM and [K⁺]ᵢ ~120 mM. Basolateral K⁺ permeability gives a cell-negative voltage (~−70 mV).
- **Paracellular electrical coupling**: in a leaky epithelium the basolateral and apical membrane voltages track each other through the tight-junction shunt. So in the early PT the apical membrane sits at ~−67 mV, close to the basolateral −70 mV — far more negative than its own channel complement would predict (Fig. 35-3B). This couples Na-K pump activity to apical Na⁺ entry: a faster pump both lowers [Na⁺]ᵢ and hyperpolarizes both membranes, enhancing the gradient for apical Na⁺ entry.
- **Leakiness decreases along the nephron** (PT most leaky → papillary collecting ducts tightest), set by claudin expression. Tighter segments can sustain steeper Na⁺/Cl⁻ gradients; the distal nephron reaches much lower luminal [Na⁺] and [Cl⁻] than the PT.

### Equations
- **Filtered load of Na⁺**:

  $$\text{Filtered load}_{Na} = \text{GFR} \times P_{Na} \approx 180~\text{L/day} \times 142~\text{mM} \approx 25{,}500~\text{mmol/day}$$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The proximal tubule reabsorbs the largest fraction of filtered Na⁺ (~67%)." (p. 754)
- > "Because Na⁺ salts are the dominant osmotically active solutes in the filtrate, reabsorption must be a nearly isosmotic process." (p. 754)
- > "Although the distal nephron reabsorbs only small amounts of Na⁺, it can establish a steep transepithelial concentration gradient and can respond to several hormones, including mineralocorticoids and arginine vasopressin (AVP)." (p. 754)
- > "In the transcellular pathway, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ sequentially traverse the apical and basolateral membranes before entering the blood. In the paracellular pathway, these ions move entirely by an extracellular route, through the tight junctions between cells." (p. 755)
- > "This Na⁺ extrusion is mediated by the Na-K pump … which keeps [Na⁺]ᵢ low (~15 mM) and [K⁺]ᵢ high (~120 mM)." (p. 755)
- > "In general, the leakiness of the paracellular pathway decreases along the nephron from the proximal tubule (the most leaky) to the papillary collecting ducts." (p. 755)

## Section 2 — Cellular and molecular transporters (pp. 756–761)

### Subsection headings
- **Na⁺ reabsorption involves apical transporters or ENaCs and a basolateral Na-K pump** (pp. 756–759)
- **Cl⁻ reabsorption involves both paracellular and transcellular pathways** (pp. 759–761)

### Core claims — Na⁺ apical entry, segment by segment (Fig. 35-4)

- **Proximal tubule (Fig. 35-4A):** apical entry by (i) electrogenic **Na⁺-coupled cotransporters** (Na/glucose, Na/amino acid, Na/phosphate, Na/sulfate, Na/lactate, Na/citrate — net positive charge in, exploiting low [Na⁺]ᵢ and negative apical voltage) and (ii) the electroneutral **Na-H exchanger NHE3** (extrudes H⁺ for Na⁺). Basolateral exit by the **Na-K pump** and, to a lesser extent, the electrogenic **Na/HCO₃ cotransporter NBCe1**. Because the early PT is leaky and lumen-negative, **~⅓ of transcellular-transported Na⁺ backleaks** to the lumen paracellularly.
- **Thin limbs of Henle's loop:** Na⁺ transport is **almost entirely passive and paracellular**.
- **Thick ascending limb (Fig. 35-4B):** two transcellular apical pathways — **NKCC2** (the Na/K/Cl cotransporter 2, electroneutral 1 Na⁺ : 1 K⁺ : 2 Cl⁻, the **furosemide/bumetanide target**) and **NHE3**. NKCC2-imported K⁺ recycles to the lumen via apical K⁺ channels (**ROMK**), replenishing luminal K⁺ to sustain cotransport. The TAL apical membrane is K⁺-selective while the basolateral membrane is permeable to both K⁺ and Cl⁻, so the apical membrane is more negative than the basolateral → **lumen-positive Vte (~+15 mV)**. This drives **paracellular reabsorption of Na⁺ (≈ half the TAL Na⁺ load), K⁺, Ca²⁺, and Mg²⁺**. Low water permeability makes the TAL a **diluting segment**.
- **Distal convoluted tubule (Fig. 35-4C):** Na⁺ reabsorption is **almost exclusively transcellular** via the electroneutral **Na-Cl cotransporter NCC** (same SLC12 family as NKCC2 but **K⁺-independent and thiazide-sensitive**). Basolateral exit by the Na-K pump. Low water permeability makes the DCT a second **diluting segment**.
- **Connecting tubule / ICT / CCT principal cells (Fig. 35-4D):** Na⁺ enters apically through **ENaC** (epithelial Na⁺ channel; trimeric α/β/γ subunits, each with two transmembrane segments; **blocked by amiloride**; distinct from voltage-gated Na⁺ channels). Apical Na⁺ entry (lumen-negative tendency) plus basolateral K⁺ exit (cell-negative tendency) plus apical K⁺ exit (opposing) net to a **lumen-negative Vte ~−40 mV** in the CCT. Aldosterone and AVP modulate the number of open ENaCs.
- **Medullary collecting duct:** reabsorbs only ~3% of filtered Na⁺ via ENaC + basolateral Na-K pump.

### Core claims — Cl⁻ handling (Fig. 35-5)

- **Early PT (Fig. 35-5A):** Cl⁻ reabsorption is mainly **paracellular**. The S1 starts with no Cl⁻ gradient ([Cl⁻]_lumen ≈ 115 mM ≈ blood), but the lumen-**negative** Vte (from electrogenic Na/glucose and Na/AA cotransport) plus solvent drag drive Cl⁻.
- **Late PT (Fig. 35-5B):** preferential HCO₃⁻ reabsorption in S2/S3 leaves Cl⁻ behind, so [Cl⁻]_lumen rises to ~135 mM (vs. blood ~115). This lumen-to-blood Cl⁻ gradient drives passive **paracellular** Cl⁻ reabsorption and generates a **lumen-positive Vte** that in turn drives paracellular Na⁺. Transcellular Cl⁻ also rises: apical **Cl-base exchange** (Cl⁻ for formate/oxalate/HCO₃⁻/OH⁻, via **SLC26A6**) — a tertiary-active transporter sustained by NHE3-supplied H⁺ recycling the base. Basolateral exit via a **CFTR-like Cl⁻ channel** and **K/Cl cotransporters (KCC1/3/4)**.
- **TAL (Fig. 35-5C):** Cl⁻ enters apically via **NKCC2**; exits basolaterally via **ClC-family Cl⁻ channels**. All TAL Cl⁻ reabsorption is transcellular (vs. only half of Na⁺); overall Na⁺ and Cl⁻ reabsorption are equal because NKCC2 carries 2 Cl⁻ per 1 Na⁺ (the extra Cl⁻ matches the paracellular Na⁺).
- **DCT (Fig. 35-5D):** apical Cl⁻ via **NCC**; basolateral exit via TAL-like Cl⁻ channels.
- **Collecting ducts (Fig. 35-5E, F):** two routes. (1) The principal-cell lumen-negative Vte (~−40 mV) drives **paracellular** Cl⁻ diffusion. (2) **β-intercalated cells** reabsorb Cl⁻ transcellularly: apical **pendrin (SLC26A4)** Cl-HCO₃ exchange; basolateral Cl⁻ channels. Two pendrin cycles in parallel with one NDCBE (Na-dependent Cl-HCO₃ exchange) cycle give net electroneutral apical NaCl entry. α-intercalated and principal cells do **not** do transcellular Cl⁻.

### Equations
- **NKCC2 (TAL) stoichiometry** — electroneutral:

  $$1~Na^+ : 1~K^+ : 2~Cl^- \quad \text{(apical, furosemide-sensitive)}$$

- **NCC (DCT) stoichiometry** — electroneutral, K⁺-independent:

  $$1~Na^+ : 1~Cl^- \quad \text{(apical, thiazide-sensitive)}$$

- **ENaC (principal cell)** — electrogenic Na⁺ channel (amiloride-blocked); no fixed coupling ratio, conductive Na⁺ entry driven by the apical electrochemical gradient.

- **Transepithelial electrochemical driving force for Na⁺** (Table 35-1): the sum of a chemical term computed from luminal vs. plasma [Na⁺] (P_Na = 142 mM reference) and the transepithelial voltage; positive favors lumen→blood reabsorption, negative favors blood→lumen backleak.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Na/K/Cl cotransporter 2 (NKCC2) couples the inward movement of 1 Na⁺, 1 K⁺, and 2 Cl⁻ ions in an electroneutral process driven by the downhill concentration gradients of Na⁺ and Cl⁻." (p. 757)
- > "First, the loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide and bumetanide) inhibit Na/K/Cl cotransport." (p. 757)
- > "A key aspect of the paracellular pathway for Na⁺ reabsorption in the TAL is a lumen-positive Vte." (p. 757)
- > "Because the TAL has a low water permeability, removing luminal NaCl leaves the remaining tubule fluid hypo-osmotic. Hence, the TAL is sometimes referred to as the diluting segment." (p. 758)
- > "The apical step of Na⁺ uptake is mediated by an electroneutral Na/Cl cotransporter (NCC) … the NCC differs from NKCC2 in being independent of K⁺ and highly sensitive to thiazide diuretics." (p. 758)
- > "This channel is unique in that low levels of the diuretic drug amiloride specifically block it." (p. 758)
- > "Overall, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ reabsorption are identical because the apical NKCC2 cotransporter moves two Cl⁻ for each Na⁺." (p. 759)
- > "the B-type intercalated cells reabsorb Cl⁻ via a transcellular process in which pendrin (SLC26A4 …) mediates Cl⁻ uptake across the apical membrane in exchange for HCO₃⁻." (pp. 760–761)

## Section 3 — Water reabsorption and renal O₂ cost (pp. 761–763)

### Subsection headings
- **Water reabsorption is passive and secondary to solute transport** (pp. 761–763)
- **The kidney's high O₂ consumption reflects a high level of active Na⁺ transport** (pp. 762–763)

### Core claims
- PT water reabsorption is **passive and follows solute**. The lumen is slightly *hypo*-osmotic (not hyper-osmotic), arguing against active water transport.
- **Windhager stationary-microperfusion experiment** (1959): introducing solutions of varying [Na⁺] (osmolality held constant with mannitol) showed that both the fluid reabsorption rate J_v and the Na⁺ reabsorption rate J_Na are **directly proportional to luminal [Na⁺]**, and the ratio J_Na/J_v equals luminal osmolality — i.e., reabsorption is **isosmotic**. At luminal [Na⁺] = 100 mM both fluxes were large and reabsorptive; at 65 mM both were zero; below that both **reversed** (net secretion), because paracellular backleak overcomes active transport. Conclusion: Na⁺ can move uphill from lumen to blood while the gradient is shallow, and **water passively follows Na⁺**.
- The lumen-to-blood osmotic gradient needed is only **2–3 mOsm** because PT water permeability is enormous — owing to high-density **AQP1** in both apical and basolateral membranes. In **AQP1-null mice**, solute/fluid reabsorption falls and the reabsorbate becomes hyperosmotic, leaving the lumen markedly hypo-osmotic.
- Water transit is mixed transcellular + paracellular, **transcellular dominant**.
- **Loop and distal nephron**: TAL and downstream segments have **low water permeability without AVP**; combined with NaCl reabsorption this lets them generate low luminal [Na⁺]/osmolality, poising the distal nephron to reabsorb water passively once AVP raises water permeability.
- **Renal O₂ consumption ∝ Na⁺ transport**: because Na⁺ transport ultimately depends on the ATP-driven Na-K pump, renal O₂ use is large — the kidneys are <0.5% of body weight but account for **7–10% of total O₂ consumption**. O₂ use vs. Na⁺ reabsorption is a straight line (Fig. 35-9); a transport-independent intercept reflects basal cell-maintenance metabolism. Renal blood flow is so high that the arteriovenous PO₂ difference is actually smaller than in heart or brain.

### Equations
- **Isosmotic proportionality** (Fig. 35-7): J_v and J_Na ∝ initial luminal [Na⁺], with

  $$\frac{J_{Na}}{J_{v}} = \text{luminal osmolality} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{reabsorbate is isosmotic}$$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "the ratio J_Na/J_v is constant and equal to the osmolality of the lumen, which indicates that Na⁺ reabsorption and secretion are approximately isosmotic." (p. 761)
- > "This experiment shows that Na⁺ can move uphill from lumen to blood as long as the opposing Na⁺ gradient is not too steep, and also suggests that the movement of water is not active but passively follows the reabsorption of Na⁺." (p. 762)
- > "because the water permeability of the proximal-tubule epithelium to water is so high, the osmolality gradient needed to generate the observed passive reabsorption of water is only 2 to 3 mOsm." (p. 762)
- > "the reason for the high rate of water movement through the proximal-tubule cell is the presence of a high density of aquaporin 1 (AQP1) water channels in both the apical and basolateral membranes." (p. 762)
- > "Despite their low weight (<0.5% of body weight), the kidneys are responsible for 7% to 10% of total O₂ consumption." (pp. 762–763)

## Section 4 — Regulation of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ transport (pp. 763–769)

### Subsection headings
- **Glomerulotubular balance stabilizes fractional Na⁺ reabsorption by the proximal tubule** (pp. 763–764)
- **The proximal tubule achieves GT balance by both peritubular and luminal mechanisms** (pp. 764–765)
- **ECF volume contraction or expansion upsets GT balance** (p. 765)
- **The distal nephron also increases Na⁺ reabsorption in response to an increased Na⁺ load** (pp. 765–766)
- **Four parallel pathways that regulate effective circulating volume all modulate Na⁺ reabsorption** (pp. 766–768)
- **Dopamine, elevated plasma [Ca²⁺], an endogenous steroid, prostaglandins, and bradykinin all decrease Na⁺ reabsorption** (pp. 768–769)

### Core claims — the three control levels
The body regulates Na⁺ excretion by three mechanisms: (1) **renal hemodynamics** altering the Na⁺ load; (2) three factors responding to a *fall* in effective circulating volume — **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone, renal sympathetic nerves, AVP** — that *increase* Na⁺ reabsorption; (3) factors responding to a *rise* in effective circulating volume — **ANP and dopamine** — that produce **natriuresis**.

### Core claims — glomerulotubular (GT) balance
- **GT balance**: the PT reabsorbs a relatively **constant fraction** of the Na⁺ load despite GFR changes (high-protein diet, exercise, pain, anesthesia), so spontaneous GFR swings do not cause large excretion swings. It is **independent of neural/hormonal control**. Absolute Na⁺ reabsorption rises proportionally with filtered load (Fig. 35-10), but GT balance is **not always perfect**, especially at the whole-kidney level (distal Na⁺ handling is under neuro-hormonal control).
- **Peritubular mechanism** (Fig. 35-11): Starling forces across peritubular capillaries govern uptake of reabsorbate. Three transport steps — apical entry; basolateral exit into the lateral interspace; then either backleak to lumen (3a) or uptake into blood (3b). When **filtration fraction rises** (e.g., ↑efferent / ↓afferent resistance), peritubular oncotic pressure (π_pc) rises and peritubular hydrostatic pressure (P_pc) falls, so more reabsorbate is pulled into the capillary and **backleak falls** → more net NaCl/water reabsorption. A fall in GFR/FF does the opposite.
- **Luminal mechanism**: higher tubule flow makes luminal [glucose]/[amino acids]/[HCO₃⁻] fall **less steeply**, leaving more substrate for Na⁺-coupled reabsorption downstream; integrated over the PT, higher flow → more Na⁺ reabsorption. Plus **flow sensing** (cilium/microvillus bending) and **humoral factors** in the filtrate (ANG II — filtered and locally secreted — stimulates PT Na⁺ reabsorption).
- **ECF volume disturbances upset GT balance**: volume **contraction** (sweating, diarrhea) *increases* fractional PT Na⁺/water reabsorption beyond perfect GT balance (sparing Na⁺); volume **expansion** *decreases* it (enhancing excretion). These arise from the FF/oncotic mechanism plus direct AT₁-receptor stimulation of PT transcellular Na⁺ reabsorption.

### Core claims — distal load dependence
- The distal nephron also **raises absolute Na⁺ reabsorption with increased flow/delivery** (Fig. 35-12), but less steeply: a 4× flow increase may only **2×** cumulative reabsorption (vs. near-proportional in the PT). This load dependence explains why **proximally-acting diuretics (e.g., carbonic anhydrase inhibitors) are relatively weak** — the extra Na⁺ they deliver is largely recaptured by the TAL and distal nephron.

### Core claims — the four effectors of effective circulating volume
- **Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone:**
  - **ANG II** binds AT₁ on apical and basolateral PT membranes → via **PKC stimulates NHE3**; upregulates **NHE3 and NKCC2** in the TAL, **NCC** in the DCT, and apical Na⁺ channels in the ICT (via WNK4/SPAK kinases). All promote Na⁺ reabsorption.
  - **Aldosterone** stimulates **NCC** (DCT) and **ENaC** (late DCT, CNT, collecting ducts). Normally <10% of filtered Na⁺ is under aldosterone control, but sustained loss exceeds daily intake — hence severe Na⁺ depletion in **Addison disease** (adrenal insufficiency). Aldosterone binds cytoplasmic **mineralocorticoid receptors (MR)** → nuclear transcription (effects take hours): upregulates apical ENaC, apical K⁺ channels, basolateral Na-K pump, and mitochondrial metabolism. ENaC effect = ↑(channel number × open probability, NP_o). It also acts through **SGK** kinase, which phosphorylates/inhibits the ubiquitin ligase **Nedd4-2**, reducing ENaC endocytosis and raising apical ENaC abundance. (**Liddle syndrome** = gain-of-function ENaC that escapes Nedd4-2 regulation.)
  - **11β-HSD2** protects MR from cortisol by converting cortisol → inactive cortisone; its deficiency or inhibition (licorice/glycyrrhetinic acid, carbenoxolone) causes **apparent mineralocorticoid excess (AME)**: Na⁺ retention, hypokalemia, hypertension.
- **Sympathetic nerves:** norepinephrine has two direct effects — high levels reduce renal blood flow/GFR (↓filtered load); even low levels activate **α-adrenergic receptors** on PT cells, stimulating apical **NHE3** and basolateral **Na-K pump**, raising Na⁺ reabsorption independent of hemodynamics. Renal denervation can lower BP in resistant hypertension.
- **AVP (ADH):** binds basolateral **V₂ receptor** → Gs → ↑cAMP. Besides concentrating urine, AVP stimulates Na⁺ reabsorption: in the TAL it stimulates **NKCC2 and K⁺ channels**; in ICT/CCT principal cells it increases the number of open ENaCs (NP_o).
- **ANP (the lone natriuretic effector):** released by atrial myocytes, activates a **receptor guanylyl cyclase → cGMP**. Mostly hemodynamic — renal vasodilation raises cortical and medullary blood flow, increasing Na⁺ load to PT/TAL and washing out the medullary interstitium (↓passive Na⁺ reabsorption in the thin ascending limb). ANP also **directly inhibits** Na⁺ transport in the IMCD (decreasing apical nonselective cation channel activity). Net: increased distal Na⁺ delivery and urinary Na⁺ excretion.

### Core claims — five additional natriuretic agents
- **Dopamine:** synthesized from filtered/circulating L-dopa by PT cells (L-amino-acid decarboxylase) and secreted into the lumen; Na⁺ loading ↑ its synthesis/excretion. Causes renal vasodilation **and** directly inhibits transport — D1 receptors ↑cAMP, inhibiting apical **NHE3** and basolateral **Na-K pump**. Low-dose dopamine → natriuresis.
- **Elevated plasma [Ca²⁺]:** basolateral **Ca²⁺-sensing receptor (CaSR)** in the cortical TAL inhibits apical **NKCC2 and K⁺ channels** (Fig. 35-14), lowering the lumen-positive Vte and paracellular Na⁺ (and Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) reabsorption. Mechanism: CaSR couples to Gαi (↓cAMP), to a Gq/G11 member → PLA₂ → arachidonic acid → **20-HETE** (inhibits NKCC2/K⁺ channels), and to Gαq → IP₃/↑[Ca²⁺]ᵢ and **PKC** (also inhibits cotransport).
- **Endogenous Na-K pump inhibitor:** an **ouabain-like steroid** in plasma; rises with salt loading and is high in hypertension. Binding collecting-duct Na-K pumps raises [Na⁺]ᵢ and enhances Na⁺ excretion.
- **Prostaglandins (PGE₂):** in the TAL inhibits apical and basolateral K⁺ channels → ↑[Cl⁻]ᵢ → impedes NKCC2 turnover and reduces the lumen-positive Vte → ↓NaCl reabsorption; in the CCT inhibits principal-cell electrogenic Na⁺ reabsorption. NSAIDs (PG-synthesis inhibitors) → Na⁺ retention, diuretic resistance, worsened hypertension.
- **Bradykinin:** in the TAL stimulates PGE₂ → ↓NaCl reabsorption; in the CCT inhibits **ENaC** → natriuresis. ACE degrades bradykinin, so **ACE inhibitors** enhance Na⁺ excretion partly by sparing bradykinin.

### Equations
- **Filtration fraction** (peritubular mechanism of GT balance):

  $$FF = \frac{\text{GFR}}{\text{renal plasma flow}}$$

  Raising FF at constant plasma flow raises peritubular oncotic pressure and lowers peritubular hydrostatic pressure, increasing net reabsorption.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "This constancy of fractional Na⁺ reabsorption along the proximal tubule—glomerulotubular (GT) balance—is independent of external neural and hormonal control, and prevents spontaneous fluctuations in GFR from causing marked changes in Na⁺ excretion." (p. 763)
- > "increasing flow by a factor of 4 in the distal nephron may cause cumulative Na⁺ reabsorption to rise by only a factor of 2." (p. 765)
- > "Aldosterone—the final element in the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis—stimulates Na⁺ reabsorption by NCC in the DCT and by the ENaC in the late DCT, connecting tubule, and collecting ducts." (p. 767)
- > "even low levels of sympathetic stimulation activate α-adrenergic receptors in proximal tubules. This activation stimulates both the apical NHE3 and basolateral Na-K pump … thereby increasing Na⁺ reabsorption, independent of any hemodynamic effects." (pp. 767–768)
- > "Of the four parallel effectors that control effective circulating volume, ANP … is the only one that promotes natriuresis." (p. 768)
- > "Thus, 11β-HSD2 enhances the apparent specificity of MRs by protecting them from illicit occupancy by cortisol." (p. 767)
- > "one of the mechanisms by which ACE inhibitors enhance Na⁺ excretion and reduce blood pressure is blocking bradykinin breakdown." (p. 769)

#### Figure 35-11 — Peritubular mechanisms of GT balance *(listed)*

Two panels comparing **A** normal GFR with **B** increased filtration fraction. P_pc and π_pc are peritubular capillary hydrostatic and oncotic pressures. Net tubule Na⁺ reabsorption = active transcellular transport − paracellular backleak; net capillary uptake = oncotic-driven absorption − hydrostatic-driven filtration. #### Figure 35-13 — Cellular actions of aldosterone *(viewed)*

Four panels on a principal cell of the cortical collecting tubule.

- **A — Genomic effects.** Aldosterone crosses the basolateral membrane, binds cytoplasmic **MR**; the Aldo-MR complex enters the nucleus and stimulates transcription (mRNA → proteins), increasing **apical ENaC Na⁺ channels, mitochondrial enzymes, and basolateral Na-K pumps**. Inset: average number of open Na⁺ channels (NP_o) rises with plasma aldosterone (rat CCT patch-clamp).
- **B — Regulation of ENaC.** The ubiquitin ligase **Nedd4-2** binds the PPXY motif of ENaC subunits and ubiquitinates them for endocytic degradation. **SGK1** phosphorylates/blocks Nedd4-2 (preventing ENaC degradation) and also directly phosphorylates the ENaC α subunit (Ser-621) to activate it. (PPXY mutation = **Liddle syndrome**.)
- **C — Role of 11β-HSD2.** The enzyme converts cortisol to inactive **cortisone** (low MR/GR affinity), so abundant cortisol cannot illicitly occupy MR; aldosterone (not metabolized) does.
- **D — Inhibition of 11β-HSD2.** **Glycyrrhetinic acid (licorice)** inhibits the enzyme → cortisol is not metabolized and occupies MR/GR → apparent mineralocorticoid excess.

## Glossary

- **Filtered Na⁺ load** — GFR × P_Na ≈ 25,500 mmol/day; ~99.6% reabsorbed.
- **Segmental fractions** — PT ~67%, loop/TAL ~25%, DCT–CCT ~5%, MCD ~3%; ~0.4% excreted.
- **NHE3** — apical Na-H exchanger; PT and TAL; α-adrenergic, ANG II, dopamine targets.
- **NKCC2 (Na/K/Cl cotransporter 2)** — apical TAL, 1 Na⁺ : 1 K⁺ : 2 Cl⁻; **furosemide/bumetanide** target.
- **ROMK** — apical K⁺ channel recycling K⁺ in the TAL to sustain NKCC2.
- **NCC (Na-Cl cotransporter)** — apical DCT, 1 Na⁺ : 1 Cl⁻, K⁺-independent; **thiazide** target.
- **ENaC** — apical epithelial Na⁺ channel (α/β/γ) in principal cells; **amiloride**-blocked; aldosterone/AVP-regulated; Liddle syndrome.
- **Na-K pump** — universal basolateral 3 Na⁺ : 2 K⁺ ATPase; the engine for all Na⁺ reabsorption.
- **Lumen-positive Vte** — the TAL's hallmark (~+15 mV); drives paracellular Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺.
- **Diluting segment** — TAL and DCT (NaCl reabsorption without water).
- **Pendrin (SLC26A4)** — apical Cl-HCO₃ exchanger in β-intercalated cells.
- **SLC26A6** — late-PT apical Cl-base exchanger (tertiary active, NHE3-coupled).
- **AQP1** — apical + basolateral PT water channel; the route for passive isosmotic water reabsorption.
- **Glomerulotubular (GT) balance** — constant fractional PT Na⁺ reabsorption; peritubular (Starling) + luminal mechanisms.
- **Filtration fraction (FF)** — GFR / renal plasma flow; rising FF raises peritubular oncotic pressure → ↑reabsorption.
- **RAAS** — ANG II (NHE3, NKCC2, NCC, ENaC) + aldosterone (MR → ENaC, NCC, Na-K pump via SGK1/Nedd4-2).
- **11β-HSD2** — converts cortisol → cortisone to protect MR; deficiency/licorice → apparent mineralocorticoid excess.
- **ANP** — the only natriuretic effector among the four; cGMP; vasodilation + IMCD inhibition.
- **CaSR** — basolateral Ca²⁺-sensing receptor in the TAL; high [Ca²⁺] inhibits NKCC2/K⁺ channels.
- **20-HETE** — arachidonate metabolite mediating CaSR inhibition of NKCC2.
