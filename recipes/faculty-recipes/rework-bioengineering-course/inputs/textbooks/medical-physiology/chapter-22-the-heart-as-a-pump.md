# Chapter 22 — The Heart as a Pump

> Section IV · The Cardiovascular System · pp. 507–532 · Author: Emile L. Boulpaep
> This chapter takes the **single arterial-pressure trace** of Ch 17 (Fig. 17-7) and the **cellular Frank–Starling preview** of Ch 9 (Fig. 9-9, length-tension; Fig. 9-10, force–velocity / Hill) and assembles them into a **whole-organ pump description**: the cardiac cycle as a sequence of four valvular phases, the Wiggers composite of pressure/volume/ECG/sounds/echocardiogram, the ventricular pressure–volume loop, and the three intrinsic-extrinsic determinants of stroke volume (**preload, afterload, contractility**). It is the bridge from Ch 21 (cardiac electrophysiology) to Ch 23 (regulation of arterial pressure and cardiac output).

## Section 1 — The cardiac cycle (pp. 507–517)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The closing and opening of the cardiac valves define four phases of the cardiac cycle** (pp. 507–508)
- **Changes in ventricular volume, pressure, and flow accompany the four phases of the cardiac cycle** (pp. 508–509) (N22-1)
- **The ECG, phonocardiogram, and echocardiogram all follow the cyclic pattern of the cardiac cycle** (pp. 509–511)
- **The cardiac cycle causes flow waves in the aorta and peripheral vessels** (pp. 511–513)
- **The cardiac cycle also causes pressure waves in the aorta and peripheral vessels** (pp. 513)
- **Distortion of pressure waves is the result of their propagation along the arterial tree** (pp. 513–515) (N22-4)
- **Pressure waves in veins do not originate from arterial waves** (pp. 515–517)

### Core claims

#### The four valvular phases
The heart is a **two-stroke reciprocating pump**. Each cycle is partitioned by valvular events into four phases:

| Phase | Inlet (AV) valve | Outlet (semilunar) valve | Event |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Inflow / filling | open | closed | ventricular filling (rapid filling → diastasis → atrial contraction) |
| 2. Isovolumetric contraction | closed | closed | LV pressure rises with no volume change |
| 3. Outflow / ejection | closed | open | rapid + decreased ejection |
| 4. Isovolumetric relaxation | closed | closed | LV pressure falls with no volume change |

- **Systole** = phases 2 + 3 (contraction). **Diastole** = phases 4 + 1 (relaxation + filling).
- At HR = 75 bpm (cycle = 800 ms), systole ≈ 300 ms, diastole ≈ 500 ms. As HR rises, **diastole shortens preferentially**.
- The AV valves (tricuspid, mitral) open passively when atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure; the semilunar valves (pulmonary, aortic) open passively when ventricular pressure exceeds great-vessel pressure. Closure is the reverse. Valve closure produces audible **heart sounds**; leaky or stenotic valves produce **murmurs** (cf. Box 17-1).
- Right- and left-heart events are **homologous** in sequence (Fig. 22-1A vs B) and produce identical SVs at steady state (continuity, p. 414), but the right-heart pressures are a scaled-down version of the left.

#### Left-heart cycle in detail (Fig. 22-1B, anchored at the four valvular crossings)
- **Diastasis** (middle of phase 1): mitral valve wide open; LV volume plateau; LV and LA pressures rise gently in parallel, driven by pulmonary venous pressure.
- **Atrial contraction** (end of phase 1, following the P-wave): adds ≤ 20% of the eventual SV at rest, up to ~40% in heavy exercise (Box 22-1).
- **Isovolumetric contraction** (phase 2): QRS triggers contraction; LV pressure rises above LA pressure → mitral valve closes (S1); aortic valve still closed → volume constant at EDV ≈ 120 mL while pressure rockets toward aortic diastolic pressure (~80 mm Hg).
- **Rapid ejection** (early phase 3): LV pressure crosses aortic pressure → aortic valve opens; LV pressure continues to rise to peak systolic ≈ 130 mm Hg as ~50 mL of blood is ejected; aortic flow rises to ~30 L/min instantaneously.
- **Decreased ejection** (late phase 3): T-wave; LV begins relaxing; both LV and aortic pressures fall; the remaining ~20 mL of SV continues to be ejected by **inertia** even as the LV-aortic pressure gradient reverses (N22-2).
- **Isovolumetric relaxation** (phase 4): aortic valve snaps shut after a brief retrograde flow → **dicrotic notch (incisura)** in the aortic trace; LV pressure plummets toward 0 with both valves closed.
- **Rapid ventricular filling** (early phase 1): LV pressure crosses below LA pressure → mitral valve opens → LV volume rises rapidly while LA and LV pressures track in parallel.

Approximate per-cycle numbers (Fig. 22-1B / pp. 519–520): EDV ≈ 120 mL, ESV ≈ 50 mL, SV ≈ 70 mL, EF ≈ 0.6.

#### Heart sounds (Table 22-2)

| Sound | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| S1 ("lub") | AV-valve closure | Two components: M1 (mitral, slightly earlier) + T1 (tricuspid). Vibrations of the AV-valve leaflets and adjacent ventricular wall — *not* mechanical apposition itself — generate the sound. |
| S2 ("dub") | Semilunar-valve closure | A2 (aortic) precedes P2 (pulmonary); **physiological splitting widens during inspiration** because the larger RV EDV prolongs RV ejection and delays P2. |
| S3 | Rapid ventricular filling | Physiologic in children; pathologic ("protodiastolic / ventricular gallop", rhythm S1-S2-S3) in adults with low LV compliance / volume overload. |
| S4 | Atrial contraction into low-compliance ventricle | Always pathologic; "presystolic / atrial gallop", rhythm S4-S1-S2. |
| OS | Opening snap | Stiffened mitral valve (mitral stenosis); occurs in early diastole just after S2. |

Heart-sound dominant frequencies are **110–180 Hz**, lower than murmur frequencies (180–500 Hz, p. 511).

#### Aortic flow and pressure waves; Windkessel (Box 22-2; Figs 22-3, 22-4, 22-5, 22-6)
- **Aortic-arch peak flow** during rapid ejection: ~30 L/min; peak linear velocity ~100 cm/s — approaching the **Reynolds turbulence threshold** (Ch 17).
- Distally, peak systolic flow falls (branching), but a **diastolic forward-flow component** appears in elastic large arteries (abdominal aorta, carotid, renal). It is the rebound of the elastic aorta — the **Windkessel effect** (Box 22-2). The compliant aorta stores volume during systole and **delivers it during diastole**, smoothing the phasic output of the ventricle into a more continuous capillary flow.
- **Pressure-wave distortion** moving distally (Fig. 22-5, 22-6): the wave gets **taller and narrower** (rising peak, narrowing pulse) until it reaches small arteries, where high resistance + high parallel compliance damp it out so capillary flow is essentially nonpulsatile. Why does the peak rise? Because pressure waves are an ensemble of frequencies (N22-4); higher-frequency components travel faster but are damped more, and constructive interference at peripheral sites produces a taller composite. Higher wall stiffness (e.g., aging) further raises wave velocity.
- **Pulse-wave velocity (PWV):** ~5–6 m/s in aorta; 10–15 m/s in small arteries. RBC convection (~1 m/s in aorta, ≈ 0 in capillaries) is far slower than the pressure wave — which is why a stethoscope at the chest and a finger on the radial pulse register with only ~0.1 s of delay.

#### Venous pressure waves (Fig. 22-7)
Three contributions to venous pulse:
1. **Cardiac cycle**, near the heart (jugular vein). Three peaks (**a, c, v**) and three dips (**av, x, y**) (Fig. 22-7A):
   - **a** = right-atrial contraction.
   - **av** = atrial relaxation + tricuspid closure.
   - **c** = early RV systole; tricuspid bulges into RA.
   - **x** = RV ejection pulls heart down, elongating veins.
   - **v** = atrial filling against closed tricuspid.
   - **y** = tricuspid opens; rapid RV filling.
2. **Respiratory cycle** (Fig. 22-7B; Poiseuille's observation). Inspiration → intrathoracic pressure drops → jugular venous pressure falls (thoracic vessels "suck in" blood); intra-abdominal pressure rises → femoral venous pressure rises.
3. **Skeletal-muscle pump** (Fig. 22-7C). Standing alone allows the foot-vein column to fill to ~95 mm Hg of hydrostatic head. Walking + venous valves rapidly drop foot-vein pressure to a low oscillating value — the **muscle-vein pump** of Ch 19, prerequisite for orthostatic tolerance.

### Equations

- **Cycle duration** (Eq. 22-1):

$$\text{Duration (s/beat)} = \frac{60 \text{ s/min}}{\text{Heart rate (beats/min)}}$$

- **Stroke volume** (Eq. 22-2):

$$\text{SV} = \text{EDV} - \text{ESV}$$

- **Ejection fraction** (Eq. 22-3):

$$\text{EF} = \frac{\text{SV}}{\text{EDV}} \quad (\text{normal} > 0.55)$$

- **Cardiac output** (Ch 17, Eq. 17-6, repeated here):

$$\text{CO} = \text{HR} \times \text{SV}$$

### Box 22-1 — Importance (and Unimportance) of Atrial Contraction (p. 508)
- Atrial fibrillation (Fig. 21-14H) abolishes the coordinated atrial "kick".
- Normal hearts tolerate AFib with no symptoms at rest; pre-existing LV dysfunction (ischemic CM, hypertension, mitral stenosis) may precipitate **congestive heart failure or cardiogenic shock**.
- Atrial thrombus → cerebral embolism / stroke risk; treatment may be elective rate control or **emergency cardioversion**.

### Box 22-2 — Effect of Aortic Compliance on Blood Flow (Windkessel) (p. 514)
- Hydraulic model: glass tube (rigid) vs rubber tube (compliant) downstream of a pulsing pressure head.
- Under continuous pressure both deliver the same flow; under **pulsatile pressure** the rubber tube **stores volume in its dilated wall during peaks** and **delivers it during pauses**, yielding higher time-averaged downstream flow and almost continuous downstream output.
- Mechanical analogue: the **Windkessel** (German *Windkessel*, "wind chamber") — a compressible air pocket above the fluid in a piston pump. The aorta and large arteries behave this way.

#### Figure 22-4 — Effect of pulsatile pressure on flow through a compliant vessel *(listed)*

A three-panel hydraulic teaching demonstration. **Panel A:** steady (continuous) input pressure through two side-by-side branches — a rigid glass tube and a compliant rubber tube — both with identical outflow resistance and identical steady output flow. **Panel B:** square-pulse pressure. The **glass tube's output flow exactly mirrors the input** (a square wave); the **rubber tube's output flow rises gradually** during the pressure peak (storing volume in its expanding wall) and **persists during the pressure trough** (the wall recoils, delivering stored volume). Time-averaged output is higher for the rubber tube. **Panel C:** the same physics drawn as a classical **Windkessel chamber** — a fluid-filled chamber with a compressible-air pocket on top, with gold arrows = systolic compression of the air pocket (energy storage) and violet arrows = diastolic recoil (energy delivery).

## Section 2 — Cardiac dynamics (pp. 517–522)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The right ventricle contracts like a bellows, whereas the left ventricle contracts like a hand squeezing a tube of toothpaste** (p. 517)
- **The right atrium contracts before the left, but the left ventricle contracts before the right** (pp. 517–519)
- **Measurements of ventricular volumes, pressures, and flows allow clinicians to judge cardiac performance** (pp. 519)
- **The pressure-volume loop of a ventricle illustrates the ejection work of the ventricle** (pp. 519–520)
- **The "pumping work" done by the heart accounts for a small fraction of the total energy the heart consumes** (pp. 520–522) (N22-5, N22-6)

### Core claims

#### Ventricular mechanics: two pumps, two architectures (Fig. 22-8)
- **RV** — thin free wall + thick septum + large surface-to-volume ratio. Contraction is a **bellows** motion: small inward excursion of a large free wall toward the septum, plus shortening of the longitudinal axis as the tricuspid annulus is pulled toward the apex, plus deep circular-LV-fiber bulging of the septum into the RV. **Geometry suits high SV, low pressure** (peak ~25 mm Hg).
- **LV** — thick concentric wall + spiral muscle layers + smaller surface-to-volume ratio. Contraction is a **toothpaste-tube squeeze**: circular-fiber constriction of chamber diameter (the dominant high-pressure component) **plus** longitudinal shortening pulling the mitral annulus toward the apex. **Geometry suits high pressure** (peak ~130 mm Hg systemic).
- **Wall stress (Laplace's law for a thin spherical or cylindrical wall)** — the high LV pressure is bought at the cost of high wall tension; thickness $h$ partially offsets radius $r$:

$$\sigma_{\text{wall}} = \frac{P \cdot r}{2 h}$$

(The textbook cites the *tension-time integral* $T \cdot \Delta t$ as the dominant energy cost; see below.)

#### Right-vs-left timing differences (Fig. 22-8C)
- **Atrial contraction:** R precedes L (SA-node origin in RA).
- **Ventricular contraction:** L precedes R; mitral closes before tricuspid (small split, normally inaudible).
- **Isovolumetric contraction:** briefer on R (smaller pressure ramp); **pulmonary valve opens before aortic**.
- **Ejection:** longer on R; **aortic valve closes before pulmonary** → physiological splitting of S2.
- **Inspiration** further delays P2 (increased RV preload → longer RV ejection), widening the split.
- **Isovolumetric relaxation:** briefer on R; **tricuspid opens before mitral** → R-ventricular filling starts first.

#### Cardiac volumes, pressures, ejection fraction (p. 519; Table 22-3)

Resting reference values:

| Chamber | Pressure (mm Hg) |
|---|---|
| RA mean (a / c / v) | 2 (3 / 4 / 4) |
| LA mean (a / c / v) | 8 (13 / 8 / 13) |
| RV peak systolic / EDP | 25 / 0–8 |
| LV peak systolic / EDP | 130 / 0–12 |
| Pulmonary artery peak / EDP / mean | 22 / 11 / 15 |
| Aorta peak / EDP / mean | 130 / 80 / 95 |
| Pulmonary capillary | ~10 |
| Systemic capillary | ~20 |

Standard volume values (Fig. 22-9): EDV ≈ 120 mL, ESV ≈ 50 mL, SV ≈ 70 mL, EF ≈ 0.6. Normal EF > 0.55.

#### The left-ventricular pressure-volume loop (Fig. 22-9)
The PV loop is a **phase plot** in (V, P) space, traversed **counterclockwise**. Time is not explicit. The four straight or curved segments correspond exactly to the four cardiac phases:

| Segment | Phase | Anchored at |
|---|---|---|
| A → B → C | Phase 1 (inflow) | bottom edge of loop; pressure rises mildly from ~5 → 10 mm Hg while volume rises from ~50 → 120 mL along the EDPVR (compliance curve) |
| C → D | Phase 2 (isovolumetric contraction) | right vertical edge; volume fixed at EDV, pressure rises ~10 → 80 mm Hg, mitral closed and aortic closed |
| D → E → F | Phase 3 (ejection) | top arc of loop; pressure rises further to ~130 (rapid ejection) then falls to ~100 (decreased ejection) while volume drops 120 → 50 mL |
| F → A | Phase 4 (isovolumetric relaxation) | left vertical edge; volume fixed at ESV, pressure drops 100 → ~7 mm Hg with both valves closed |

- **Width** of the loop along the volume axis = **SV**.
- **Right edge** at end-diastole = **EDV (preload)**.
- **Left edge** at end-systole = **ESV**.
- **Top edge** ≈ aortic pressure (afterload during ejection).

#### Cardiac work and energetics (Fig. 22-10)
- **External (pressure-volume) work per beat** (Eq. 22-4):

$$W = P \cdot \Delta V$$

  Geometrically, the **net external work** is the area enclosed by the PV loop (Fig. 22-10C): area under DEF (work done *by* the ventricle on the blood) minus area under ABC (work done *by* the blood on the ventricle as it fills passively).

- **Total external work including kinetic energy** (Eq. 22-5):

$$W_{\text{total external}} = P \cdot \Delta V + \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$$

  The kinetic-energy term is a small fraction at rest but grows during exercise (faster ejection velocities).

- **Total energy expenditure including tension heat** (Eq. 22-6):

$$E = P \cdot \Delta V + \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 + k \cdot T \cdot \Delta t$$

  where $T$ is ventricular-wall tension and $\Delta t$ is the time over which it is maintained. The product $T \cdot \Delta t$ is the **tension-time integral** (N22-6); $k$ converts to energy units.

- **The tension-time integral dominates.** External pressure-volume work is only ~**3% of total cardiac energy** at rest, rising to ~**10% in exercise**. The remaining ~90% is heat associated with maintaining wall tension — including during isovolumetric phases when no useful external work is done at all.
- **Clinical implication.** A coronary-disease patient who needs to raise CO is much better off doing so by raising **SV** (one good beat that delivers a large $\Delta V$ at modest $T$) than by raising **HR** (more total time at high $T$, larger $\sum T \cdot \Delta t$) or by raising **arterial pressure** (raises $T$ directly via Laplace).
- **Mechanical efficiency** $= W_{\text{total external}} / E$. This is distinct from EF — a high-EF heart is not necessarily efficient.
- The RV pressure-volume loop has the same shape as the LV's, but its enclosed area (external work) is ~**one-fifth** of the LV's because pulmonary pressures are correspondingly lower.

### Equations

- **Stroke volume / ejection fraction** (Eqs 22-2, 22-3, repeated from above):

$$\text{SV} = \text{EDV} - \text{ESV}, \qquad \text{EF} = \frac{\text{SV}}{\text{EDV}}$$

- **Pressure-volume work** (Eq. 22-4):

$$W = P \cdot \Delta V \approx \oint_{\text{loop}} P \, dV \quad (\text{area enclosed by PV loop})$$

- **Total work with kinetic energy** (Eq. 22-5):

$$W_{\text{total}} = P \cdot \Delta V + \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$$

- **Total energy budget** (Eq. 22-6):

$$E = \underbrace{P \cdot \Delta V}_{\text{PV work}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2}_{\text{KE}} + \underbrace{k \cdot T \cdot \Delta t}_{\text{tension heat}}$$

- **Laplace wall stress** (LV approximated as thin-walled sphere; conceptually invoked throughout but not numbered in the text):

$$\sigma = \frac{P \, r}{2 h}$$

  Predicts: hypertensive pressure overload (↑P) and dilated ventricle (↑r) both raise wall stress, driving compensatory hypertrophy (↑h) — Box 22-3.

- > "Total external work represents a relatively small fraction (3%) of the total energy needs of the heart at rest, rising to as much as 10% during exercise. The heat developed as part of the tension-time integral remains the major component of the total energy consumption, even during exercise." (p. 521)
- > "Thus, it is advantageous to the patient to have a low wall tension (T)—that is, a low blood pressure. It is also advantageous for the patient not to spend too much time (Δt) in systole." (p. 521)
- > "The ratio of the ventricle's total external work … to the total energy cost … is the heart's mechanical efficiency. Note that the mechanical efficiency has nothing to do with how effective the ventricle is at expelling blood (i.e., ejection fraction)." (p. 522)

## Section 3 — From contractile filaments to a regulated pump (pp. 522–528)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The entry of Ca²⁺ from the outside triggers Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum** (pp. 522–523)
- **A global rise in [Ca²⁺]ᵢ initiates contraction of cardiac myocytes** (p. 523)
- **Phosphorylation of phospholamban and of troponin I speeds cardiac muscle relaxation** (pp. 523–524)
- **The overlap of thick and thin filaments cannot explain the unusual shape of the cardiac length-tension diagram** (pp. 524–525)
- **Starling's law states that a greater fiber length (i.e., greater ventricular volume) causes the heart to deliver more mechanical energy** (pp. 525–526) (N22-10)
- **The velocity of cardiac muscle shortening falls when the contraction occurs against a greater opposing force (or pressure) or at a shorter muscle length (or lower volume)** (pp. 526–528)
- **Increases in heart rate enhance myocardial tension** (p. 528)

### Core claims

#### Cardiac EC coupling — CICR (Fig. 22-11)
Recap of the cellular mechanism developed in Ch 9 Section 5:
1. AP from adjacent myocyte → gap junction → depolarization of target cell → spreads down **radial and axial T-tubules** (the latter unique to cardiac).
2. **Cav1.2 (L-type DHPR)** opens; small trigger Ca²⁺ entry — a **calcium spark** in microdomains of ~1 μm and [Ca²⁺] up to ~10 μM.
3. **CICR**: spark Ca²⁺ activates the apposed cluster of **RYR2** tetramers in the SR membrane via a ~15-nm diffusion gap. Many simultaneously firing sparks summate to a **global cytoplasmic [Ca²⁺] transient** that outlasts the AP.
4. **Atrial myocytes** have poor T-tubule development; subsurface sparks initiate an inward propagating Ca²⁺ wave.
5. **Contraction termination** depends on four Ca²⁺ removal processes (Fig. 22-11, green arrows): (a) sarcolemmal extrusion via **NCX1** + sarcolemmal **PMCA**; (b) SR reuptake via **SERCA2a**; (c) mitochondrial uptake via **MiCa** channels; (d) **Ca²⁺ dissociation from TnC**.

#### Lusitropy — β-adrenergic phosphorylation switches
- **Phospholamban (PLN)** is an integral SR membrane protein; unphosphorylated monomeric PLN tonically inhibits SERCA2a.
- **PKA phosphorylates PLN** (downstream of β1-adrenergic Gs–AC–cAMP) → relieves inhibition → faster Ca²⁺ resequestration → **faster relaxation** (positive lusitropy).
- **PKA also phosphorylates TnI** → reduces TnC's Ca²⁺ affinity → faster Ca²⁺-TnC dissociation → faster relaxation.
- Net β1 effect = positive inotropy (more Ca²⁺ entry + more SR load + larger Ca²⁺ spark) **and** positive lusitropy (faster Ca²⁺ removal).
- **Dephosphorylation by PP1** terminates the inotropic/lusitropic state.
- **PLN knockout mice** have constitutively active SERCA2a and a constitutively rapid relaxation phenotype.

#### Cardiac length-tension diagram (Fig. 22-12A)
- **Passive cardiac muscle (violet curve)** is much **stiffer** than passive skeletal muscle (green curve): passive tension rises steeply above sarcomere length ~1.9 μm and the muscle ruptures at ~2.6 μm (vs. 3.6 μm for skeletal). Titin is the dominant elastic element and is **stiffer in the cardiac isoform**.
- **Active cardiac tension (brown curve)** has a **steep ascending limb** between 1.8 and 2.4 μm — much steeper than the skeletal-muscle active curve (turquoise). The peak is at ~2.4 μm. Filament overlap alone cannot explain this; **two cellular mechanisms** are added on top of the sliding-filament geometry:
  1. **Length-dependent Ca²⁺ sensitivity of the myofilaments.** Stretching reduces lateral interfilament spacing (constant-volume cell) → more frequent cross-bridge formation → **increased TnC Ca²⁺ affinity** → more force at the same [Ca²⁺]ᵢ. Titin strain may also rearrange the thick-filament packing.
  2. **Stretch-activated Ca²⁺ channels** open more → more Ca²⁺ entry → more CICR → larger global [Ca²⁺]ᵢ transient.
- In a healthy ventricle, **sarcomere length never exceeds ~2.2–2.4 μm** in vivo. Therefore the Frank-Starling curve **has no descending limb in clinical practice** — physiological filling sits on the steep ascending limb of the active length-tension diagram.

#### Frank-Starling law (Fig. 22-12B, C)
- **Statement (Starling 1914, isolated heart-lung preparation):** *"The mechanical energy set free on passage from the resting to the contracted state depends on … the length of the fibres."*
- The whole-organ analogue (Fig. 22-12B): plot **systolic pressure** (a proxy for tension) and **diastolic pressure** (a proxy for passive tension) versus **EDV** (a proxy for initial sarcomere length). The systole curve rises with EDV — the Frank-Starling curve.
- The **ventricular performance curve** (Fig. 22-12C) is the clinically obtainable form: **stroke work** ($P \cdot \Delta V$, Newton·m or J) on the y-axis vs. **left-atrial pressure** (proxy for EDV / preload) on the x-axis. Multiple curves exist for the same heart:
  - **Sympathetic stimulation (NE)** steepens the curve and shifts it **up and to the left** — an inotropic shift (more stroke work for any given preload).
  - **Negative inotropic agents** flatten the curve and shift it down.
- Because the in vivo sarcomere length stays on the ascending limb, **performance curves are monotonically rising — no descending limb**.

#### Force-velocity / load-velocity diagram applied to cardiac muscle (Fig. 22-13)
The two determinants of cardiac shortening velocity:
1. **Preload** (initial sarcomere length / EDV) — set before contraction.
2. **Afterload** (the force the contracting muscle must overcome / arterial pressure) — sensed only after contraction begins.

The textbook walks through an isolated-muscle experiment (Fig. 22-13A, B) that maps onto the cardiac cycle as follows:
- **Isometric phase** (Fig. 22-13A, mimics isovolumetric contraction CD on the PV loop): the screw stop holds the muscle at its preload length; the weight rests on the table; the muscle builds tension at constant length.
- **Isotonic phase** (Fig. 22-13B, mimics ejection DEF): once tension equals the weight (afterload), the muscle lifts it and shortens at constant tension; velocity = ΔL/Δt (Fig. 22-13C).
- **Load-velocity curve** (Fig. 22-13E): velocity of shortening is plotted against afterload; the curve is the textbook **Hill hyperbola** carried over from Ch 9. At zero afterload, velocity = $v_{\max}$; at the maximum afterload that allows no shortening at all, velocity = 0 (isometric).
- **Effect of preload on the load-velocity curve:** **a greater preload (red curve) lies above and to the right of a smaller preload (black curve)** — at any given afterload, a more-stretched muscle shortens faster, and against any given velocity it can develop more tension. This is the **cellular content of Starling's law** translated into the force-velocity plane.
- **Effect of inotropy:** positive inotropic agents lift the *entire family* of curves up and to the right.

#### Hill equation (Ch 9 Eq. 9-12, recalled here)

$$(P + a)(v + b) = (P_0 + a) \cdot b$$

with $P$ = afterload, $v$ = shortening velocity, $P_0$ = isometric tension ($v = 0$), and $a$, $b$ Hill constants (Hill linked them thermodynamically to heat of shortening). Maximum power output occurs at $P \approx 0.3 \, P_0$. In cardiac muscle, **$P_0$ rises and the entire curve scales up** with preload (length-dependent activation) and with inotropy ([Ca²⁺]ᵢ).

#### The Bowditch staircase (positive force-frequency relationship)
- Stimulating heart muscle at higher rates **increases peak tension** — the **positive staircase phenomenon** (Bowditch 1871).
- Mechanism: more APs per minute → more total Ca²⁺ entry per minute through Cav1.2 → more Ca²⁺-loaded SR. Three sub-pieces:
  1. Each plateau opens Cav1.2 (more APs → more aggregate plateau time).
  2. Each plateau biases **NCX1 into reverse mode** (Ca²⁺ in, Na⁺ out), augmenting the entry; faster pacing means $[\text{Na}^+]_i$ accumulates, which **further** reverses NCX.
  3. Rising $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i$ activates **CaMKII**, which phosphorylates PLN → SERCA2a runs faster → more Ca²⁺ stored in the SR for the next release.
- The positive staircase is the **intrinsic** inotropic response of heart muscle to tachycardia; in failing myocardium this relationship can flatten or invert.

### Equations

- **Hill force-velocity** (carried from Ch 9, applied to cardiac afterloaded shortening):

$$(P + a)(v + b) = (P_0 + a) \cdot b$$

- **Cardiac power output**:

$$\text{Power} = P \cdot v, \qquad \text{Power}_{\max} \text{ at } P \approx 0.3 \, P_0$$

- **Cellular Frank-Starling sensitivity (length-dependent activation, conceptual)**:

$$\sigma_{\text{active}}(L) = \sigma_{\max}(L) \cdot \frac{[\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i^n}{K(L)^n + [\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i^n}$$

  where the Hill coefficient $n$ and the half-activation [Ca²⁺] $K(L)$ both **depend on sarcomere length** — the molecular content of the steep ascending Frank-Starling limb.

- **Stroke work** (the y-axis of the ventricular performance curve):

$$W_{\text{stroke}} = \int P \, dV \approx \bar P_{\text{ejection}} \cdot \text{SV}$$

## Section 4 — Contractility as an intrinsic measure (pp. 528–532)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Contractility is an intrinsic measure of cardiac performance** (pp. 528–529) (N22-8, N22-9)
- **Positive inotropic agents increase myocardial contractility by raising [Ca²⁺]ᵢ** (pp. 530)
- (Negative inotropic agents — closing subsection)

### Core claims

#### Why we need a load-independent contractility index
- EF alone is **not** a load-independent measure: it depends on EDV (preload, Starling) and aortic pressure (afterload).
- Two bedside proxies that correlate better:
  - **dP/dt during isovolumetric contraction** — the peak rate of LV pressure rise; correlates with the velocity of shortening (Fig. 22-13E, F).
  - **Outflow velocity in the aortic root** — Doppler echocardiography (Ch 17).
- The cleanest descriptor uses the **end-systolic pressure-volume relationship (ESPVR)** — the locus of end-systolic (V, P) points obtained by varying preload and afterload while keeping contractility constant.

#### Construction of the ESPVR (Fig. 22-14A)
- For a given EDV, the end-systolic corner of the PV loop sits at point **F** (~50 mL, ~100 mm Hg).
- Reduce EDV → smaller loop (orange in Fig. 22-14A) with end-systolic corner **F′** down-and-left.
- Increase EDV → larger loop (green) with end-systolic corner **F″** up-and-right.
- The **F-F′-F″ line is the ESPVR** — approximately linear, passing near (V₀, 0) where V₀ ≈ 5–15 mL.
- A theoretical asymptote: if the aortic valve were prevented from opening, the LV would generate its maximum isovolumetric pressure at the same EDV — points **G, G′, G″** lie above F, F′, F″ along a slightly steeper line; conceptually equivalent to the isometric Frank-Starling curve (brown curve in Fig. 22-12A).
- **The slope of the ESPVR (= end-systolic elastance $E_{\text{max}}$) is the load-independent contractility index.** Steeper line ⇒ greater contractility.

#### Effects of changing contractility, preload, afterload on the PV loop (Fig. 22-14B, C, D)

| Perturbation | Geometric effect on the loop | Effect on SV / EF |
|---|---|---|
| ↑ Contractility (positive inotropy, Fig. 22-14B) | ESPVR slope rises (gold → red dashed line); end-systolic corner slides up the steeper line so ESV decreases | SV ↑, EF ↑ |
| ↑ Preload (Frank-Starling, Fig. 22-14C) | Loop's right edge moves rightward to higher EDV; ESPVR slope unchanged so end-systolic point slides up the same line | SV ↑, EF ≈ same |
| ↑ Afterload (Fig. 22-14D) | Top of loop rises (aortic-valve opens at higher P); loop becomes taller and narrower; end-systolic point slides up the same ESPVR | SV ↓, EF ↓ |

Each effect is **separable on the loop diagram**, which is what gives the loop its central clinical / teaching utility.

#### Positive inotropic agents (Box 22-3 setup; p. 530)
All act by **raising $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i$**.
1. **β1-adrenergic agonists** (epinephrine, norepinephrine, dobutamine). Gs → AC → cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates Cav1.2 (more Ca²⁺ entry), PLN (faster SR uptake → larger SR store), TnI (faster Ca²⁺-TnC dissociation), and the L-type channel directly. The α₁ subunit of the heterotrimeric protein may also gate the L-type channel directly.
2. **Cardiac glycosides** (digitalis / digoxin). Inhibit Na/K-ATPase → raise $[\text{Na}^+]_i$ → slow NCX1 → raise $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i$. Recently proposed alternative: increased Ca²⁺ permeability of plasma-membrane Na channels.
3. **High extracellular [Ca²⁺]** — slows NCX (less driving force for Ca²⁺ extrusion) and increases Ca²⁺ entry through L-type channels.
4. **Low extracellular [Na⁺]** — same effect on NCX1, raising $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i$.
5. **Increased heart rate (Bowditch staircase).**

#### Negative inotropic agents (p. 532)
All act by **lowering $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i$**.
1. **L-type Ca²⁺-channel blockers** (verapamil, diltiazem, nifedipine).
2. **Low extracellular [Ca²⁺]**.
3. **High extracellular [Na⁺]** — accelerates Ca²⁺ extrusion via NCX1.

### Equations

- **End-systolic elastance** (slope of ESPVR; load-independent contractility):

$$E_{\text{max}} = \frac{P_{\text{ES}}}{V_{\text{ES}} - V_0}$$

  with $V_0$ the (small) extrapolated dead volume at zero end-systolic pressure.

- **End-diastolic compliance** (inverse-slope of EDPVR):

$$C_{\text{ED}} = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta P_{\text{ED}}}$$

- **Stroke volume as the width of the loop bounded by EDPVR and ESPVR** (geometric identity):

$$\text{SV} = V_{\text{ED}}(P_{\text{ED}}) - V_{\text{ES}}(P_{\text{aortic}})$$

  i.e., SV is determined jointly by (i) where EDPVR meets the right edge (preload-side, set by venous return + ventricular compliance), (ii) where ESPVR meets the top (afterload-side, set by aortic pressure), and (iii) the slope of ESPVR (contractility).

- > "Contractility is a somewhat vague but clinically useful term that distinguishes a better-performing heart from a poorly performing one." (p. 528)
- > "Two somewhat better gauges of contractility are the rate of pressure development during ejection (dP/dt) and the velocity of ejection. Both correlate well with the velocity of shortening … and they are very sensitive guides to the effect of inotropic interventions." (p. 528)
- > "The corner points of many such pressure-volume loops fall along a line—the end-systolic pressure-volume relation (ESPVR)." (p. 528)

### Box 22-3 — Cardiac Hypertrophy (pp. 530–531)
- **Volume overload (↑EDV; e.g., large AV shunt)** → both ventricles see increased preload → **eccentric hypertrophy** (myocyte length grows out of proportion to width).
- **Pressure overload (↑afterload; e.g., systemic or pulmonary hypertension)** → **concentric hypertrophy** (myocyte width > length growth) — thickens the wall to normalize wall stress (Laplace).
- **Athlete's heart** = **physiological hypertrophy**: proportional length and width increase; reversible with detraining.
- Adult cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated; chronic load → cellular hypertrophy, not hyperplasia.
- **Triggers** include: catecholamines, ANG II, endothelin-1, IGF-2, TGF-β, IL-1 → MAPK cascade → transcription via GATA-4, SRF, Sp1, TEF-1. **Mechanical stretch** acts via muscle-LIM-protein (MLP) → Raf-1 → ERK/SAPK → AP-1.
- **Ca²⁺-calcineurin-NFAT3** axis: elevated $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i$ dephosphorylates NFAT3 → nuclear translocation → binds GATA-4 → hypertrophic gene program. Constitutively active calcineurin in mice → hypertrophy + heart failure.
- Hypertrophic myocytes downregulate the SR-membrane proteins **Ca²⁺-release channel, PLN, and SERCA2**, and switch myosin-heavy-chain isoforms — explaining their reduced cross-sectional-area-normalized contractility.

### Box 22-4 — Cellular Basis of Heart Failure (p. 531)
- Heart failure = inability to sustain adequate CO; clinically appears as **pulmonary congestion (LV failure)** and **peripheral edema (RV failure / biventricular failure)**.
- Multiple cellular contributors:
  - **Impaired Cav1.2-RYR2 coupling** in hypertrophic cells (altered T-tubule architecture, lattice spacing).
  - **Leaky RYR2** due to depletion of the regulatory protein **calstabin 2 (FKBP12.6)** → diastolic Ca²⁺ leak → delayed afterdepolarizations → ventricular arrhythmias → **sudden cardiac death**.
  - **Myosin-heavy-chain isoform switch**: αMHC (fast) drops from ~35% to ~2% of total MHC in failing human heart; the slower βMHC dominates.
- **MLP-knockout mouse** = animal model of dilated cardiomyopathy with disrupted cytoskeletal architecture.

> Vision note: This is the figure students draw on the back of every clinical cardiology exam paper. The clean orthogonality — contractility = slope of ESPVR; preload = right-edge x-coordinate; afterload = top-edge y-coordinate; SV = loop width — is the chapter's central conceptual payoff and the entry point to Ch 23 (Guyton-Starling intersection diagrams).
