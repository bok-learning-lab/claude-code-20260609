## Section 1 — Neuronal synapses (pp. 307–314)

### Subsection headings
- **The molecular mechanisms of neuronal synapses are similar but not identical to those of the neuromuscular junction** (pp. 307–309)
- **Presynaptic terminals may contact neurons at the dendrite, soma, or axon and may contain both clear vesicles and dense-core granules** (pp. 309–310)
- **The postsynaptic membrane contains transmitter receptors and numerous proteins clustered in the postsynaptic density** (pp. 310–311)
- **Some transmitters are used by diffusely distributed systems of neurons to modulate the general excitability of the brain** (pp. 311–314)
- **Electrical synapses serve specialized functions in the mammalian nervous system** (p. 314)

### Core claims — the seven-step cycle (shared with Ch 8 neuromuscular junction)

| Step | Event |
|---|---|
| 1 | Transmitter packaged into vesicles; vesicles docked at active zones. |
| 2 | Presynaptic depolarization (usually AP; some synapses are graded). |
| 3 | Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters terminal. |
| 4 | Rise in [Ca²⁺]ᵢ triggers SNARE-mediated fusion; release rate jumps ~10⁵-fold. **Synaptotagmin** is the Ca²⁺ sensor; individual fusion completes in a fraction of a millisecond. |
| 5 | Transmitter is released in **quanta** into the cleft; diffuses passively. |
| 6 | Transmitter binds postsynaptic receptors (ligand-gated channel or GPCR). |
| 7 | Transmitter cleared by diffusion, enzymatic degradation, or active reuptake; vesicle membrane endocytosed. |

### Core claims — structural and modulatory anatomy
- **Clear vesicles** (40–50 nm) carry small-molecule transmitters and dock at **active zones** marked by a presynaptic grid; CNS synapses have only 1–20 active zones vs hundreds at NMJ.
- **Dense-core granules** (100–200 nm) carry neuropeptides, are distributed throughout the cytoplasm, release away from active zones, and require higher [Ca²⁺]ᵢ.
- **Postsynaptic density (PSD)** clusters >30 proteins: receptors, scaffolds (PSD-95 etc.), kinases (CaMKII), endocytic and glycolytic machinery.
- **Spines** carry >90% of CNS excitatory synapses; high spine-neck resistance can amplify EPSP locally and chemically isolate Ca²⁺ — relevant for compartmentalized LTP.
- **Synapse-naming convention**: axodendritic, axosomatic, axoaxonic, axospinous; dendrodendritic, somatosomatic also exist.
- **Modulatory diffuse systems** — small cores of brainstem neurons project widely via meandering axons:
  - **Norepinephrine** — locus coeruleus (~12,000 neurons each side) → essentially all of cortex, hippocampus, cerebellum, thalamus, hypothalamus, spinal cord; one cell can make >250,000 synapses; controls arousal, attention, sleep-wake.
  - **Serotonin (5-HT)** — nine raphé nuclei → most of CNS; sleep-wake, mood; LSD acts here; SSRIs (fluoxetine) prolong action.
  - **Dopamine** — substantia nigra → striatum (motor; loss = Parkinson disease); ventral tegmental area → prefrontal cortex + limbic (reward, addiction, schizophrenia; neuroleptics block D2).
  - **Acetylcholine** — basal forebrain complex (septal + nucleus basalis → hippocampus, neocortex) and pontomesencephalotegmental complex → thalamus. Arousal, sleep-wake, learning.
  - **Histamine** — tuberomammillary nucleus (introduced here).

### Core claims — electrical synapses
- Gap-junction-coupled pairs (most via **Cx36** in CNS neurons) are fast, bidirectional, metabolically cheap, but cannot amplify, are inherently neither excitatory nor inhibitory by themselves, and have very limited time-domain flexibility.
- Specialized roles: synchronization of inhibitory cortical and thalamic interneurons; rapid escape circuits; retinal photoreceptor and glial syncytia.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Synaptic transmission at chemical synapses occurs in seven steps." (p. 307)
- > "Chemical synapses use diffusible transmitter molecules to communicate messages between two cells." (p. 307)
- > "Most synapses in the central nervous system (CNS) have relatively few active zones, often only 1 but occasionally as many as 10 or 20." (p. 310)
- > "In >90% of all excitatory synapses in the CNS, the postsynaptic site is a dendritic spine." (p. 310)
- > "Each of these modulatory systems uses a different neurotransmitter, and the axons of each make widely dispersed, diffuse, almost meandering synaptic connections to carry a simple message to vast regions of the brain." (p. 311)
- > "Electrical synapses would seem to have many advantages over chemical synapses: they are extremely fast … highly reliable, and they can be bidirectional." (p. 314)
- > "Chemical synapses are champions of plasticity; their strength can be a strong function of recent neural activity." (p. 314)

## Section 2 — Neurotransmitter systems of the brain (pp. 314–322)

### Subsection headings
- **Most of the brain's transmitters are common biochemicals** (pp. 315–318)
- **Synaptic transmitters can stimulate, inhibit, or modulate the postsynaptic neuron** (pp. 318–320)
- **G proteins may affect ion channels directly, or indirectly through second messengers** (pp. 320–321)
- **Signaling cascades allow amplification, regulation, and a long duration of transmitter responses** (pp. 321–322)
- **Neurotransmitters may have both convergent and divergent effects** (p. 322)

### Core claims — transmitter chemistry

| Class | Members | Synthesis substrate |
|---|---|---|
| **Excitatory amino acids** | glutamate, aspartate | TCA cycle (α-KG → Glu via aminotransferase) |
| **Inhibitory amino acids** | GABA, glycine | Glu → GABA via GAD; serine → glycine |
| **Monoamines** | ACh, serotonin, histamine | choline + acetyl-CoA → ACh; Trp → 5-HT; His → histamine |
| **Catecholamines** | dopamine, NE, epinephrine | Tyr → L-DOPA → DA → NE → Epi |
| **Purines** | ATP, adenosine | metabolic |
| **Neuropeptides** | enkephalin, substance P, CCK, VIP, somatostatin, TRH, NPY, oxytocin, vasopressin, β-endorphin, ACTH, GnRH, NT, GRP, angiotensin II | prepro-hormone cleavage in dense-core granules |
| **Gasotransmitters** | NO (from L-Arg via NOS), CO, possibly H₂S | enzymatic; not vesicular |
| **Endocannabinoids** | anandamide, 2-arachidonoyl glycerol | from arachidonic acid; retrograde messengers |

- **Colocalization (Table 13-1)**: most CNS terminals contain a small transmitter *plus* one or more neuropeptides. Examples include ACh + VIP, DA + CCK, GABA + somatostatin or NPY, 5-HT + substance P + TRH, NE + enkephalin + NPY. Both vesicle types also contain ATP. Release ratio is frequency-dependent (see Fig. 13-18).
- **NO** is membrane-permeable, requires no vesicle or [Ca²⁺]ᵢ for release (only for synthesis), acts as a **retrograde messenger** from post- to presynaptic structures and can diffuse across multiple cells. Activates soluble guanylyl cyclase → cGMP.
- **Endocannabinoids** are also retrograde messengers (postsynaptic → presynaptic CB1 receptors); they suppress further transmitter release from GABAergic or glutamatergic terminals. THC mimics endocannabinoids at CB1 — the brain has more cannabinoid receptors than any other GPCR type.

### Core claims — three synapse functional classes
- **Excitatory** — opens cation-permeable channels (e.g. AMPA, NMDA) with reversal potential ~0 mV; depolarizes postsynaptic cell toward AP threshold.
- **Inhibitory** — opens anion channels (Cl⁻, e.g. GABA_A, glycine receptor) with reversal at E_Cl (around −71 mV); hyperpolarizes or shunts.
- **Modulatory** — typically GPCR-mediated; little effect at rest but powerfully modifies the response of the cell to other inputs (canonical example: NE acting on β-adrenergic receptors → cAMP → PKA → suppress K⁺ channels → reduce spike-frequency adaptation in hippocampal pyramidals; see also Ch 12 §5).

### Core claims — G-protein cascade architecture
- **Membrane-delimited pathway** (fast, 30–100 ms, localized): receptor → G protein → ion channel directly (canonical example: cardiac M₂ muscarinic ACh receptor → Gᵢβγ → GIRK K⁺ channel opens).
- **Second-messenger cascades**: AC/cAMP/PKA, PLC/IP₃+DAG/PKC, PLA₂/AA pathways; each can amplify, integrate, and persist over seconds–minutes.
- **Amplification math (Fig. 13-13)**: one receptor activates 10–20 G proteins; each Gα activates one AC; one AC makes many cAMP; each PKA phosphorylates many channels — total gain ~10³–10⁶.
- **Divergence and convergence (Fig. 13-14)**: one transmitter (NE) can target multiple receptor classes (α₁, α₂, β₁, β₂, β₃) → multiple second messengers → multiple channels (suppress I_AHP, suppress I_K-leak, enhance I_h, enhance I_Ca). Multiple transmitters (GABA_B, 5-HT_1A, A₁, SSTR, μ-opioid) converge on the same GIRK K⁺ channel via Gᵢβγ.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The most ubiquitous [transmitters] are amino acids: glutamate and aspartate excite, whereas gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and glycine inhibit." (p. 308)
- > "The molecular mechanisms of neuromodulators are complex and diverse, but all begin with a GPCR that activates an intracellular signal cascade." (p. 320)
- > "The membrane-delimited pathway is … relatively fast, beginning within 30 to 100 ms — not quite as fast as a ligand-gated channel … but faster than the many-messenger cascades." (p. 320)
- > "When activated, one ligand-gated channel is just that: one ion channel in one place. However, when activated, one GPCR potentially influences many channels." (pp. 321–322)
- > "Divergence is a rule among neurotransmitters. Nearly every well-studied transmitter can activate multiple receptor subtypes." (p. 322)
- > "Convergence … means that multiple transmitters, each activating its own receptor type, converge on a single type of ion channel in a single cell." (p. 322)

## Section 3 — Fast amino-acid-mediated synapses (pp. 322–328)

### Subsection headings
- **Most EPSPs in the brain are mediated by two types of glutamate-gated channels** (pp. 323–325)
- **Most IPSPs in the brain are mediated by the GABA_A receptor, which is activated by several classes of drugs** (pp. 325–326)
- **The ionotropic receptors for ACh, serotonin, GABA, and glycine belong to the superfamily of ligand-gated/pentameric channels** (pp. 326–327)
- **Most neuronal synapses release a very small number of transmitter quanta with each action potential** (pp. 327–328)
- **When multiple transmitters colocalize to the same synapse, the exocytosis of large vesicles requires high-frequency stimulation** (pp. 328)

### Core claims — glutamate receptors

| Family | Members | Type | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **mGluR group I** | mGluR1, mGluR5 | Gq → PLC → IP₃/DAG → PKC | postsynaptic; raises [Ca²⁺]ᵢ; involved in LTD induction |
| **mGluR group II** | mGluR2, mGluR3 | Gi/o → ↓AC → ↓cAMP | mainly presynaptic autoreceptors |
| **mGluR group III** | mGluR4, mGluR6–8 | Gi/o → ↓AC → ↓cAMP | presynaptic; mGluR6 in retinal ON-bipolar cells |
| **AMPA** (GluA1–4) | heterotetramers | ionotropic; cation; Na⁺/K⁺ ≈ equal; Ca²⁺ low unless GluA2 absent | fast EPSP (~1–10 ms); ~15 pS |
| **Kainate** (GluK1–5) | heterotetramers | ionotropic; Na⁺/K⁺; some Ca²⁺ | EPSP minority; presynaptic on GABAergic/glutamatergic terminals |
| **NMDA** (GluN1, GluN2A–D, GluN3A/B) | obligate GluN1 + GluN2 | ionotropic; Na⁺, K⁺, *high Ca²⁺*; voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block; needs glycine/D-serine co-agonist | slow EPSP (~50–200 ms); ~50 pS; coincidence detector |

- **AMPA + NMDA at the same synapse**: AMPA fires fast EPSP at any V_m; NMDA only opens if V_m has already been depolarized (Mg²⁺ ejected). Therefore the NMDA receptor is the **postsynaptic AND gate** — requires presynaptic glutamate release AND postsynaptic depolarization.
- **Pharmacology**:
  - **CNQX** blocks AMPA + kainate (not NMDA).
  - **GYKI 53655** (2,3-benzodiazepine) selectively blocks AMPA.
  - **APV (2-amino-5-phosphonovaleric acid)** selectively blocks NMDA.
  - **Ketamine, MK-801, PCP** are open-channel NMDA blockers (Mg²⁺-binding site analogs); ketamine is a clinically used dissociative anesthetic and rapid-acting antidepressant.
  - **Domoate** (kainate agonist) is the marine toxin behind amnesic shellfish poisoning.
- **Structure**: each ionotropic glutamate subunit has a large extracellular glutamate-binding domain, three transmembrane segments, and one re-entrant cytoplasmic loop (M2-like) lining the pore; **four subunits per channel** (tetramer) — distinct from the Cys-loop/pentameric superfamily below.

### Core claims — GABA and glycine inhibition
- **GABA_A** is an ionotropic Cl⁻-selective **pentamer** (typical stoichiometry 2α + 2β + 1γ). The two GABA-binding sites lie at α/β interfaces.
- **Allosteric modulator sites on GABA_A**:
  - **Benzodiazepines** (diazepam/Valium, lorazepam, midazolam) bind α/γ interface → ↑ frequency of channel opening when GABA is bound.
  - **Barbiturates** (pentobarbital, phenobarbital) → ↑ mean open duration when GABA is bound; at high concentrations can open channel without GABA → respiratory depression.
  - **Neurosteroids** — progesterone/cortisol metabolites (e.g., allopregnanolone) act like barbiturates; mechanism of premenstrual mood shifts and brexanolone for postpartum depression.
  - **Ethanol** potentiates GABA_A at some subunit compositions.
  - **General anesthetics** (etomidate, propofol, volatiles) potentiate GABA_A at distinct sites.
  - **Picrotoxin** is an open-channel blocker at the central Cl⁻ pore.
- **GABA_B** is a **metabotropic** (GPCR) receptor → Gᵢ/o → ↑K⁺ (slow IPSP) and ↓Ca²⁺ (presynaptic autoinhibition). **Baclofen** is the prototype agonist (antispasticity).
- **GABA_C** is a separate ionotropic Cl⁻ channel composed of ρ subunits, predominantly retinal.
- **Glycine receptor** is also an ionotropic pentameric Cl⁻ channel; predominant inhibitory transmitter in spinal cord and brainstem. **Strychnine** is the selective antagonist (convulsant).

### Core claims — ligand-gated pentameric Cys-loop superfamily
- All share: 5 subunits × 4 transmembrane segments per subunit; M2 segment from each subunit lines the central pore; the ring of M2 polarities (T/S near the cytoplasmic mouth; charged residues at the extracellular and cytoplasmic vestibules) sets selectivity:
  - **Anion-selective** (Cl⁻): GABA_A, GABA_C, glycine, invertebrate ACh-gated Cl⁻.
  - **Cation-selective**: nicotinic ACh receptor (Ch 8), 5-HT₃ (clinically blocked by ondansetron for chemotherapy-induced nausea).
- **ATP-gated P2X channels** are *not* in this superfamily: each subunit has only 2 TM segments and the channel is a **trimer** — structurally akin to the voltage-gated Na⁺/K⁺ family by convergent evolution.

### Core claims — quantal release at central synapses
- Quantal-release equation (Katz):

  $$m = n \cdot p \tag{13-1}$$

  with $m$ = mean quanta per AP, $n$ = number of releasable quanta (~number of active zones), $p$ = release probability per active zone.
- At a typical CNS glutamatergic bouton: 1 active zone, single-quantum EPSP amplitude 10–1000 μV (vs ~40 mV at NMJ); $p$ often < 1 (so many APs evoke *zero* release); ~10–40 receptors open per quantum.
- **It takes summation of 10–100 simultaneous EPSPs** from cooperating synapses to drive a postsynaptic cell to threshold — physiological basis for cortical population coding.
- **Exception**: cerebellar climbing-fiber → Purkinje synapse makes ~200 synaptic contacts per pair, generating a ~40 mV EPSP that is essentially fail-safe and evokes a complex spike — the strongest CNS chemical synapse.

### Core claims — frequency-dependent colocalized release (Fig. 13-18)
- **Low-frequency presynaptic activity** → highly localized [Ca²⁺]ᵢ at the active zone → only small clear vesicles fuse → only the small transmitter is released.
- **High-frequency burst** → wider [Ca²⁺]ᵢ elevation throughout the terminal → distant dense-core granules also fuse → co-release of neuropeptides (e.g. NE + NPY in sympathetic terminals; ACh + VIP in salivary parasympathetic terminals).
- This is why peptide co-transmitters are released selectively during salient/intense input — they signal the "magnitude" or "salience" of activation, not its mere occurrence.

### Equations

- Quantal release: $m = n p$ (Eq. 13-1, p. 327).
- Reversal potential at a non-selective cation channel (AMPA/NMDA):

  $$E_{\text{cation}} = \frac{RT}{F}\,\ln\frac{P_K[\mathrm{K^+}]_o + P_{Na}[\mathrm{Na^+}]_o}{P_K[\mathrm{K^+}]_i + P_{Na}[\mathrm{Na^+}]_i} \;\approx\; 0\,\text{mV}$$

  for $P_K \approx P_{Na}$ (GHK reduced; Ch 5 §3).

- Voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block of NMDAR (Woodhull-type):

  $$g_{\text{NMDA}}(V_m) = g_{\text{max}} \cdot \dfrac{1}{1 + [\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}]_o \, e^{-V_m / V_0}}$$

  with $V_0 \approx 17\,\text{mV}$ at physiological $[\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}]_o \approx 1\,\text{mM}$. The exponential voltage dependence is the algebraic root of NMDAR coincidence detection.

- Reversal potential at GABA_A / glycine receptor: $E_{\text{IPSP}} = E_{\text{Cl}} \approx -71\,\text{mV}$.

## Section 4 — Plasticity of central synapses (pp. 328–333)

### Subsection headings
- **Use-dependent changes in synaptic strength underlie many forms of learning** (pp. 328–329)
- **Short-term synaptic plasticity usually reflects presynaptic changes** (p. 329)
- **Long-term potentiation in the hippocampus may last for days or weeks** (pp. 329–331)
- **Long-term depression exists in multiple forms** (pp. 331–332)
- **Long-term depression in the cerebellum may be important for motor learning** (pp. 332–333)

### Core claims — short-term plasticity (Fig. 13-19, *Hz–min*)

| Phenomenon | Direction | Time course | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Facilitation** | ↑ | tens–hundreds ms | residual presynaptic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ saturating buffers |
| **Augmentation** | ↑ | seconds | as above + PKC, CaMK |
| **Post-tetanic potentiation (PTP)** | ↑ | tens of s to min | continued residual [Ca²⁺]ᵢ + presynaptic protein phosphorylation |
| **Depression** | ↓ | during high-freq train | vesicle-pool depletion; Ca²⁺-channel inactivation |
| **Habituation** | ↓ | slow with low-freq | reduced p (Aplysia gill-withdrawal; Castellucci & Kandel) |

- Quantal analysis at these synapses consistently identifies presynaptic mechanisms (Δm via Δp), not postsynaptic ones.
- **Synaptotagmin** is the fast Ca²⁺ sensor for vesicle fusion; PKC and CaMK regulate slower presynaptic enhancement.

### Core claims — long-term potentiation (LTP) at Schaffer-collateral → CA1 synapses

- **Discovery**: Bliss & Lømø, 1973, dentate gyrus.
- **Three induction protocols all converge on the same molecular trigger**:
  - High-frequency tetanus (50–100 Hz × 1 s) to the presynaptic axons.
  - Low-frequency presynaptic stimulation paired with strong postsynaptic depolarization (Fig. 13-20).
  - Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP): presynaptic AP arriving ≤10–20 ms before a postsynaptic AP → LTP; reverse order → LTD. STDP is the Hebbian rule made temporally precise.
- **Three induction properties**:
  - **Input specificity** — only the activated set of synapses on a CA1 cell is potentiated.
  - **Cooperativity** — multiple weak inputs must fire together to depolarize the cell enough to relieve Mg²⁺-block of NMDARs.
  - **Associativity** — a weak input paired with a strong (cooperatively-driven) input gets potentiated even though by itself it would not — the cellular basis for "neurons that fire together wire together" and for associative learning.
- **Induction mechanism**: glutamate activates AMPA (depolarization) + NMDA (Ca²⁺ influx once Mg²⁺ leaves the pore). Postsynaptic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ must rise above a critical threshold for ~1–2 s; if it does, **CaMKII** autophosphorylates, becoming persistently active, and phosphorylates AMPA-receptor GluA1-S831 (↑ conductance) and other targets. Other implicated kinases: PKC, PKA, MAPK, Src tyrosine kinase.
- **Expression mechanism** (NMDAR-dependent CA1 LTP): postsynaptic — recruitment of new AMPA receptors into the spine via exocytosis and lateral diffusion from extrasynaptic pools, increasing the slope and amplitude of the AMPA EPSC. The phenomenon of **silent synapses** (synapses with NMDA receptors but no functional AMPA receptors at rest) → unsilencing by AMPAR insertion is a classical correlate.
- **Maintenance** (hours → days): protein synthesis dependent (anisomycin blocks late-phase LTP); requires cAMP/PKA → CREB → transcription of new synaptic proteins; structural growth/enlargement of spines.
- **Presynaptic component**: some forms of LTP also enhance release probability; this requires a retrograde messenger (NO, endocannabinoids, BDNF, or arachidonate metabolites have all been proposed) carrying the signal from spine to bouton.

### Core claims — long-term depression (LTD)

- **Hippocampal NMDAR-dependent LTD** (CA1): paradoxically requires NMDAR activation and postsynaptic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ rise — but to a *lower* level than LTP. The decision is governed by the **Bienenstock–Cooper–Munro (BCM) sliding-threshold rule**: low postsynaptic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ preferentially activates **protein phosphatases (calcineurin → PP1)** → dephosphorylation of AMPAR and accessory proteins → AMPAR internalization (endocytosis from PSD) → reduced EPSP. High [Ca²⁺]ᵢ flips the balance to kinases → LTP.
- **mGluR-LTD** (group I mGluR via PLC/PKC) at the same CA1 synapses uses a different molecular path and is required for AMPAR endocytosis triggered by low-frequency mGluR activation.
- **Endocannabinoid-LTD** at inhibitory and some excitatory synapses: postsynaptic depolarization → endocannabinoid synthesis → retrograde CB1 activation → presynaptic ↓ release. Called depolarization-induced suppression of inhibition (DSI) / excitation (DSE).
- **Cerebellar LTD** at parallel-fiber → Purkinje-cell synapses (Marr–Albus–Ito theory of motor learning):
  - Induced by coactivation of parallel fibers (weak glutamate input via AMPA + mGluR1) with the climbing fiber (massive complex spike opening Cav2.1 / P/Q-type Ca²⁺ channels in dendrites).
  - The conjunction raises postsynaptic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ + activates PKC via mGluR1/Gq/PLC; PKC phosphorylates AMPAR (GluA2) → internalization.
  - No NMDARs in mature Purkinje cells — Ca²⁺ comes from voltage-gated channels and IP₃-evoked release.
  - Cerebellar LTD is the cellular hypothesis for vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) gain adaptation and conditioned eyeblink learning.

### Core claims — the threshold model (Fig. 13-21)

- Postsynaptic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ is the master switch:
  - **Modest [Ca²⁺]ᵢ** rise (low-freq stimulation) → calcineurin/PP1 dominant → AMPAR dephosphorylated + internalized → **LTD**.
  - **High [Ca²⁺]ᵢ** rise (high-freq tetanus or strong pairing) → CaMKII / PKC / PKA dominant → AMPAR phosphorylated + inserted → **LTP**.
- Same synapse can move bidirectionally (depotentiation, de-depression), consistent with reversible memory engrams.

### Equations and learning rules

- **Hebbian/coincidence rule (NMDAR AND-gate)**: postsynaptic learning signal ∝ [glutamate]_cleft × ${[Ca^{2+}]_{NMDA-mediated}}$, with NMDAR conductance gated by both:

  $$\Delta w_{ij} \propto \int g_{\text{AMPA}}(t)\,[\text{glutamate}]_j(t) \cdot \frac{1}{1 + [\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}]_o e^{-V_i(t)/V_0}}\, dt$$

- **BCM sliding-threshold model**:

  $$\Delta w \;\propto\; w \cdot \phi([\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i,\, \theta_M), \qquad \phi(c, \theta_M) > 0 \text{ if } c > \theta_M;\ \phi(c, \theta_M) < 0 \text{ if } c < \theta_M$$

  with $\theta_M$ itself a slow function of recent postsynaptic activity (the metaplasticity term).

- **Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)** window (Bi & Poo, 1998):

  $$\Delta w(\Delta t) = \begin{cases} A_+ \, e^{-\Delta t / \tau_+} & \Delta t = t_{\text{post}} - t_{\text{pre}} > 0 \\ -A_- \, e^{+\Delta t / \tau_-} & \Delta t < 0 \end{cases}$$

  with $\tau_+, \tau_- \approx 10$–$20\,\text{ms}$.

## Equations summary

| Quantity | Equation | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Quantal release | $m = n\,p$ | mean transmitter release per AP |
| EPSP reversal | $E_{\text{EPSP}} \approx 0\,\text{mV}$ | non-selective cation channel (AMPA, nicotinic) |
| IPSP reversal | $E_{\text{IPSP}} = E_{\text{Cl}} \approx -71\,\text{mV}$ | GABA_A, glycine |
| NMDAR Mg²⁺ block | $g_{\text{NMDA}}(V_m) \propto 1/(1 + [\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}]_o e^{-V_m/V_0})$ | coincidence detection |
| LTP/LTD threshold | $\text{sign}(\Delta w) = \text{sign}([\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i - \theta_M)$ | BCM rule |
| Hebbian product | $\Delta w \propto \text{pre} \times \text{post}$ | associative learning |
| STDP window | $\Delta w(\Delta t) = \pm A_\pm e^{-|\Delta t|/\tau_\pm}$ | spike-timing rule, $\tau \sim 20$ ms |

Typical numbers: CNS single-quantum EPSP 10–1000 μV; AMPA channel γ ≈ 15 pS; NMDA channel γ ≈ 50 pS; NMDA decay τ ≈ 50–200 ms; AMPA decay τ ≈ 1–10 ms; quantal content $m \lesssim 1$ at many central synapses; LTP induction Ca²⁺-window ≥ 1–2 s; LTP duration ≥ hours–weeks in vivo.
