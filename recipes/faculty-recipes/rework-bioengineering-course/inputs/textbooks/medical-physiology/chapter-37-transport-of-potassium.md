# Chapter 37 — Transport of Potassium

> Section VI · The Urinary System · pp. 792–805 · Authors: Gerhard Giebisch, Erich E. Windhager, Peter S. Aronson

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Potassium balance and overall renal handling** (pp. 792–795) — internal vs. external balance; ICF/ECF distribution; insulin, catecholamines, aldosterone, acid-base, and osmolality shifting K⁺ in and out of cells; the kidney as the dominant excretory route.
2. **K⁺ transport by different nephron segments** (pp. 795–798) — PT and loop reabsorb ~90%; the distal nephron (DCT, CNT, ICT, CCT, MCD) is the regulated site that reabsorbs *or* secretes; medullary K⁺ recycling and trapping.
3. **K⁺ transport at the cellular and molecular level** (pp. 798–800) — paracellular PT reabsorption; transcellular NKCC2-based TAL reabsorption; principal-cell secretion (basolateral Na-K pump → apical ROMK/BK); α-intercalated-cell reabsorption via H-K pump.
4. **Regulation of renal K⁺ excretion** (pp. 800–805) — distal flow, luminal Na⁺ and voltage, aldosterone (and the aldosterone paradox / escape), glucocorticoids, dietary K⁺ load and adaptation, acid-base, epinephrine, AVP; opposing factors that stabilize secretion.

## Section 1 — Potassium balance and overall renal handling (pp. 792–795)

### Subsection headings
- **Changes in K⁺ concentrations can have major effects on cell and organ function** (pp. 792–793)
- **K⁺ homeostasis involves external K⁺ balance and internal K⁺ balance** (p. 792)
- **Ingested K⁺ moves transiently into cells for storage before excretion by the kidney** (pp. 793–795)
- **The kidney excretes K⁺ by a combination of filtration, reabsorption, and secretion** (p. 795)

### Core claims
- **Distribution.** ~98% of total-body K⁺ (~50 mmol/kg body weight) is intracellular; only ~2% is in the ECF. K⁺ is the most abundant intracellular cation — the mirror image of Na⁺, which is largely extracellular. Plasma [K⁺] is tightly held at **3.5–5.0 mM**.
- **Why the gradient matters.** High intracellular [K⁺] supports cell-volume regulation, intracellular pH, enzyme function, and DNA/protein synthesis; low extracellular [K⁺] preserves the steep transmembrane gradient that sets the resting membrane potential of excitable and non-excitable cells. **Doubling or halving plasma [K⁺]** causes severe skeletal- and cardiac-muscle dysfunction; rising plasma [K⁺] is the dangerous, potentially lethal cardiac threat (see Box 37-1).
- **The leverage problem.** Intracellular K⁺ content is ~3000 mmol; shuttling as little as **1%** of it into the ECF would change extracellular [K⁺] by ~50%. Internal balance (ICF↔ECF distribution) is therefore as physiologically critical as external balance.
- **External balance.** Dietary K⁺ intake (~60–80 mmol/day) roughly equals the *entire* ECF K⁺ content (~65–75 mmol), so excretion must match intake to keep plasma [K⁺] constant. The **kidneys excrete 90–95%** of daily intake; the **colon 5–10%** (the colon can up-regulate but cannot by itself maintain balance).
- **Buffering an acute load.** About **four-fifths of an ingested K⁺ load moves transiently into cells**, so plasma [K⁺] rises only modestly; the kidney then excretes the surfeit over hours, retrieving the stored K⁺ from cells (Fig. 37-2).
- **Hormonal drivers of cellular uptake.** Insulin, epinephrine (β-adrenergic), and aldosterone all drive K⁺ from ECF into cells via the **Na-K pump** (Fig. 37-3). Lack of insulin, a deficient renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, or β-blockade impairs tolerance of a K⁺ load and predisposes to hyperkalemia.
- **Acid-base coupling.** As a rule, **acidemia → hyperkalemia** and **alkalemia → hypokalemia**, behaving like a K-for-H "exchange" although no single transporter mediates it (Fig. 37-4). Conversely, hyperkalemia causes intracellular alkalosis and extracellular acidosis; K⁺ depletion causes intracellular acidosis and extracellular alkalosis.
- **Osmolality.** A rise in extracellular osmolality (e.g., severe hyperglycemia in diabetes) draws water — and K⁺ — out of cells, raising plasma [K⁺].
- **Renal mass balance.** The kidney **filters ~800 mmol/day** of K⁺, far exceeding intake, and normally excretes **10–15%** of the filtered load. On a low-K⁺ diet it excretes 1–3% (balance achievable by filtration + reabsorption alone); on a chronic high-K⁺ diet, urinary K⁺ can exceed **150%** of the filtered load — proof that the tubule must be capable of **secreting** K⁺.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Some 98% of the total-body K⁺ content (~50 mmol/kg body weight) is inside cells; only 2% is in the extracellular fluid (ECF). The body tightly maintains the plasma [K⁺] at 3.5 to 5.0 mM." (p. 792)
- > "Of the total intracellular K⁺ content of ~3000 mmol, shuttling as little as 1% to or from the ECF would cause a 50% change in extracellular [K⁺], with severe consequences for neuromuscular function." (p. 793)
- > "The kidneys excrete 90% to 95% of the daily K⁺ intake; the colon excretes 5% to 10%." (p. 793)
- > "Some four fifths of an ingested K⁺ load temporarily moves into cells, so that plasma [K⁺] rises only modestly." (p. 794)
- > "The hormones insulin, epinephrine (a β-adrenergic agonist), and aldosterone all promote the transfer of K⁺ from extracellular to intracellular fluid via the ubiquitous Na-K pump." (p. 794)
- > "As a rule, acidemia leads to hyperkalemia as tissues release K⁺." (p. 794)
- > "Even if the tubules reabsorb none of the filtered K⁺, they must be capable of secreting an amount equivalent to at least 50% of the filtered K⁺ load." (p. 795)

#### Figure 37-4 — Effect of acidosis on K⁺ uptake into cells *(viewed)*

Net effect: K⁺ leaves cells → hyperkalemia. The reverse occurs in alkalosis.

## Section 2 — K⁺ transport by different nephron segments (pp. 795–798)

### Subsection headings
- **The proximal tubule reabsorbs most of the filtered K⁺, whereas the distal nephron reabsorbs or secretes K⁺ depending on K⁺ intake** (pp. 795–797)
- **Medullary trapping of K⁺ helps to maximize K⁺ excretion when K⁺ intake is high** (pp. 796–798)

### Core claims
- **Fixed-fraction upstream reabsorption.** Regardless of diet, the **PT reabsorbs ~80%** and the **loop of Henle ~10%** of filtered K⁺, so only ~10% reaches the DCT. The **MCD reabsorbs** K⁺ in either case.
- **The distal nephron is the regulated, diet-sensitive site.** Five segments respond to dietary K⁺: **DCT, CNT, ICT, CCT, MCD**.
  - **Low-K⁺ diet (Fig. 37-5A):** the "classic distal tubule" (DCT, CNT, ICT) and CCT *reabsorb* K⁺, so only **1–3%** of the filtered load appears in urine — lifesaving K⁺ conservation, though the kidney cannot restrict K⁺ loss as effectively as Na⁺ loss (hypokalemia can develop with prolonged low intake).
  - **Normal/high-K⁺ diet (Fig. 37-5B):** the ICT, CCT, and early MCD *secrete* K⁺. Together these segments are the **distal K⁺-secretory system** — responsible for most urinary K⁺ excretion and the target of most regulatory stimuli. Even at normal excretion (10–15%), upstream segments reabsorb ~90% first, so urinary K⁺ is largely *newly secreted*.
- **Medullary K⁺ recycling and trapping (Fig. 37-6).** Interstitial [K⁺] is highest at the papillary tip. Three steps build and exploit the medullary K⁺ store:
  1. Juxtamedullary nephrons **secrete K⁺ passively into the thin descending limb (tDLH)** — so much that K⁺ at the hairpin bend can exceed even the filtered load.
  2. The **thin (tALH) and thick (TAL) ascending limbs reabsorb K⁺** into the interstitium; net loop reabsorption exceeds tDLH secretion, sustaining the high interstitial [K⁺].
  3. The **MCDs reabsorb K⁺**, returning it to the interstitium.
- **Purpose of recycling.** Though seemingly futile, trapping minimizes the [K⁺] gradient between the MCD lumen (which can reach ~100 mM during high secretion) and interstitium, reducing passive back-loss and **maximizing net K⁺ excretion when intake is high**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The kidney filters K⁺ in the glomerulus and then extensively reabsorbs it along the proximal tubule (~80%) and the loop of Henle (~10%), so that only ~10% of the filtered K⁺ enters the distal convoluted tubule (DCT)." (p. 795)
- > "Together, these segments, known as the distal K⁺ secretory system, account for most of the urinary excretion of K⁺." (p. 796)
- > "The kidney traps K⁺ in the medullary interstitium, with the interstitial [K⁺] being highest at the tip of the papilla and falling toward the cortex." (p. 796)
- > "This K⁺ secretion by the tDLH is the first step of a process known as medullary K⁺ recycling." (p. 796)
- > "Regardless of whether the distal K⁺-secretory system … reabsorbs K⁺ … or secretes K⁺ …, the medullary collecting ducts reabsorb some K⁺ and thereby contribute to medullary K⁺ trapping." (p. 797)

## Section 3 — K⁺ transport at the cellular and molecular level (pp. 798–800)

### Subsection headings
- **Passive K⁺ reabsorption along the proximal tubule follows Na⁺ and fluid movements** (pp. 798–799)
- **K⁺ reabsorption along the TAL occurs predominantly via a transcellular route that exploits secondary active Na/K/Cl cotransport** (pp. 798–799)
- **K⁺ secretion by principal and intercalated cells of the ICT and CCT involves active K⁺ uptake across the basolateral membrane** (pp. 799–800)
- **K⁺ reabsorption by intercalated cells involves apical uptake via an H-K pump** (p. 800)
- **K⁺ reabsorption along the MCD is both passive and active** (p. 800)

### Core claims
- **Proximal tubule (paracellular).** K⁺ is reabsorbed across tight junctions by **electrodiffusion** (luminal [K⁺] rises as water is reabsorbed; transepithelial voltage shifts from lumen-negative early to lumen-positive late, both favoring K⁺ exit) and by **solvent drag**. PT K⁺ reabsorption is therefore tightly coupled to fluid reabsorption — anything that depresses fluid reabsorption depresses K⁺ reabsorption. PT cells also carry a basolateral Na-K pump, apical/basolateral K⁺ channels (a basolateral **ROMK**-type inwardly-rectifying, ATP-sensitive channel), and a basolateral **KCC**, but these recycle K⁺ rather than reabsorb it net.
- **TAL (transcellular, NKCC2).** Most TAL K⁺ reabsorption uses the apical **Na/K/Cl cotransporter NKCC2**; removing any of the three ions abolishes transport, and **loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide)** block it. The combined inward Na⁺ and Cl⁻ gradients power uphill K⁺ uptake (the apical membrane sits ~10–12 mV positive to the K⁺ equilibrium potential, so K⁺ alone would diffuse *out*). The basolateral Na-K pump is the primary active driver. Apical **ROMK** and **BK (maxi-K)** channels recycle K⁺ back to the lumen to keep luminal [K⁺] from limiting NKCC2; blocking NKCC2 unmasks this leak and makes the TAL *secrete* K⁺.
- **Principal-cell secretion (the regulated step).** Three elements (Fig. 37-7C): **(1)** a basolateral **Na-K pump** loading the cell with K⁺; **(2)** a high, variable apical K⁺ permeability via **ROMK**; **(3)** a favorable apical electrochemical driving force for K⁺ exit, set by the lumen-negative voltage that apical **ENaC** Na⁺ entry generates. Net result: active K⁺ uptake from blood, passive K⁺ exit to lumen. An apical **KCC** offers a secondary exit. High luminal flow recruits **BK (maxi-K) channels** for additional flow-dependent secretion.
- **α-Intercalated-cell reabsorption.** Under K⁺ depletion (Fig. 37-7D), intercalated cells reabsorb K⁺ in two steps: an active apical **H-K ATPase** (homologous to the gastric parietal-cell pump) takes K⁺ up against its gradient while secreting H⁺, and a basolateral K⁺ channel lets K⁺ leak to blood. K⁺ depletion up-regulates H-K-pump abundance — but the coupled H⁺ secretion contributes to **hypokalemic alkalosis**.
- **MCD.** Reabsorbs K⁺ both passively (paracellular, down a favorable gradient — luminal [K⁺] is high from upstream secretion and from AVP-driven water reabsorption) and actively (apical H-K pumps, especially during low K⁺ intake).

### Equations

- **Apical driving force for K⁺ in the TAL** (expressed in electrical terms): the apical membrane potential is ~10–12 mV positive to the K⁺ Nernst potential, so K⁺ tends to diffuse from cell to lumen. The K⁺ equilibrium (Nernst) potential is

  $$E_K = \frac{RT}{zF}\,\ln\!\frac{[K^+]_o}{[K^+]_i} \approx 61.5\,\text{mV}\cdot\log_{10}\!\frac{[K^+]_\text{lumen}}{[K^+]_\text{cell}} \quad (37\text{°C})$$

  with $z=+1$. K⁺ moves across a membrane only when the membrane potential $V_m$ differs from $E_K$; the **electrochemical driving force is** $(V_m - E_K)$, and the K⁺ flux is proportional to apical K⁺ conductance $\times (V_m - E_K)$.

- **Principal-cell secretion.** Secretion is favored when the apical membrane is **depolarized** relative to $E_K$ — exactly what ENaC-mediated Na⁺ entry accomplishes by clamping $V_m$ lumen-negative (i.e., the cell-interior side positive to the lumen), making $(V_m - E_K)$ drive K⁺ outward. Raising luminal [Na⁺] (more ENaC current) and raising flow (steeper cell-to-lumen K⁺ gradient + BK recruitment) both increase secretion; blocking ENaC with **amiloride** hyperpolarizes the apical membrane and abolishes the driving force.

## Section 4 — Regulation of renal K⁺ excretion (pp. 800–805)

### Subsection headings
- **Increased luminal flow increases K⁺ secretion** (pp. 800–801)
- **An increased lumen-negative transepithelial potential increases K⁺ secretion** (p. 800)
- **Low luminal [Cl⁻] enhances K⁺ secretion** (p. 800)
- **Aldosterone increases K⁺ secretion** (pp. 800–802) — mineralocorticoids and glucocorticoids; the aldosterone paradox and aldosterone escape.
- **High K⁺ intake promotes renal K⁺ secretion** (pp. 802–803) — dietary loading, K⁺ adaptation; dietary deprivation.
- **Acidosis decreases K⁺ secretion** (pp. 803–804)
- **Epinephrine reduces and AVP enhances K⁺ excretion** (p. 804)
- **Opposing factors stabilize K⁺ secretion** (pp. 804–805)

### Core claims
- **Flow is one of the most potent stimuli.** Increased flow along the ICT/CCT almost always increases K⁺ secretion (Fig. 37-8) → **kaliuresis**, via two mechanisms: (1) sweeping away secreted K⁺ keeps luminal [K⁺] low, steepening the cell-to-lumen gradient; (2) flow **activates apical BK (maxi-K) channels**, raising apical K⁺ permeability. High flow also raises luminal **[Na⁺]**, boosting ENaC entry and Na-K-pump turnover. This explains the K⁺ wasting of osmotic diuresis, volume expansion, and diuretics acting upstream of the ICT/CCT.
- **Lumen-negative voltage drives secretion.** The apical K⁺-exit step depends on the apical electrochemical gradient. Raising luminal **[Na⁺]** → more ENaC current → apical depolarization → more K⁺ exit; lowering luminal [Na⁺] hyperpolarizes and inhibits secretion. **Amiloride** blocks ENaC → hyperpolarizes the apical membrane *and* lowers [Na⁺]ᵢ (reducing Na-K-pump activity) → it is a **K⁺-sparing diuretic**. The Na⁺ sensitivity is steepest when luminal [Na⁺] < ~35 mM.
- **Low luminal [Cl⁻]** (replacing Cl⁻ with a poorly reabsorbed anion such as SO₄²⁻ or HCO₃⁻) promotes K⁺ loss independently of voltage, probably via an apical **KCC** in principal cells.
- **Aldosterone.** Both mineralocorticoids and glucocorticoids are kaliuretic. Aldosterone stimulates principal-cell secretion three ways: (1) stimulates the **Na-K pump** (hours) and amplifies basolateral membrane area / pump number (days); (2) stimulates apical **ENaC**, depolarizing the apical membrane; (3) increases apical **K⁺ conductance**. Primary hyperaldosteronism → K⁺ wasting + hypokalemia; adrenal insufficiency → K⁺ retention + hyperkalemia.
- **The aldosterone paradox (Na⁺ dependence of the K⁺ effect).** Aldosterone's effect on K⁺ excretion depends strongly on Na⁺ delivery. When Na⁺ intake/excretion is **low**, aldosterone-induced Na⁺ retention drops distal flow and Na⁺ delivery so far that K⁺ excretion **fails to rise**; under **high-flow** conditions (high Na⁺ load, or TAL/DCT diuretics) the same aldosterone raises K⁺ excretion. A second decoupling: in **hypovolemia**, high **ANG II** + aldosterone activate intracellular kinases (**WNK4, SPAK**) that turn on **ENaC** but inhibit **ROMK**, so Na⁺ is retained without large K⁺ loss — the appropriate response to volume depletion. When **hyperkalemia** (not hypovolemia) drives aldosterone, ANG II does *not* rise, and signaling activates both **ENaC and ROMK**, appropriately enhancing K⁺ secretion. This differential response is how the kidney secretes K⁺ when needed without obligatorily dumping Na⁺.
- **Aldosterone escape.** Chronic mineralocorticoid excess causes Na⁺ retention → volume expansion → suppressed proximal Na⁺ reabsorption → more Na⁺ delivered distally → Na⁺ excretion returns toward baseline (the kidney "escapes" the Na⁺-retaining effect, at the cost of hypertension). But the continued high distal Na⁺ delivery keeps **stimulating K⁺ secretion**, producing ongoing K⁺ loss and hypokalemia.
- **Glucocorticoids** enhance K⁺ excretion mainly by **raising distal flow** (↑GFR, ↓distal water permeability); at constant perfusion they have little direct effect. At high doses they can act on mineralocorticoid receptors despite 11β-HSD2.
- **Dietary K⁺ loading and adaptation.** A high-K⁺ diet raises excretion (after a delay) via three mechanisms: (1) a transient rise in plasma [K⁺] stimulates basolateral Na-K-pump uptake (and amplifies basolateral membrane area, *independent of aldosterone*); (2) increased plasma [K⁺] stimulates glomerulosa cells to release **aldosterone**; (3) acute loading inhibits proximal Na⁺/fluid reabsorption → more distal flow/Na⁺ delivery. A possible **feed-forward gut-sensing** mechanism stimulates excretion before plasma [K⁺] or aldosterone rise. **K⁺ adaptation:** chronic high intake lets the kidney excrete previously lethal K⁺ doses with little rise in plasma [K⁺].
- **Dietary K⁺ deprivation.** Excretion falls to **1–3%** of filtered load via: (1) low plasma [K⁺] suppressing principal-cell secretion (↓basolateral uptake, ↓aldosterone); (2) low plasma [K⁺] stimulating **H-K-pump** reabsorption in α-intercalated cells (apical membrane amplification, more rod-shaped pump particles); (3) MCD up-regulating apical K-H pump and paracellular K⁺ permeability. Floor: urinary K⁺ cannot fall below ~15 meq/day, so extreme restriction can still produce hypokalemia.
- **Acid-base.** **Alkalosis → increased K⁺ excretion** (it stimulates distal secretion even though plasma [K⁺] is falling — worsening hypokalemia). **Acidosis → decreased K⁺ secretion** (more variable). The cellular basis (Fig. 37-10): low pHᵢ inhibits the **Na-K pump**, inhibits **ENaC**, and — most importantly — sharply lowers the **open probability of apical ROMK** channels. Flow effects can modulate this: alkalosis delivers HCO₃⁻-rich, high-flow fluid that *potentiates* secretion; acidosis also raises distal flow (by inhibiting proximal reabsorption), which *opposes* its direct inhibitory effect — chronic acidosis (e.g., renal tubular acidosis) actually tends to cause renal K⁺ wasting.
- **Epinephrine** lowers K⁺ excretion both by driving K⁺ into extrarenal tissues (↓plasma [K⁺], ↓filtered load) and by directly inhibiting distal secretion.
- **AVP** can stimulate distal secretion (↑apical Na⁺ conductance → depolarization; ↑apical K⁺ permeability) but simultaneously reduces urine flow — the two effects roughly cancel, so AVP is not a major K⁺ regulator.
- **Opposing factors stabilize secretion (Fig. 37-11).** Many disturbances pit a direct effect against an opposing flow effect: acidosis, volume expansion, and high water intake each have a *direct inhibitory* effect on secretion offset by a *flow-driven stimulatory* effect; volume contraction raises aldosterone (stimulatory) but cuts distal flow/Na⁺ delivery (inhibitory), so K⁺ excretion stays roughly constant. **Additive** cases: metabolic alkalosis (direct stimulation + HCO₃⁻-driven flow), hyperkalemia (direct stimulation + flow from proximal inhibition), and upstream diuretics (↑Na⁺ delivery + volume-contraction-driven aldosterone) — these can drive marked kaliuresis and diuretic-induced hypokalemia.

Five stacked precordial-lead (V₃) ECG traces spanning plasma [K⁺]. **Hyperkalemia** (6 mM): tall, peaked, "tented" T wave, prolonged PR, flattening P; (8 mM): P wave gone, QRS widened, merging toward a sine-wave; (10 mM): risk of ventricular fibrillation. **Normal** (~4 mM): normal complex. **Hypokalemia** (~3 mM): prolonged QT, flattened T; (1.5 mM): prominent **U wave** (delayed ventricular repolarization). Clinical companion to Box 37-1.

#### Figure 37-10 — Effect of intracellular acidosis on K⁺-channel activity in the apical membrane of a principal cell *(viewed)*

Single-channel patch-clamp data (inside-out patches; ROMK). **Panel A — single-channel currents** recorded with the cytoplasmic side at **pH 7.4** vs **pH 7.2**: at pH 7.4 the channel shows frequent openings (1-open, 2-open levels); at pH 7.2 openings are sparser. The text notes channels are **almost never open at pH ≤ 7.0**. **Panel B — open probability** plotted against cytoplasmic pH (x-axis 6.8–7.4; y-axis $P_o$ 0–1.0): a steep sigmoid rising from near 0 at pH 6.8 to ~1.0 at pH 7.4. This is the molecular basis for acidosis suppressing — and alkalosis enhancing — distal K⁺ secretion.

#### Figure 37-11 — Effects of interaction of opposing factors on K⁺ secretion *(listed)*

Four flow-chart panels. **(A) Acidosis:** direct ↓ in intracellular K⁺ content / secretion vs. ↑ distal flow (from ↓ proximal reabsorption) → near cancellation. **(B) Volume expansion:** ↓ proximal reabsorption → ↑ distal flow → ↑ secretion offsetting the direct effect. **(C) High water intake (water diuresis):** ↓ distal water reabsorption → ↑ flow → ↑ secretion. **(D) Volume contraction:** ↑ ANG II → ↑ aldosterone (stimulatory) but ↓ distal flow/Na⁺ delivery (inhibitory) → K⁺ secretion roughly constant. Illustrates the chapter's "stabilizing" theme.

## Glossary

- **Internal vs. external K⁺ balance** — ICF↔ECF distribution vs. body↔environment intake/output.
- **Distal K⁺-secretory system** — ICT + CCT (+ proximal MCD); the regulated site of net K⁺ secretion.
- **Principal cell** — secretes K⁺: basolateral Na-K pump loads, apical ROMK secretes, ENaC sets lumen-negative voltage.
- **ROMK** — apical inwardly-rectifying, ATP- and pH-sensitive K⁺ channel; the main secretory channel and the acid-base sensor.
- **BK / maxi-K (BK_Ca)** — flow-activated apical K⁺ channel; mediates flow-dependent secretion.
- **ENaC** — apical epithelial Na⁺ channel; Na⁺ entry sets the lumen-negative driving force for K⁺ exit; blocked by amiloride.
- **NKCC2** — apical Na/K/Cl cotransporter of the TAL; loop-diuretic target; transcellular K⁺ reabsorption.
- **α-Intercalated cell** — reabsorbs K⁺ via apical H-K ATPase (couples to H⁺ secretion → hypokalemic alkalosis).
- **Medullary K⁺ recycling / trapping** — tDLH secretion → tALH/TAL/MCD reabsorption → high papillary interstitial [K⁺]; maximizes excretion at high intake.
- **Electrochemical driving force** — $(V_m - E_K)$; sign determines secretion vs. reabsorption across the apical membrane.
- **Aldosterone paradox** — aldosterone's K⁺-secretory effect depends on distal Na⁺/flow; WNK4/SPAK signaling separates Na⁺ retention (hypovolemia, ANG II high) from K⁺ secretion (hyperkalemia, ANG II low).
- **Aldosterone escape** — Na⁺ excretion normalizes under chronic aldosterone (volume-mediated) but K⁺ wasting persists.
- **K⁺ adaptation** — chronic high intake raises the kidney's K⁺-excretory capacity.
- **Kaliuresis** — increased urinary K⁺ excretion.
- **Hyperkalemia / hypokalemia** — plasma [K⁺] above/below 3.5–5.0 mM; ECG signatures (peaked T / U wave).
