## Section 1 — Principles of endocrine function (pp. 974–981)

### Subsection headings
- **Chemical signaling can occur through endocrine, paracrine, or autocrine pathways** (pp. 974–975)
- **Hormones may be peptides, metabolites of single amino acids, or metabolites of cholesterol** (pp. 975–976)
- **Hormones can circulate either free or bound to carrier proteins** (p. 976)
- **Immunoassays allow measurement of circulating hormones** (pp. 976–977)
- **Hormones can have complementary and antagonistic actions** (pp. 977–978)
- **Endocrine regulation occurs through feedback control** (pp. 977–978)
- **Endocrine regulation can involve hierarchic levels of control** (pp. 978–979)
- **The anterior pituitary regulates reproduction, growth, energy metabolism, and stress responses** (pp. 979–980)
- **The posterior pituitary regulates water balance and uterine contraction** (pp. 980–981)

### Core claims

- **Two integrating systems.** The body coordinates organ function through the **nervous system** (a network of cells signaling over short distances) and the **endocrine system** (glands secreting **hormones** into the ECF that travel via blood to distant targets bearing specific high-affinity receptors). Many hormones circulate at extraordinarily low concentrations (~10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹² M), so receptor recognition must be both highly sensitive and highly specific.
- **Three modes of chemical signaling** (all referencing Fig. 3-1): **endocrine** — secreted into blood, acts on distant targets; **paracrine** — secreted into ECF, acts on neighboring cells without entering the systemic circulation; **autocrine** — acts on receptors on/in the very cell that secreted it. Summation of all three frames a complex regulatory system.
- **Seven classic endocrine glands/gland-pairs:** pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, testes, ovaries, adrenals (cortex + medulla), endocrine pancreas. Non-classic hormone-producing tissues include the CNS (especially hypothalamus), GI tract, adipose tissue, liver, heart, and kidney.
- **Paracrine factors** (interleukins/lymphokines, growth factors such as PDGF and FGF) are not hormones in the usual sense — not made by glands, usually local — but share machinery with peptide/amine hormones (surface receptors, intracellular signaling). The line blurs further for **neuropeptides** (e.g., somatostatin acts as a paracrine islet factor *and* as a hypothalamic releasing-inhibitory factor delivered via the portal system).
- **Three chemical classes** (Table 47-1): **peptide/protein** hormones (largest group; pancreas, pituitary, parathyroid, thyroid C-cells, hypothalamus, GI tract); **amine** hormones (catecholamines and serotonin from tyrosine/tryptophan; plus thyroid hormone); **steroid** hormones (cholesterol-derived; adrenal cortex and gonads). Catecholamine and steroid synthesis require enzymes confined to very specialized tissues; thyroid-hormone synthesis is essentially restricted to the thyroid.
- **Transport — free vs. bound.** Many hormones circulate free; others complex with **binding proteins** — especially thyroid hormones (T₄, T₃), steroids, IGF-1/IGF-2, and GH. Binding (a) provides a circulating reservoir that buffers minute-to-minute fluctuations and (b) extends half-life. Example: **>99.99% of T₄ is protein-bound**; bound-T₄ half-life is **7–8 days** vs. **minutes** for free T₄. Free hormone is the biologically active fraction; short-acting hormones (catecholamines, many peptides) circulate free.
- **Binding proteins change total without changing free.** In pregnancy, hepatic synthesis of T₄-binding globulin rises; total T₄ rises, but the pituitary senses the transient dip in free T₄, raises TSH, and free T₄ is restored — **total ↑, free unchanged**. This is the central pitfall in interpreting hormone assays.
- **Immunoassay (RIA → ELISA/chemiluminescence).** Berson and Yalow's **radioimmunoassay (RIA)** exploits competition between labeled and unlabeled hormone for a fixed amount of high-affinity antibody: more unlabeled hormone → less labeled hormone bound (Fig. 47-1). A displacement (standard) curve then quantifies unknowns. Valid only if (1) nothing else in the sample binds the antibody and (2) nothing interferes with hormone–antibody binding. Modern assays substitute **chemiluminescent or enzymatic (ELISA)** detection for radioactivity. Yalow won the 1977 Nobel Prize.
- **Complementary and antagonistic actions.** Epinephrine, cortisol, and glucagon act **complementarily** in exercise (loss of any impairs performance and risks hypoglycemia/hyperkalemia); GH, insulin, IGF-1, thyroid hormone, and sex steroids together drive normal growth. **Antagonistic** pairs balance an end organ — e.g., insulin (lowers glucose) vs. glucagon/epinephrine (raise glucose).
- **Feedback control** (Fig. 47-2A): the hormone-secreting cell acts as a **sensor** monitoring a regulated variable (a metabolite such as glucose, or another hormone's activity) and adjusts secretion to oppose deviations — classic **negative feedback**. Simple example: β-cell senses plasma [glucose] → secretes insulin → liver/muscle lower glucose → β-cell senses the fall → reduces secretion.
- **Hierarchic control** (Fig. 47-2B): stress → cerebral cortex → hypothalamus releases **CRH** → (via pituitary portal system) anterior pituitary releases **ACTH** → adrenal cortex makes **cortisol**. Feedback acts at multiple levels: cortisol inhibits hypothalamic CRH production *and* reduces pituitary sensitivity to CRH (so ACTH falls). This is **long-loop negative feedback**; analogous multi-level loops govern the menstrual cycle and GH secretion.
- **The pituitary (hypophysis)** sits in the **sella turcica** below the hypothalamus and has two lobes. The **posterior pituitary receives arterial blood**; the **anterior pituitary receives only portal venous inflow** from the median eminence — the **pituitary portal system** carries hypothalamic neuropeptides to the anterior lobe.
- **Anterior pituitary** synthesizes six peptide hormones — **GH, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH, PRL** — each under control of hypothalamic **hypophysiotropic (releasing/inhibitory) factors** (Table 47-2). These factors are made by **small-diameter periventricular neurons** that discharge into leaky median-eminence capillaries (outside the blood-brain barrier) and travel via portal veins to the anterior lobe. Four anterior hormones (**TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH**) themselves target other endocrine glands, so they are "releasing hormones" one tier down. **GH → IGF-1**, and circulating IGF-1 feeds back on hypothalamus (↓GHRH) and pituitary (↓GH). **PRL** is unique: normally **tonically inhibited by hypothalamic dopamine** (no classic endocrine feedback loop identified); breast stimulation inhibits DA release, disinhibiting lactation.
- **Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)** is literally part of the brain — the nerve endings of **large-diameter** supraoptic/paraventricular neurons that synthesize **AVP (ADH)** and **oxytocin (OT)** and transport them axonally for release. AVP → collecting-duct water reabsorption; OT → uterine contraction (parturition) and milk ejection. Each is made as a **prohormone with a neurophysin** (proneurophysin II for AVP, proneurophysin I for OT); cleavage occurs during axonal transport, and neurophysin is co-secreted stoichiometrically. Defective processing of pro-AVP → diabetes insipidus.

### Equations

- **Free vs. bound hormone (mass-action equilibrium).** A hormone $H$ and binding globulin $P$ equilibrate as $H + P \rightleftharpoons HP$:

  $$K_a = \frac{[HP]}{[H]\,[P]} \qquad [H]_{\text{total}} = [H]_{\text{free}} + [HP]$$

  The **free** concentration $[H]_{\text{free}}$ — not the total — drives receptor occupancy and biological effect. Raising $[P]$ (e.g., TBG in pregnancy) shifts equilibrium toward $HP$, transiently lowering $[H]_{\text{free}}$; the feedback axis then restores it while $[H]_{\text{total}}$ rises.

- **Negative-feedback gain.** A regulated variable $x$ is held near setpoint $x_0$ when secretion rate $S$ falls as $x$ rises:

  $$\frac{dS}{dx} < 0$$

  The steeper this relation (higher loop gain), the tighter the regulation of $x$.

## Section 2 — Peptide hormones (pp. 981–984)

### Subsection headings
- **Specialized endocrine cells synthesize, store, and secrete peptide hormones** (pp. 981–982)
- **Peptide hormones bind to cell-surface receptors and activate a variety of signal-transduction systems** (pp. 982–984)

### Core claims

- **Synthesis as prehormones/prohormones.** Transcription is controlled by cis- and trans-acting elements; mRNA is translated on rough-ER ribosomes. An **N-terminal signal (pre-) sequence** targets the nascent chain to the ER lumen, where processing (glycosylation, proteolytic cleavage of the **prohormone**) yields the mature hormone. Trafficking proceeds ER → cis-Golgi → trans-Golgi → **secretory vesicle/granule**, where the mature peptide is **stored** before release.
- **Two secretory pathways.** **Regulated pathway** — mature hormone stored in granules; external stimuli (e.g., GHRH binding somatotrophs → GH release) trigger exocytosis and also upregulate synthesis. **Constitutive pathway** — secretion directly from ER/cis-Golgi vesicles, less responsive to stimuli. Both end in **exocytosis** (vesicle–plasma-membrane fusion). The regulated pathway can release much larger amounts on demand, yet even when stimulated a cell secretes only a small fraction of its granule store; ongoing synthesis maintains the **secretory reserve**.
- **Water-soluble; surface receptors.** Most peptide hormones circulate **free** (exception: IGF-1/IGF-2, bound by ≥6 plasma proteins). They engage **intrinsic membrane (cell-surface) receptors** with high affinity ($K_D \approx 10^{-9}$–$10^{-11}$ M). Receptor occupancy activates an intracellular signal-transduction system that relays the message inward (Table 47-3; Fig. 47-4).
- **Signal-transduction families** (each detailed in Ch 3):
  - **GPCR → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP** (e.g., **PTH** via Gαs; **ANG II** inhibits AC via Gαi). cAMP → PKA → phosphorylation of Ser/Thr residues; terminated by phosphodiesterases (degrade cAMP) and phosphoprotein phosphatases.
  - **GPCR → PLC → IP₃ + DAG** (e.g., **AVP, ANG II, TRH** via Gαq): IP₃ releases ER Ca²⁺ (activating Ca²⁺-calmodulin kinases); DAG (with Ca²⁺) activates **PKC**.
  - **GPCR → PLA₂ → arachidonic acid → eicosanoids** (e.g., **TRH**): prostaglandins, prostacyclins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes.
  - **Receptor guanylyl cyclase → cGMP** (e.g., **ANP**): activates cGMP-dependent kinases, phosphatases, ion channels.
  - **Receptor tyrosine kinases** (e.g., **insulin, IGF-1/2, EGF, PDGF**): the receptor autophosphorylates tyrosines and phosphorylates cytosolic substrates.
  - **Tyrosine-kinase–associated receptors / JAK-STAT** (e.g., **GH, erythropoietin, LIF**): receptor recruits a cytoplasmic **JAK** kinase that triggers a phosphorylation cascade (STAT signaling).
- **Clinical (Box 47-2 — pseudohypoparathyroidism):** a defect in the **stimulatory Gαs subunit** impairs PTH signaling, giving low serum Ca²⁺ and high phosphate **despite elevated PTH** (target-tissue resistance); the same Gαs defect also predisposes to hypothyroidism and gonadal dysfunction.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "This pathway is referred to as the regulated pathway of hormone synthesis because external stimuli can trigger the cell to release hormone that is stored in the secretory granule as well as to increase synthesis of additional hormone." (p. 981)
- > "In both the regulated and constitutive pathways, fusion of the vesicular membrane with the plasma membrane—exocytosis of the vesicular contents—is the final common pathway for hormone secretion." (p. 981)
- > "Once secreted, most peptide hormones exist free in the circulation… this lack of binding proteins contrasts with the situation for steroid and thyroid hormones, which circulate bound to plasma proteins." (p. 981)
- > "These receptors are intrinsic membrane proteins that bind the hormone with very high affinity (typically, K_D ranges from 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹¹ M)." (p. 982)
- > "cAMP, the prototypical second messenger, was discovered during an investigation of the action of glucagon on glycogenolysis in the liver." (p. 982)
- > "For some peptide hormones, notably insulin and IGF-1 and IGF-2, the hormone receptor itself possesses tyrosine kinase activity." (p. 984)

## Section 3 — Amine hormones (pp. 984–985)

### Subsection headings
- **Amine hormones are made from tyrosine and tryptophan** (pp. 984–985)
- **Amine hormones act via surface receptors** (p. 985)

### Core claims

- **Four amine hormones.** The adrenal medulla makes the catecholamines **epinephrine** and **norepinephrine** from **tyrosine** (chromaffin-granule storage; secretion driven by sympathetic stimulation). **Dopamine** (also from tyrosine) is chiefly a CNS neurotransmitter. **Serotonin (5-HT)** is made from **tryptophan** by neuroendocrine cells lining the small intestine and bronchi — not by a discrete gland.
- **Indirect (end-effect) feedback.** Unlike peptide axes where the circulating hormone (e.g., TSH) feeds back on its releasing hormone (TRH), amine hormones have **no hierarchic feedback**; control senses a **physiological end effect** (e.g., blood pressure via peripheral stretch receptors → CNS → sympathetic efferent → catecholamine release), not the amine level itself.
- **Carcinoid tumors** of serotonin-secreting cells cause **carcinoid syndrome** (flushing of head/neck, diarrhea, bronchospasm, right-sided valvular heart disease).
- **Surface adrenoceptors (GPCRs).** Circulating catecholamines bind α- and β-adrenoceptors (subtypes in Table 14-2). **Epinephrine** prefers β; **norepinephrine** acts predominantly through α. **β (and α₂)** → AC/cAMP; **α₂** inhibits AC (Gαi); **α₁** → Gαq → PLC → IP₃/DAG → Ca²⁺-dependent kinases. Tissue response depends on the **receptor repertoire** of the cell — e.g., hepatic glycogenolysis (β), vascular smooth-muscle contraction (α₁) vs. relaxation (β₂), cardiac inotropy/chronotropy (β₁). **Dopamine:** D1 → Gαs, D2 → Gαi.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The adrenal medulla makes the catecholamine hormones epinephrine and norepinephrine from the amino acid tyrosine." (p. 984)
- > "Unlike the situation for many peptide hormones… the amine hormones do not have such a hierarchic feedback system. Rather, the feedback of amine hormones is indirect." (p. 985)
- > "Once secreted, circulating epinephrine is free to associate with specific adrenergic receptors, or adrenoceptors, located on the surface membranes of target cells." (p. 985)
- > "The response of a specific cell to adrenergic stimulation… depends on the receptor repertoire displayed by the cell." (p. 985)

## Section 4 — Steroid and thyroid hormones (pp. 985–989)

### Subsection headings
- **Cholesterol is the precursor for the steroid hormones: cortisol, aldosterone, estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone** (pp. 985–986)
- **Steroid hormones bind to intracellular receptors that regulate gene transcription** (pp. 986–988)
- **Thyroid hormones bind to intracellular receptors that regulate metabolic rate** (pp. 988–989)
- **Steroid and thyroid hormones can also have nongenomic actions** (p. 989)

### Core claims

- **Cholesterol-derived; only two tissues.** Only the **adrenal cortex** (cortisol, aldosterone, androgens) and the **gonads** (estrogen/progesterone in ovary; testosterone in testis) can convert cholesterol to active steroid hormones; production is driven by pituitary trophic hormones (and, for aldosterone, the renin-angiotensin system).
- **Cholesterol sourcing** (Fig. 47-6): ~**80%** from circulating **LDL** taken up by **LDL-receptor–mediated endocytosis** (clathrin-coated vesicles → lysosomal hydrolysis of cholesteryl esters → free cholesterol); the rest synthesized **de novo from acetate/acetyl-CoA**. Cholesterol → **pregnenolone** (the common precursor) → divergent pathways to aldosterone, cortisol, testosterone, estradiol.
- **No storage pool.** Unlike peptide/amine hormones, **steroids are not stored** in vesicles (Table 47-4); synthesis and secretion are tightly coupled, but cells can ramp secretion many-fold within hours. Being lipophilic, steroids **diffuse** across membranes rather than exocytose.
- **Lipophilic; carried by binding globulins.** Poorly water-soluble, steroids circulate bound to carriers — e.g., **cortisol-binding globulin (CBG)**, **thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG)** for thyroid hormone, and **sex-hormone–binding globulin (SHBG)**. Carrier concentrations vary physiologically, complicating measurement of the active (free) fraction.
- **Intracellular nuclear receptors → transcription.** Steroids diffuse in and bind **cytosolic/nuclear receptors** with high affinity ($K_D \approx 1$ nM, Fig. 47-7). The hormone–receptor complex changes conformation, **dimerizes**, and binds **hormone (steroid) response elements (HREs/SREs)** in the 5′ region of target genes (upstream of the TATA box), altering transcription rate. Receptors are 80–100 kDa monomeric phosphoproteins; glucocorticoid, sex-steroid, retinoic-acid, vitamin-D, and thyroid-hormone receptors form a **nuclear-receptor superfamily** with a conserved modular six-domain (A–F) structure and a **C-domain (zinc-finger) DNA-binding** region. Response times are **hours to days** (transcription/translation).
- **Specificity from receptor expression.** Because every somatic cell carries the same SRE-bearing genes, **which** steroid a tissue responds to is set by **which receptors it expresses** (breast → estrogen, prostate → androgen, collecting duct → mineralocorticoid). Striking magnitude example: a 7-day estrogen course increases chick-oviduct **ovalbumin mRNA ~10,000-fold**.
- **Receptor regulation — up/down-regulation and dose–response.** Receptor number is not fixed: estrogen-priming **upregulates** estrogen receptors in the uterus; progesterone **downregulates** its own receptor; agonist exposure transiently depletes cytosolic receptors (movement to nucleus) before repopulation by synthesis or recycling. Tissue sensitivity tracks receptor concentration. Receptor affinity and number are quantified by **saturation curves and Scatchard plots** (Fig. 47-8; clinical use in Box 47-3).
- **Thyroid hormone.** Synthesized by **iodination of tyrosines within thyroglobulin** in the follicle lumen; secretion requires endocytosis and lysosomal degradation of thyroglobulin to free **T₄ and T₃**. T₄ is tightly **protein-bound** in plasma; peripheral tissues **deiodinate T₄ → T₃**. Both enter target cells and bind **cytosolic/nuclear receptors** of the same superfamily; **T₃ has higher receptor affinity** and, though only ~5% of circulating thyroid hormone, is the **main effector**. Thyroid-hormone receptors are widespread (heart, vascular/skeletal muscle, liver, kidney, skin, CNS); the dominant role is **setting metabolic rate**.
- **Nongenomic actions.** Some effects appear within **2–15 min** — too fast for new protein synthesis. Steroid/thyroid hormones can act through membrane receptors: e.g., estrogen and aldosterone bind the GPCR **GPR30/GPER**, rapidly activating **Gαs → AC → cAMP → PKA**, and indirectly the **EGFR (a receptor tyrosine kinase)** with downstream **PI3K** and **MAPK** signaling.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Members of the family of hormones called steroids share a common biochemical parentage: all are synthesized from cholesterol." (p. 985)
- > "In humans, LDL cholesterol appears to furnish ~80% of the cholesterol used for steroid synthesis." (p. 986)
- > "Unlike the peptide and amine hormones considered above, steroid hormones are not stored in secretory vesicles before their secretion." (p. 986)
- > "On their release into the circulation, some steroid hormones associate with specific binding proteins (e.g., cortisol-binding globulin) that transport the steroid hormones through the circulatory system to their target tissues." (p. 986)
- > "Binding of steroid hormone to its receptor results in a change in the receptor conformation so that the 'active' receptor-hormone complex now binds with high affinity to specific DNA sequences called hormone response elements." (pp. 986–987)
- > "Steroid hormone receptors dimerize on binding to their target sites on DNA. Dimerization appears essential for the regulation of gene transcription." (p. 987)
- > "Regulation of steroid receptor number is clearly one factor that alters overall tissue sensitivity to these hormones." (p. 988)
- > "Even though it accounts for only ~5% of the circulating thyroid hormone, T₃ is probably the main effector of thyroid hormone signaling." (p. 989)
- > "However, the very rapid onset of some effects (occurring within 2 to 15 minutes) appear incompatible with a mechanism requiring new protein synthesis." (p. 989)

### Equations

- **Receptor occupancy (mass action).** For hormone $H$ binding receptor $R$ with dissociation constant $K_D$:

  $$[HR] = \frac{[R]_{\text{total}}\,[H]}{K_D + [H]} \qquad \text{(saturation / dose–response)}$$

  Half-maximal occupancy occurs at $[H] = K_D$ (here $K_D \approx 1$ nM for steroid receptors, Fig. 47-8A).

- **Scatchard linearization** (Fig. 47-8B) for measuring affinity and receptor number:

  $$\frac{[\text{bound}]}{[\text{free}]} = -\frac{1}{K_D}\,[\text{bound}] + \frac{[R]_{\text{total}}}{K_D}$$

  The plot of bound/free vs. bound is a line of slope $-1/K_D$; the **x-intercept gives total receptor number** $[R]_{\text{total}}$.

- **Spare receptors / dose–response decoupling.** Because maximal biological response can be reached with only a fraction of receptors occupied, the **EC₅₀ for response can be lower than $K_D$**; the difference reflects **spare receptors**. Net tissue sensitivity scales with $[R]_{\text{total}}$, which up-/down-regulation adjusts.

## Glossary

- **Endocrine / paracrine / autocrine** — distance modes of chemical signaling (blood-borne / local-ECF / self).
- **Hormone chemical classes** — peptide/protein, amine (catecholamine, serotonin, thyroid), steroid.
- **Prehormone / prohormone** — precursor forms; pre- = signal sequence, pro- = cleaved to mature hormone.
- **Regulated vs. constitutive secretory pathway** — stimulus-triggered granule release vs. direct ER/Golgi release.
- **Binding globulins** — CBG (cortisol), TBG (thyroxine), SHBG (sex steroids); set free vs. bound fractions.
- **Free vs. bound hormone** — only free hormone is bioactive; binding extends half-life and buffers levels.
- **Immunoassay (RIA / ELISA)** — competitive antibody binding; displacement standard curve.
- **Negative feedback (long / short / ultrashort loop)** — hormone inhibits its own upstream controllers.
- **Hypothalamic-pituitary axis** — master integrator; portal system; hypophysiotropic releasing/inhibitory factors.
- **Anterior pituitary** — GH, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH, PRL; "troph" cells; portal-venous supply.
- **Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)** — AVP and OT; large-neuron axonal release; neurophysin co-secretion.
- **GPCR second messengers** — cAMP (Gαs/Gαi), IP₃/DAG (Gαq), eicosanoids (PLA₂), cGMP (guanylyl cyclase).
- **Receptor tyrosine kinase / JAK-STAT** — peptide-hormone phosphorylation cascades.
- **Nuclear-receptor superfamily** — steroid, thyroid, retinoic-acid, vitamin-D receptors; zinc-finger DBD; HRE/SRE binding; dimerization.
- **Up-/down-regulation; spare receptors** — receptor number sets tissue sensitivity; EC₅₀ may be below K_D.
- **Scatchard plot** — slope −1/K_D, x-intercept = receptor number.
- **Nongenomic action** — rapid (2–15 min) membrane-receptor effects of steroid/thyroid hormones (e.g., GPR30/GPER).
