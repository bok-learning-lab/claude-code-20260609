# Chapter 39 — Transport of Acids and Bases

> Section VI · The Urinary System · pp. 821–835 · Authors: Giebisch, Windhager & Aronson

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Acid-base balance and overall renal handling of acid** (pp. 821–825) — the daily fixed-acid load (~70 mmol/day); reclamation of the 4320 mmol/day of filtered HCO₃⁻; the three components of net acid excretion (titratable acid, NH₄⁺, minus excreted HCO₃⁻).
2. **Acid-base transport by different nephron segments** (pp. 825–827) — proximal tubule ~80%, TAL ~10%, distal ~10% of HCO₃⁻ reclamation; generation of new HCO₃⁻ via titratable acid and NH₄⁺.
3. **Acid-base transport at the cellular/molecular level** (pp. 827–832) — apical H⁺ exit (NHE3, electrogenic H pump, H-K pump); carbonic anhydrases (CA II, CA IV); basolateral HCO₃⁻ exit (NBCe1, AE1/AE2); ammoniagenesis and NH₃/NH₄⁺ medullary recycling; hepatorenal nitrogen handling.
4. **Regulation of renal acid secretion** (pp. 832–835) — respiratory and metabolic acidosis/alkalosis; glomerulotubular balance for HCO₃⁻; ECF volume; K⁺; corticosteroids; diuretics.

## Section 1 — Acid-base balance and overall renal handling of acid (pp. 821–825)

### Subsection headings
- **Whereas the lungs excrete the large amount of CO₂ formed by metabolism, the kidneys are crucial for excreting nonvolatile acids** (pp. 821–823)
- **To maintain acid-base balance, the kidney must not only reabsorb virtually all filtered HCO₃⁻ but also secrete generated nonvolatile acids** (p. 823)
- **Secreted H⁺ titrates HCO₃⁻ to CO₂ (HCO₃⁻ reabsorption) and also titrates filtered non-HCO₃⁻ buffers and endogenously produced NH₃** (pp. 823–825)

### Core claims
- **Two organs, two limbs of the buffer system.** The lungs control plasma [CO₂]; the kidneys control plasma [HCO₃⁻]. Metabolism on a Western diet produces ~15,000 mmol/day of CO₂ (volatile, excreted by the lung) but also ~70 mmol/day of **nonvolatile** acid the lung cannot handle.
- **Origin of the ~70 mmol/day fixed-acid load.** Net endogenous H⁺ production ≈ 40 mmol/day (sulfuric acid from S-containing amino acids, phosphoric acid, organic acids, minus metabolically generated HCO₃⁻ from anionic amino acids/organic anions); diet contributes ~20 mmol/day of H⁺ and obligatory fecal base loss ~10 mmol/day of OH⁻. Total ≈ 70 mmol/day ≈ 1 mmol/kg/day.
- **Two tasks.** (1) Reclaim the entire filtered HCO₃⁻ load: 180 L/day × 24 mM = **4320 mmol/day**. (2) Excrete the 70 mmol/day of new acid and return 70 mmol/day of **new** HCO₃⁻ to blood.
- **Why buffers are needed.** Pouring 70 mmol H⁺ into the ~1.5 L of daily urine unbuffered would give pH ≈ 1.3; the kidney's minimum achievable urine pH is only ~4.4. So secreted H⁺ must be carried on **buffers**: filtered phosphate (pK 6.8 — the dominant filtered buffer), creatinine, urate (titratable acid), and synthesized **NH₃/NH₄⁺** (pK ~9.2).
- **Three buffers compete for secreted H⁺.** Secreted H⁺ titrates (1) filtered HCO₃⁻, (2) filtered non-HCO₃⁻ buffers → titratable acid, and (3) NH₃ → NH₄⁺. The kidney secretes ~4390 mmol/day of H⁺ total: ~4320 (98%) to reclaim HCO₃⁻, ~70 to generate new HCO₃⁻.
- **Carbonic anhydrase makes reclamation fast.** Uncatalyzed H₂CO₃ → H₂O + CO₂ is far too slow; CA splits HCO₃⁻ into CO₂ + OH⁻ (and hydrates CO₂), letting secreted H⁺ neutralize OH⁻ and accelerating CO₂/H₂O production. HCO₃⁻ that disappears from the lumen is **not the same molecule** that appears in blood.
- **Reabsorption ≠ net acid excretion.** HCO₃⁻ reabsorption merely prevents loss of filtered alkali; only titratable acid + NH₄⁺ represent net new acid disposal.

### Equations

The four buffering reactions of the body's three-step response to a fixed-acid load:

- **Step 1 — extracellular HCO₃⁻ buffers most of the H⁺** (Eq. 39-1):
  $$\text{HCO}_3^- + \text{H}^+ \;\rightarrow\; \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
- **Non-HCO₃⁻ blood buffers take the remainder** (Eq. 39-2): $\;\text{B}^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{HB}$
- **Step 3 — kidney regenerates base** (Eq. 39-3): $\;\text{HCO}_3^- + \text{HB} \rightarrow \text{B}^- + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, i.e. the kidney returns 70 mmol/day **more** HCO₃⁻ via renal vein than entered via renal artery.
- **Titratable-acid formation** (Eq. 39-4): $\;\text{H}^+ + \text{B}^- \rightarrow \text{HB}$ (B⁻ mainly HPO₄²⁻, also creatinine, urate).

The governing buffer relation throughout is **Henderson-Hasselbalch**:

$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K + \log_{10}\frac{[\text{HCO}_3^-]}{0.03 \cdot P_{\text{CO}_2}}$$

and, in the general form used for each filtered buffer (phosphate pK 6.8, urate 5.8, creatinine 5.0):

$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K + \log_{10}\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$$

**Net (urinary) acid excretion** — the chapter's central balance (Table 39-2):

$$\text{NAE} = \underbrace{\text{(titratable acid)}}_{\text{H}^+\text{ on phosphate, creatinine, urate}} + \underbrace{[\text{NH}_4^+]}_{\text{excreted}} - \underbrace{[\text{HCO}_3^-]}_{\text{excreted}}$$

At steady state NAE equals the rate of new-HCO₃⁻ generation (~70 mmol/day). In **alkalosis**, component 3 (excreted HCO₃⁻) can exceed components 1+2, so NAE goes **negative** and the kidney becomes a net excretor of alkali.

- > "Thus, the body is faced with a total load of nonvolatile acids (i.e., not CO₂) of ~70 mmol/day—or ~1 mmol/kg body weight—derived from metabolism, diet, and intestinal losses." (p. 821)
- > "The daily filtered load of HCO₃⁻ is 180 L × 24 mM = 4320 mmol. If this filtered HCO₃⁻ were all left behind in the urine, the result would be … a catastrophic metabolic acidosis." (p. 823)
- > "The lowest urine pH that the kidney can achieve is ~4.4, which corresponds to an [H⁺] that is three orders of magnitude lower than required to excrete the 70 mmol/day of nonvolatile acids." (pp. 821–823)
- > "Because of its favorable pK of 6.8 and its relatively high rate of excretion, phosphate is the most important nonvolatile filtered buffer." (p. 822)
- > "HCO₃⁻ reabsorption does not represent net H⁺ excretion into the urine. It merely prevents the loss of the filtered alkali." (p. 823)

#### Figure 39-2 — Titration of luminal buffers by secreted H⁺ *(listed)*

Three generic cell models. **A — HCO₃⁻ reabsorption:** secreted H⁺ titrates luminal HCO₃⁻; luminal CA splits HCO₃⁻ → CO₂ + OH⁻; the retrieved HCO₃⁻ exits basolaterally. **B — titratable-acid formation:** secreted H⁺ protonates luminal HPO₄²⁻ → H₂PO₄⁻; one **new** HCO₃⁻ is generated and exits to blood. **C — ammonium excretion:** secreted H⁺ traps luminal NH₃ as NH₄⁺; one new HCO₃⁻ exits to blood. ## Section 2 — Acid-base transport by different nephron segments (pp. 825–827)

### Subsection headings
- **Most nephron segments secrete H⁺ to varying degrees** (p. 825)
- **The nephron reclaims virtually all the filtered HCO₃⁻ in the proximal tubule (~80%), TAL (~10%), and distal nephron (~10%)** (p. 825)
- **The nephron generates new HCO₃⁻, mostly in the proximal tubule** (pp. 825–827)

### Core claims
- **Segmental HCO₃⁻ reclamation.** PT ~80% (luminal pH falls only to ~6.8 → high-capacity, low-gradient); TAL +10% (cumulative ~90% by the DCT); the rest of the distal nephron (DCT → IMCD) reabsorbs the remaining ~10% but can drive luminal pH to ~4.4 → **low-capacity, high-gradient** system.
- **Fractional HCO₃⁻ excretion is tiny.** At urine pH 5.4, urine [HCO₃⁻] ≈ 0.24 mM → ~0.36 mmol/day lost = fractional excretion ~0.01% (reclaim ~99.99%). At pH 4.4, reclaim ~99.999%.
- **New HCO₃⁻ generation has two arms** (Fig. 39-3B): titratable acid (mostly phosphate; ~30 mmol/day) and NH₄⁺ excretion (~40 mmol/day). In healthy people **NH₄⁺ is the larger arm — ~60% of net acid excretion / new HCO₃⁻.**
- **Titratable acid develops segmentally** (Table 39-4): phosphate titratable acid is already ~14 mmol/day at the end of the PT (because there luminal pH ≈ phosphate pK 6.8), rises only slightly along the classical distal tubule, then climbs further in the collecting ducts as pH falls to 4.4. Creatinine titratable acid is minuscule in the PT (pH ≫ pK 5.0) but rises sharply in the collecting ducts.
- **NH₄⁺ excretion is a five-process result.** (1) PT secretes slightly more than ~40 mmol/day NH₄⁺; (2) TAL reabsorbs some NH₄⁺ → interstitium; (3) some interstitial NH₄⁺ recycles to PT/tDLH; (4) some enters the collecting-duct lumen; (5) some enters vasa recta and leaves the kidney. Net new HCO₃⁻ from NH₄⁺ = (1) − (2) + (3) + (4) − (5).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The kidney reabsorbs the largest fraction of filtered HCO₃⁻ (~80%) along the proximal tubule … the proximal tubule is a high-capacity, low-gradient system for H⁺ secretion." (p. 825)
- > "Thus, the collecting tubules and ducts are a low-capacity, high-gradient system for H⁺ transport." (p. 825)
- > "In other words, the kidneys reclaim ~99.99% of the filtered HCO₃⁻." (p. 825)
- > "In healthy people, NH₄⁺ excretion is the more important of the two and contributes ~60% of net acid excretion or new HCO₃⁻." (p. 825)

## Section 3 — Acid-base transport at the cellular and molecular level (pp. 827–832)

### Subsection headings
- **H⁺ moves across the apical membrane by Na-H exchange, electrogenic H pumping, and K-H pumping** (pp. 827–828)
- **CAs in the lumen and cytosol stimulate H⁺ secretion by accelerating CO₂/HCO₃⁻ interconversion** (pp. 828–829)
- **HCO₃⁻ efflux across the basolateral membrane takes place by electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransport and Cl-HCO₃ exchange** (p. 829)
- **NH₃ is synthesized by proximal tubules, partly reabsorbed in the loop of Henle, and secreted passively into papillary collecting ducts** (pp. 829–832)

### Core claims
- **The shared three-step cell mechanism.** (1) H⁺ (from H₂O) moves cell→lumen, leaving intracellular OH⁻; (2) CA converts OH⁻ + CO₂ → HCO₃⁻; (3) HCO₃⁻ exits cell→blood. Because filtered buffer power is finite, adequate new-HCO₃⁻ generation requires the kidney to make buffer **de novo** — that buffer is NH₃.
- **Three apical H⁺-exit mechanisms** (not all in any one cell):
  - **NHE3** (Na-H exchanger) — the dominant H⁺ exporter; throughout PT, plus TAL and DCT. Driven by the lumen-to-cell Na⁺ gradient, hence ultimately by the basolateral Na-K pump. Cannot exceed ~1 pH-unit gradient. Stimulated by ANG II and PKC; inhibited by PTH, dopamine, and PKA.
  - **Electrogenic H pump** (vacuolar H⁺-ATPase) — can drive urine pH to ~4.0–5.0; concentrated in apical membrane of **α-intercalated cells** (CNT, ICT, CCT) and IMCD/OMCD cells, also present apically in PT, TAL, DCT, and **basolaterally in β-intercalated cells**. Regulated by transepithelial voltage (aldosterone-driven lumen-negativity), aldosterone directly, and acidosis (recruitment of pumps to the membrane). Mutations → **distal RTA**.
  - **H-K exchange pump** (electroneutral, Na-K-pump-related) — in ICT, CCT, OMCD; minor under normal conditions, but **K⁺ depletion** induces it → retrieves luminal K⁺ and secretes H⁺ → hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.
- **Carbonic anhydrases.** **CA IV** (GPI-anchored, extracellular faces of apical + basolateral membranes, mainly PT) prevents excessive luminal acidification and sustains high PT HCO₃⁻ reabsorption; its inhibition stalls NHE3. **CA II** (cytoplasmic, soluble) supplies H⁺ for apical extrusion and HCO₃⁻ for basolateral exit; present in intercalated cells but **not** principal cells. **CA XII** is a basolateral membrane CA of uncertain role. Acetazolamide (a sulfonamide CA inhibitor) profoundly slows HCO₃⁻ reabsorption → alkaline urine + diuresis.
- **Two basolateral HCO₃⁻-exit mechanisms.** **NBCe1 (SLC4A4)** — electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransporter, highest in S1, carries **3 HCO₃⁻ : 1 Na⁺** (2 net negative charges) cell→blood; DIDS-sensitive; stimulated by chronic acidosis, hypokalemia, hyperfiltration, ANG II, PKC; inhibited by PTH/PKA. NBCe1 mutations → **proximal RTA** (with eye and enamel defects). **Cl-HCO₃ exchangers** — **AE1 (SLC4A1)** in the basolateral membrane of α-intercalated cells (CNT, ICT, CCT); **AE2** in TAL and DCT; AE1 in S3 of the PT.
- **Ammoniagenesis (Fig. 39-5A).** Glutamine enters PT cells (apical + basolateral Na⁺-coupled cotransporters, e.g. SNAT3/SLC38A3). Mitochondrial **glutaminase** → NH₄⁺ + glutamate; **glutamate dehydrogenase** → α-ketoglutarate + second NH₄⁺. Metabolism of **2 glutamines → 4 NH₄⁺ + 2 α-KG**; gluconeogenesis of 2 α-KG (with 4 H⁺) → 1 glucose + **4 new HCO₃⁻**. So **each NH₄⁺ secreted into lumen ↔ one new HCO₃⁻ to blood.**
- **NH₃/NH₄⁺ diffusion trapping.** pK ≈ 9.2 (NH₃:NH₄⁺ = 1:100 at pH 7.2). Neutral **NH₃** diffuses through gas channels (Rh glycoproteins) into the acid lumen and is "trapped" as **NH₄⁺**; apical NHE3 also directly secretes NH₄⁺ (NH₄⁺ substituting for H⁺).
- **Medullary recycling and the countercurrent NH₄⁺ trap.** TAL **reabsorbs** NH₄⁺ (apical Na/K/Cl cotransporter NKCC2 and K⁺ channels, NH₄⁺ substituting for K⁺) → interstitial NH₄⁺ accumulates, building a corticomedullary [NH₄⁺] gradient. Three fates: (1) recycle to PT/tDLH; (2) enter the medullary collecting-duct lumen (NH₃ via basolateral RhBG/RhCG, into lumen via RhCG, trapped as NH₄⁺) — a **cortical bypass** that keeps toxic NH₃ out of the systemic circulation; (3) washout into vasa recta back to the liver.
- **Hepatorenal coupling (Fig. 39-6).** The liver disposes of ~1000 mmol/day of amino groups: ~95% as **urea** (consumes 2 HCO₃⁻ per urea: one –NH₂ from NH₄⁺, one from aspartate, C=O from HCO₃⁻); ~5% as **glutamine** (HCO₃⁻-neutral), which the kidney then uses for ammoniagenesis. To the extent the kidney excretes NH₄⁺, the liver makes less urea and consumes less HCO₃⁻ — the two pathways have **opposite effects on HCO₃⁻**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Of the known NHE isoforms, NHE3 is particularly relevant for the kidney because it moves more H⁺ from tubule cell to lumen than any other transporter." (p. 827)
- > "The ATP-driven H pump can establish steep transepithelial H⁺ concentration gradients, thus lowering the urine pH to ~4.0 to 5.0." (p. 827)
- > "Because, in proximal-tubule cells, this transporter usually transports three HCO₃⁻ ions for each Na⁺ ion, the electrochemical driving forces cause it to carry these ions from cell to blood." (p. 829)
- > "Human mutations that reduce either NBCe1 activity or NBCe1 targeting to the basolateral membrane cause a severe metabolic acidosis—proximal renal tubular acidosis (pRTA)." (p. 829)
- > "Because the pK of the NH₃/NH₄⁺ equilibrium is ~9.2, the NH₃/NH₄⁺ ratio is 1:100 at a pH of 7.2." (p. 829)
- > "For each NH₄⁺ secreted into the tubule lumen, the cell secretes one new HCO₃⁻ into the peritubular fluid." (p. 829)

- > "For each catabolized amino group excreted as urea, the liver consumes the equivalent of one HCO₃⁻. For each … excreted as NH₄⁺ via the glutamine pathway, the proximal tubule produces one new HCO₃⁻." (p. 832)

### Clinical box

**Box 39-1 — Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA)** — reduced ability to eliminate H⁺ or HCO₃⁻ wasting → metabolic acidosis (and, in children, severe developmental impairment).

|---|---|---|

## Section 4 — Regulation of renal acid secretion (pp. 832–835)

### Subsection headings
- **Respiratory acidosis stimulates renal H⁺ secretion** (pp. 832–833)
- **Metabolic acidosis stimulates both proximal H⁺ secretion and NH₃ production** (pp. 833–834)
- **Metabolic alkalosis reduces proximal H⁺ secretion and, in the CCT, may even provoke HCO₃⁻ secretion** (p. 834)
- **A rise in GFR increases HCO₃⁻ delivery, enhancing HCO₃⁻ reabsorption (glomerulotubular balance for HCO₃⁻)** (p. 834)
- **Extracellular volume contraction — via ANG II, aldosterone, and sympathetic activity — stimulates renal H⁺ secretion** (p. 834)
- **Hypokalemia increases renal H⁺ secretion** (pp. 834–835)
- **Both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids stimulate acid secretion** (p. 835)
- **Diuretics can change H⁺ secretion, depending on transepithelial voltage, ECF volume, and plasma [K⁺]** (p. 835)

### Core claims
- **Respiratory acidosis** (↑Pco₂) stimulates H⁺ secretion three ways: (1) acute Pco₂ directly stimulates PT cells (which sense basolateral CO₂) via exocytotic insertion of apical H pumps; (2) acute insertion of H pumps into distal intercalated cells; (3) chronic upregulation of NHE3 and NBCe1 → metabolic compensation.
- **Metabolic acidosis** stimulates proximal H⁺ secretion (PT senses ↓basolateral [HCO₃⁻]; intercalated cells insert apical H pumps via proton-sensing GPCRs + HCO₃⁻-sensitive soluble adenylyl cyclase) **and ammoniagenesis** (↑glutaminase + ↑PEPCK). Chronic acidosis upregulates NHE3 (endothelin-dependent), H pumps, and NBCe1, and shifts the urinary acid load progressively toward **NH₄⁺** (Fig. 39-8). Also ↑Na/citrate cotransport (Fig. 39-7) — defends pH but promotes Ca-containing stones.
- **Metabolic alkalosis** depresses PT H⁺ secretion (cell senses ↑plasma [HCO₃⁻], lowering NHE3 and NBCe1); in the ICT/CCT it can shift the intercalated-cell population toward **β-intercalated cells**, which secrete HCO₃⁻ via apical **pendrin (SLC26A4)** and basolateral H pump. Chronic acidosis shifts the balance the other way, toward α cells.
- **Glomerulotubular balance for HCO₃⁻.** ↑Luminal flow or [HCO₃⁻] raises the microenvironment pH at the brush border, stimulating NHE3/H pumps → more reabsorption. This minimizes HCO₃⁻ loss when GFR rises (and prevents alkalosis when GFR falls); explains compensatory H⁺ transport after uninephrectomy.
- **ECF volume.** Contraction → ↑ANG II, ↑aldosterone, ↑sympathetic (norepinephrine) → all stimulate PT Na-H exchange and distal α-intercalated H⁺ secretion. **Volume regulation takes precedence over pH regulation** (basis of contraction alkalosis).
- **K⁺.** Hypokalemia → intracellular acidification → ↑NHE3, ↑NBCe1, ↑ammoniagenesis, ↑apical K-H exchange → ↑H⁺ secretion (hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis). Hyperkalemia → ↓ammoniagenesis + K⁺ competes with NH₄⁺ at TAL NKCC2 and at medullary Na-K pumps → ↓NH₄⁺ excretion → acidosis.
- **Corticosteroids.** Glucocorticoids ↑PT Na-H exchange and ↓phosphate reabsorption (more buffer for titration). Mineralocorticoids (aldosterone) stimulate H⁺ secretion three ways: directly (apical H pump + basolateral Cl-HCO₃ exchanger in collecting ducts), indirectly via ↑Na⁺ reabsorption → lumen-negativity, and via K⁺ depletion.
- **Diuretics — two groups.** (1) *Alkaline-urine* group: CA inhibitors (inhibit H⁺ secretion) and K⁺-sparing diuretics (amiloride/triamterene block ENaC → less lumen-negativity → harder for H pump; spironolactone blocks aldosterone). (2) *Acidifying / alkalosis-inducing* group: loop diuretics (furosemide → NKCC2) and thiazides (→ NCC) — via volume contraction (↑ANG II/aldosterone), ↑distal Na⁺ delivery (↑lumen-negativity), and K⁺ wasting.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In respiratory acidosis … the compensatory response is an increase in renal H⁺ secretion, which translates to increased production of new HCO₃⁻ via NH₄⁺ excretion." (pp. 832–833)
- > "Indeed, as chronic metabolic acidosis develops, the kidneys progressively excrete a larger fraction of urinary H⁺ as NH₄⁺." (p. 833)
- > "The a-intercalated cells in the ICT and CCT secrete H⁺ by using an apical H pump and a basolateral Cl-HCO₃ exchanger, which is AE1 (SLC4A1)." (p. 834)
- > "The apical Cl-HCO₃ exchanger in β cells is pendrin (SLC26A4)." (p. 834)
- > "Thus, the regulation of effective circulating volume takes precedence over the regulation of plasma pH." (p. 834)
- > "Because a side effect of K⁺ depletion is increased renal H⁺ secretion, K⁺ depletion is frequently associated with metabolic alkalosis." (p. 834)

## Glossary

- **Net acid excretion (NAE)** — titratable acid + NH₄⁺ − excreted HCO₃⁻; equals new HCO₃⁻ generated (~70 mmol/day).
- **Titratable acid (TA)** — base needed to titrate urine back to blood pH; H⁺ on phosphate (pK 6.8, dominant), creatinine, urate; excludes NH₄⁺.
- **Filtered HCO₃⁻ load** — 180 L/day × 24 mM = 4320 mmol/day; ~99.99% reclaimed.
- **NHE3** — apical Na-H exchanger; dominant H⁺ exporter; Na⁺-gradient-driven; max ~1 pH-unit gradient.
- **Electrogenic H pump** — vacuolar H⁺-ATPase; drives urine to pH ~4.0–5.0; α-intercalated apical / β-intercalated basolateral.
- **H-K pump** — electroneutral; minor normally; induced by K⁺ depletion → hypokalemic alkalosis.
- **CA IV / CA II / CA XII** — extracellular (apical+basolateral) / cytosolic / basolateral-membrane carbonic anhydrases.
- **NBCe1 (SLC4A4)** — basolateral electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransporter, 3:1, PT; mutation → proximal RTA.
- **AE1 (SLC4A1) / AE2 / pendrin (SLC26A4)** — Cl-HCO₃ exchangers: α-intercalated basolateral / TAL+DCT basolateral / β-intercalated apical.
- **Ammoniagenesis** — glutamine → 2 NH₄⁺ + α-KG; gluconeogenesis of α-KG → new HCO₃⁻; 1 NH₄⁺ ↔ 1 new HCO₃⁻.
- **NH₃/NH₄⁺ diffusion trapping** — neutral NH₃ enters acid lumen (Rh gas channels) and is trapped as NH₄⁺ (pK ~9.2).
- **Medullary NH₄⁺ recycling / cortical bypass** — TAL reabsorbs NH₄⁺; countercurrent buildup; bypass keeps toxic NH₃ from circulation.
- **α- vs. β-intercalated cells** — acid-secreting (apical H pump, basolateral AE1) vs. base-secreting (apical pendrin, basolateral H pump).
- **Glomerulotubular balance for HCO₃⁻** — ↑flow/[HCO₃⁻] → ↑reabsorption; buffers HCO₃⁻ against GFR changes.
- **Renal tubular acidosis (RTA)** — types 1 (distal), 2 (proximal/NBCe1), 3 (CA II), 4 (hypoaldosterone/hyperkalemic), uremic.
