## Section 1 — Body fluid compartments (pp. 102–104)

### Subsection headings
- **Total body water is distributed among several physical compartments** (pp. 102–103)
- **The barriers that separate body fluid compartments determine the composition of each compartment** (pp. 103–104)
- **The Gibbs–Donnan equilibrium describes the consequences of impermeant charged macromolecules in one compartment** (pp. 103–104)

### Core claims
- **Compartments (Table 5-1, Fig. 5-1):** Total body water (TBW) ≈ 60% of body weight in men, 50% in women. Subdivided:
  - **Intracellular fluid (ICF)** ≈ 60% of TBW (≈ 25 L in a 70 kg adult).
  - **Extracellular fluid (ECF)** ≈ 40% of TBW. ECF further subdivided into:
    - **Interstitial fluid** ≈ 75% of ECF (≈ 13 L).
    - **Plasma** ≈ 20% of ECF (≈ 3 L).
    - **Transcellular fluid** ≈ 5% of ECF (≈ 1 L — CSF, intraocular fluid, lumen of GI tract, etc.).
- **Composition** (typical values, mM):

| Solute | ICF | Plasma | Interstitial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na⁺ | 15 | 142 | 145 |
| K⁺ | 120 | 4.4 | 4.5 |
| Cl⁻ | 20 | 102 | 116 |
| HCO₃⁻ | 16 | 24 | 25 |
| Protein (mEq/L) | 54 | 14 | 1 |
| Osmolality (mOsm/kg) | 290 | 290 | 290 |

- The **plasma membrane** sustains the ICF/ECF gradient by selective permeability + active transport; the **capillary endothelium** is freely permeable to small solutes but not to plasma proteins.
- **Gibbs–Donnan effect**: the presence of impermeant negatively charged proteins in plasma sets up a small asymmetry in diffusible-ion distributions and creates a small osmotic pressure difference (∼1 mOsm); critical for capillary fluid balance.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Total body water is distributed among several physical compartments." (p. 102)
- > "intracellular fluid (ICF) accounts for about 60% of the total body water; the extracellular fluid (ECF) makes up the remainder." (p. 102)
- > "the ECF is also subdivided into several smaller compartments, namely the blood plasma, the interstitial fluid, and the transcellular fluid." (p. 102)
- > "the plasma membrane … is freely permeable to water but selectively permeable to most solutes." (p. 103)
- > "Plasma proteins affect solute distribution because of the volume they occupy and the electrical charge they carry." (p. 103)

## Section 2 — Mechanisms of transport (pp. 104–113)

### Subsection headings
- **Solutes cross membranes by passive diffusion, by mediated transport, or by exocytosis/endocytosis** (pp. 104–105)
- **Passive diffusion follows Fick's law and the membrane partition coefficient** (pp. 105–106)
- **Channels and carriers are distinguished by gating, saturation, and turnover number** (pp. 106–110)
- **Primary active transport uses ATP directly; secondary active transport couples uphill solute movement to an existing ion gradient** (pp. 110–113)

### Core claims
- Three classes of permeation:
  - **Passive (simple) diffusion** through the bilayer — accessible to gases (O₂, CO₂, NO), water (slowly), and small uncharged lipid-soluble molecules.
  - **Mediated transport** — via channels, pores, or carriers (transporters); selective and saturable.
  - **Vesicular transport** — exocytosis/endocytosis; large cargo and bulk membrane traffic.
- **Channels vs. carriers (Table 5-5):**

| Property | Pore / channel | Carrier |
|---|---|---|
| Conduit | continuous aqueous path | alternates between two conformations |
| Throughput (turnover) | 10⁶–10⁸ ions/s | 10²–10⁴ molecules/s |
| Saturation kinetics | weak | strong (Michaelis–Menten) |
| Gating | fast (ligand, voltage, mechanical) | slow conformational |
| Energization | none | passive (uniport) or coupled to ion gradient |
| Coupled transport | not usually | symport or antiport |

- **Primary active transport** = direct ATP hydrolysis on the transporter:
  - **P-type ATPases** (form a phosphorylated intermediate on an Asp): Na⁺/K⁺ pump, gastric H⁺/K⁺ pump, SERCA Ca²⁺ pump, PMCA Ca²⁺ pump, Cu²⁺ pumps (Menkes ATP7A, Wilson ATP7B), H⁺ pump.
  - **V-type ATPases** (vacuolar): acidify lysosomes, secretory vesicles, intercalated-cell apical pumping.
  - **F-type ATPases** (mitochondrial ATP synthase; same architecture as V-type, runs in reverse).
  - **ABC transporters** (Table 5-6): CFTR (Cl⁻ channel that is also ATP-binding), MDR1/P-glycoprotein (drug efflux), TAP (peptide transport for MHC I), MRP family, ABC1 (cholesterol efflux in Tangier disease).
- **Secondary active transport** couples uphill movement of one solute to downhill movement of another (most often Na⁺ down its electrochemical gradient set up by the Na/K pump):
  - **Symport (cotransport)** — solutes move in the same direction (e.g., SGLT Na⁺/glucose; NKCC1/2 Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻; NCC Na⁺/Cl⁻; PepT1 H⁺/peptide; NaPi-IIa Na⁺/PO₄³⁻).
  - **Antiport (exchange)** — solutes move in opposite directions (e.g., NHE Na⁺/H⁺; NCX Na⁺/Ca²⁺ — 3 Na⁺ in, 1 Ca²⁺ out; AE Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻; OCT/OAT organic cation/anion exchangers).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Solutes cross the plasma membrane by passive diffusion, by mediated transport, or by exocytosis or endocytosis." (p. 104)
- > "Channels and carriers can be distinguished by considering the kinetics of transport. Carriers exhibit saturation kinetics, but channels do not." (p. 106)
- > "Active transport requires the input of energy. In primary active transport, the energy comes directly from the hydrolysis of ATP. In secondary active transport, the energy is provided by an electrochemical gradient." (p. 110)
- > "The Na-K pump is the most thoroughly studied P-type ATPase and is the prototypical primary active transporter." (p. 111)
- > "The F-type and V-type ATPases transport H⁺." (p. 113)

## Section 3 — Energetics of transport (pp. 113–117)

### Subsection headings
- **The electrochemical potential difference is the driving force for ion transport** (pp. 113–114)
- **The Nernst equation gives the equilibrium potential for a single ion** (p. 114)
- **The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation gives the resting membrane potential when several ions are permeant** (pp. 115–116)
- **The Ussing flux-ratio equation distinguishes passive from active transport** (p. 116)

### Equations

- **Fick's first law for diffusion** across a membrane:

$$J_X = -D_X \, A \, \frac{d[X]}{dx} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad J_X = P_X \, A \, \big([X]_o - [X]_i\big)$$

with $P_X = D_X \, \beta_X / \Delta x$ (membrane permeability, $\beta_X$ = partition coefficient).

- **Electrochemical potential** for an ion $X$ with charge $z$:

$$\bar{\mu}_X = \mu_X^{\circ} + RT \ln [X] + zF\psi$$

- **Nernst equilibrium potential** (no net flux of $X$, $\Delta\bar{\mu}_X = 0$):

$$E_X = \frac{RT}{zF} \, \ln \frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i} \;\approx\; \frac{61.5\,\text{mV}}{z} \, \log_{10} \frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i} \quad (\text{at } 37^\circ\text{C})$$

Worked example values for a typical mammalian cell (Table 5-3 territory):

| Ion | $[X]_o$ (mM) | $[X]_i$ (mM) | $E_X$ (mV) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na⁺ | 145 | 15 | +61 |
| K⁺ | 4.5 | 120 | −88 |
| Cl⁻ | 116 | 20 | −47 |
| Ca²⁺ | 1.2 | 10⁻⁴ | +125 |
| H⁺ (pH 7.4 / 7.1) | 4 × 10⁻⁵ | 8 × 10⁻⁵ | −18 |

- **Electrochemical driving force on an ion** at membrane potential $V_m$:

$$\Delta\bar{\mu}_X / F = (V_m - E_X) \quad \text{(units: mV; sign convention: positive means inward driving on cations)}$$

- **Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation** for $V_m$ when Na⁺, K⁺, and Cl⁻ are the dominant permeant ions:

$$V_m = \frac{RT}{F} \, \ln \frac{P_K [\mathrm{K^+}]_o + P_{Na}[\mathrm{Na^+}]_o + P_{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_i}{P_K[\mathrm{K^+}]_i + P_{Na}[\mathrm{Na^+}]_i + P_{Cl}[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_o}$$

(Note the inverted positions of Cl⁻ because of its negative charge.)

- **Ussing flux-ratio equation** (a flux is passive iff the ratio of unidirectional fluxes obeys):

$$\frac{J_X^{o \to i}}{J_X^{i \to o}} = \frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i} \, e^{- z F V_m / RT}$$

Departures of measured flux ratio from this prediction prove active transport of $X$.

- **Donnan equilibrium** with impermeant macromolecule $P^{n-}$ at concentration $[P]$:

  Two-compartment equilibrium across a membrane permeable to a cation $C^+$ and an anion $A^-$ but not to $P$. At equilibrium:

  $$\frac{[C^+]_L}{[C^+]_R} = \frac{[A^-]_R}{[A^-]_L} \quad (\text{Donnan ratio})$$

  with electroneutrality and impermeance constraints. Worked example in Figure 5-15.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The electrochemical potential difference for an ion is the driving force for net ion movement." (p. 113)
- > "If the electrochemical potential difference for an ion is zero, the ion is at equilibrium. The membrane potential at which this occurs is called the Nernst equilibrium potential for that ion." (p. 114)
- > "When several ions can move across the membrane, the resting membrane potential is given by the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation." (p. 115)
- > "The Ussing flux-ratio equation provides a test for active transport." (p. 116)

## Section 4 — Pumps and carriers in detail (pp. 117–124)

### Subsection headings
- **The Na-K pump is the prototypical P-type ATPase** (pp. 117–119)
- **Other P-type ATPases transport H⁺, Ca²⁺, H⁺/K⁺, and heavy metals** (pp. 119–120)
- **The F-type and V-type ATPases transport H⁺** (pp. 120–121)
- **ABC transporters move a wide variety of substrates using ATP** (pp. 121–123)
- **Secondary active transporters: cotransporters and exchangers** (pp. 123–124)

### Core claims — Na/K pump (Na⁺,K⁺-ATPase)
- Stoichiometry: **3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in / 1 ATP hydrolyzed**. Net 1 positive charge expelled per cycle → electrogenic.
- Subunits: catalytic **α** (binds ATP, Na⁺, K⁺, ouabain) + **β** (chaperone / membrane insertion / regulation) + sometimes **γ (FXYD)**.
- Cycle (Post–Albers): E₁(Na₃) inward-open → ATP phosphorylates Asp → E₁P-(Na₃) occluded → E₂P-(Na₃) outward-open → 3 Na⁺ released → 2 K⁺ bind → E₂(K₂) occluded → dephosphorylation → 2 K⁺ released into cytoplasm.
- Inhibitors: **cardiac glycosides (ouabain, digoxin)** bind the extracellular E₂P state. Digoxin's positive inotropic effect in heart failure follows from ↓ Na/K → ↑ [Na⁺]ᵢ → ↓ NCX-mediated Ca²⁺ extrusion → ↑ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ in cardiomyocytes (Ch 22).
- Roles: maintains low [Na⁺]ᵢ and high [K⁺]ᵢ (basis of resting potential), provides the Na⁺ gradient that powers nearly all secondary active transport, regulates cell volume (see § 5).

### Core claims — other P-type pumps
- **SERCA** — sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase. 2 Ca²⁺ pumped into the ER/SR per ATP; inhibited by thapsigargin. Phospholamban regulation in cardiac SR.
- **PMCA** — plasma-membrane Ca²⁺-ATPase. 1 Ca²⁺ extruded per ATP; calmodulin-stimulated.
- **H⁺/K⁺-ATPase** — gastric parietal cell apical pump; secretes H⁺ into lumen; **omeprazole/PPI** target.
- **ATP7A/B** — Cu²⁺ pumps; mutations cause **Menkes disease** and **Wilson disease**, respectively.

### Core claims — F- and V-type ATPases
- Both share the same architecture: an integral-membrane rotor (V₀ or F₀) coupled to a soluble cytoplasmic head (V₁ or F₁) by a stalk.
- **F-ATP synthase (mitochondrial inner membrane)**: H⁺ gradient drives rotation → ADP + Pi → ATP.
- **V-ATPase**: ATP hydrolysis drives H⁺ pumping into lysosomes, endosomes, secretory vesicles; in α-intercalated cells of the renal collecting duct and in osteoclasts, V-ATPase is on the plasma membrane.

### Core claims — ABC transporters
- **CFTR** — Cl⁻ channel uniquely gated by ATP-binding/hydrolysis at its two NBDs. **Cystic fibrosis** is caused by loss-of-function mutations (ΔF508 is most common). Targeted potentiators (ivacaftor) and correctors (lumacaftor/elexacaftor combinations) restore function.
- **MDR1 / P-glycoprotein** — broad-spectrum drug efflux; intestinal epithelium and BBB → limits oral bioavailability of many drugs.
- **MRP family** — organic anion efflux.
- **ABC1** — cholesterol efflux to apoA-I; Tangier disease.
- **TAP1/2** — peptide loading of MHC class I.

### Core claims — secondary active transport canonical examples
- **NHE1** — ubiquitous Na⁺/H⁺ exchange; cell pH regulation and volume regulation.
- **NHE3** — apical Na⁺/H⁺ exchange in renal proximal tubule and intestine; bulk Na⁺ reabsorption.
- **NCX** — 3 Na⁺ in / 1 Ca²⁺ out; cardiomyocyte calcium extrusion.
- **NKCC1 (basolateral)** and **NKCC2 (apical, thick ascending limb)** — 1 Na⁺ / 1 K⁺ / 2 Cl⁻ cotransport. **Loop diuretics (furosemide)** target NKCC2.
- **NCC** — Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransport, distal convoluted tubule. **Thiazide** target.
- **SGLT1/2** — Na⁺/glucose. SGLT2 inhibitors (gliflozins) target proximal tubule reabsorption.
- **AE1 / SLC4A1** — Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger; erythrocyte and basolateral renal type-A intercalated cell.
- **PepT1/2** — H⁺/peptide cotransport.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The Na-K pump is the most thoroughly studied P-type ATPase and is the prototypical primary active transporter." (p. 117)
- > "The Na-K pump exchanges three Na⁺ for two K⁺ for each ATP molecule hydrolyzed." (p. 117)
- > "The F-type and V-type ATPases transport H⁺." (p. 120)
- > "ABC transporters move a wide variety of substrates using ATP." (p. 121)
- > "Cell volume changes trigger rapid changes in ion channels or transporters, returning volume toward normal." (p. 130)

## Section 5 — Water transport and osmosis (pp. 124–131)

### Subsection headings
- **Osmotic pressure depends on the number of dissolved particles, not their identity** (pp. 124–125)
- **The van 't Hoff equation gives the osmotic pressure of an ideal dilute solution** (pp. 125–126)
- **Real biological membranes have intermediate reflection coefficients for most solutes** (pp. 126–127)
- **Water moves through aquaporins** (pp. 127–128)
- **Cells regulate their volume by adjusting solute content** (pp. 128–131)

### Equations

- **van 't Hoff equation** for an ideal dilute solute:

  $$\pi = i \, C \, R \, T$$

  with $\pi$ the osmotic pressure (atm), $i$ the number of dissociated particles per solute molecule, $C$ the molar concentration, $R$ the gas constant, $T$ in Kelvin. Equivalently in mOsm:

  $$\pi \approx (\text{mOsm/kg}) \times R T \times 10^{-3}$$

  At 37 °C, 1 mOsm/kg corresponds to ~19.3 mm Hg of osmotic pressure.

- **Reflection coefficient** $\sigma$ (Staverman) — accounts for non-ideal membranes that pass some solute:

  $$\pi_{\text{eff}} = \sigma \, R T \, \Delta C$$

  $\sigma = 1$ → perfectly impermeant solute (full osmotic effect, "effective osmole"); $\sigma = 0$ → membrane is fully permeant to the solute (no osmotic effect, e.g., urea across many membranes). Table 5-2 in source.

- **Water flux through a membrane**:

  $$J_v = L_p \, A \, (\Delta P - \sigma \, R T \Delta C)$$

  $L_p$ = hydraulic conductivity; $\Delta P$ = hydrostatic pressure difference; the bracket is the Starling-like net driving force. This is the founding equation for the **Starling principle** in Section IV (capillary fluid balance).

### Core claims
- **Osmolality** ≈ 290 mOsm/kg in plasma; matched on both sides of the plasma membrane at steady state.
- **Tonicity** = effective osmolality across the plasma membrane. Urea has $\sigma \approx 0.6$ in most membranes (often less in renal medulla), so a urea-rich solution can be isosmotic but hypotonic (cells swell). Worked example in Fig. 5-18.
- **Aquaporins (AQP1–13)** are water-selective channels; AQP1 is constitutive in RBCs and proximal tubule, AQP2 is vasopressin-regulated in renal collecting duct, AQP0 is in lens, AQP4 in CNS perivascular astrocyte foot processes. AQPs raise membrane $P_f$ by orders of magnitude.
- **Cell volume regulation:** acute swelling → **regulatory volume decrease (RVD)** via K⁺ + Cl⁻ + organic-osmolyte efflux; acute shrinkage → **regulatory volume increase (RVI)** via NHE1 + AE2 + NKCC1 driving NaCl uptake. Chronic adaptation uses organic osmolytes (sorbitol, inositol, betaine, taurine) — critical in renal medulla.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Osmotic pressure is determined by the total concentration of dissolved particles, regardless of their chemical nature." (p. 124)
- > "The reflection coefficient describes the relative effectiveness of an osmole in causing water movement." (p. 126)
- > "Cells regulate their volume by adjusting their osmolyte content." (p. 128)

## Section 6 — Transport across epithelia (pp. 131–140)

### Subsection headings
- **Epithelial cells achieve vectorial transport by polarizing transporters between apical and basolateral membranes** (pp. 131–134)
- **Transepithelial transport may be transcellular or paracellular** (pp. 134–135)
- **The transepithelial voltage and resistance reflect a circuit of cellular and paracellular pathways** (pp. 135–137)
- **Water transport across epithelia follows the solute gradient** (pp. 138–140)

### Core claims
- The textbook formalism (Ussing model): an epithelial cell has an **apical (mucosal/luminal)** membrane and a **basolateral (serosal)** membrane, each with its own complement of channels and transporters, connected to neighboring cells by **tight junctions** at the apical edge.
- **Transcellular** transport requires entry across one membrane and exit across the other; in a polarized cell, the two membranes have different selectivity so net flux can be vectorial.
- **Paracellular** transport bypasses cells through the tight junctions; selectivity is set by claudin composition (e.g., claudin-2 makes leaky proximal-tubule junctions; claudin-16 specifically allows Mg²⁺ in the thick ascending limb — mutations cause FHHNC).
- **Transepithelial potential difference (TEPD)** and **transepithelial resistance (TER)** define epithelium as **tight** (high TER, high TEPD; e.g., distal nephron, urinary bladder) vs. **leaky** (low TER, low TEPD; e.g., proximal tubule, gallbladder).
- **Isosmotic water absorption** (e.g., proximal tubule, small intestine, gallbladder): vectorial Na⁺ transport creates a local hyperosmolality in the lateral intercellular space → water follows osmotically through AQPs and the paracellular route → isosmotic absorbate.

### Equations

- **Ussing short-circuit current ($I_{sc}$):** when the transepithelial voltage is clamped to 0 and chemical gradients are absent, the measured net charge flux equals the sum of all active ionic transport currents:

  $$I_{sc} = F \, \sum_i z_i \, J_i^{\text{active}}$$

  This is the experimental gold standard for quantifying active transepithelial transport.

- **Equivalent circuit** of an epithelium:

  $$V_{\text{te}} = \frac{E_{ap} R_{bl} R_{para} + E_{bl} R_{ap} R_{para} - E_{para} R_{ap} R_{bl}}{R_{ap} R_{bl} + R_{ap} R_{para} + R_{bl} R_{para}}$$

  where $E$ and $R$ are EMF and resistance of each pathway (apical, basolateral, paracellular). Algebra is rarely needed clinically but is the basis for how nephrologists reason about leaky vs. tight epithelia.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Epithelial cells achieve vectorial transport because their apical and basolateral membranes contain different transport proteins." (p. 131)
- > "Transepithelial transport may proceed by transcellular or paracellular pathways." (p. 134)
- > "Water transport across an epithelium follows the solute gradient set up by active transepithelial solute transport." (p. 138)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **TBW / ICF / ECF / interstitial / transcellular / plasma** — fluid compartments.
- **Osmolality / osmolarity / tonicity / reflection coefficient $\sigma$** — water-driving descriptors.
- **van 't Hoff equation** — $\pi = iCRT$.
- **Donnan equilibrium / Donnan ratio** — impermeant-charged-macromolecule asymmetry.
- **Fick's law** — passive flux $\propto$ concentration gradient.
- **Permeability $P$ vs hydraulic conductivity $L_p$** — solute vs. water transit parameters.
- **Pore / channel / carrier** — turnover and saturation distinctions.
- **Nernst equation / $E_X$** — equilibrium potential.
- **GHK equation** — resting potential with multiple permeant ions.
- **Ussing flux-ratio** — passive-vs.-active test.
- **Driving force $V_m - E_X$** — sign of net ion flux at a given $V_m$.
- **Primary active transport / secondary active transport** — direct ATP vs. coupled-gradient energy.
- **P-type / V-type / F-type / ABC ATPases** — energy-coupled transporter classes.
- **Na/K pump (Na⁺,K⁺-ATPase)** — 3 Na⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in / 1 ATP. Ouabain target.
- **SERCA / PMCA / NCX** — Ca²⁺ pumps and exchanger.
- **NHE / NCC / NKCC / SGLT / GLUT / AE / PepT / NaPi-IIa** — secondary active transporter classes.
- **CFTR / MDR1 / ABC1 / TAP** — ABC transporters.
- **Aquaporin (AQP0–13)** — water channels; AQP2 is vasopressin-regulated.
- **RVD / RVI** — regulatory volume decrease / increase.
- **Transcellular vs. paracellular transport** — epithelial routes.
- **Tight vs. leaky epithelium** — defined by TEPD and TER.
- **Ussing chamber / $I_{sc}$** — gold-standard active-transport measurement.
- **Isosmotic absorption** — water follows actively transported solute.
- **Tonicity vs. osmolality** — urea example shows the distinction.
