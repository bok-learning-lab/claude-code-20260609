## Section 1 — Functional anatomy of the stomach (pp. 863–865)

### Subsection headings
- **The mucosa is composed of surface epithelial cells and glands** (pp. 863–864)
- **With increasing rates of secretion of gastric juice, the H⁺ concentration rises and the Na⁺ concentration falls** (p. 864)
- **The proximal portion of the stomach secretes acid, pepsinogens, intrinsic factor, bicarbonate, and mucus, whereas the distal part releases gastrin and somatostatin** (pp. 864–865)
- **The stomach accommodates food, mixes it with gastric secretions, grinds it, and empties the chyme into the duodenum** (p. 865)

### Core claims
- **Gross segments**: (1) **cardia** — just distal to the gastroesophageal junction, devoid of parietal cells; (2) **body/corpus** (most proximal part = **fundus**) — the largest portion, the acid-secreting region; (3) **antrum** — distal, no parietal cells. Gastric **glands** consist of a pit, neck, and base.
- **Two gland classes** map onto two functional zones:
  - **Oxyntic (fundic/corpus) gland mucosa** — contains **parietal (oxyntic) cells**, **chief (peptic) cells**, **mucous neck cells**, and endocrine cells including **ECL cells**. Secretes acid, pepsinogens, intrinsic factor, HCO₃, mucus.
  - **Pyloric (antral) gland mucosa** — **no parietal cells**, hence no acid or intrinsic factor; contains chief cells, mucous cells, and endocrine **G cells** (gastrin) and **D cells** (somatostatin).
- **Cell-type roles**:
  - **Parietal (oxyntic) cell** — secretes both **acid (HCl)** and **intrinsic factor** (glycoprotein required for cobalamin/vitamin B₁₂ absorption in the ileum). Large triangular cell, central nucleus, abundant mitochondria, intracellular tubulovesicular membranes and canalicular structures.
  - **Chief (peptic) cell** — secretes **pepsinogens** (not acid); much smaller than parietal cells.
  - **Mucous neck cells** — secrete mucus (and group II pepsinogens), confined to the neck of the gland.
  - **ECL (enterochromaffin-like) cell** — releases **histamine** (the central amplifier of acid secretion).
  - **G cell** (antrum, duodenum) — releases **gastrin**.
  - **D cell** (antrum, corpus) — releases **somatostatin**.
  - **Surface epithelial cells** — secrete **HCO₃ and mucus** (the protective barrier).
- **~2 L/day** of gastric juice, approximately isotonic with plasma. Secretion has two components: an **Na⁺-rich basal (nonparietal) secretion** and a **stimulated, H⁺-rich pure parietal-cell secretion**.
- **Inverse [H⁺]/[Na⁺] relationship** (Fig. 42-2): at high secretory rate (gastrin/histamine stimulation) luminal [H⁺] is high and [Na⁺] low; at low rate (or in pernicious anemia, where parietal cells are lost) [H⁺] is low and [Na⁺] high. Cl⁻ stays high; K⁺ stays low throughout.
- **Two humoral agents** — gastrin and somatostatin — have both endocrine and paracrine actions and are the principal regulators of gastric secretion. **Gastrin** is also a major trophic/growth factor for GI epithelial proliferation.
- The stomach is **not required for survival**: total gastrectomy for non-neoplastic disease is compatible with adequate nutrition and good longevity.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Parietal cells (or oxyntic cells) secrete both acid and intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein that is required for cobalamin (vitamin B₁₂) absorption in the ileum." (p. 864)
- > "Chief cells (or peptic cells) secrete pepsinogens, but not acid." (p. 864)
- > "The glands in the antrum of the stomach do not contain parietal cells. Therefore, the antrum does not secrete either acid or intrinsic factor." (p. 865)
- > "gastric secretion consists of (1) an Na⁺-rich basal secretion that originates from nonparietal cells, and (2) a stimulated component that represents a pure parietal-cell secretion that is rich in H⁺." (p. 864)
- > "its primary role in gastric physiology is to inhibit both gastrin release and parietal-cell acid secretion." (p. 865) — on somatostatin.

## Section 2 — Acid secretion (pp. 865–871)

### Subsection headings
- **The parietal cell has a specialized tubulovesicular structure that increases apical membrane area when the cell is stimulated to secrete acid** (p. 865)
- **An H-K pump is responsible for gastric acid secretion by parietal cells** (pp. 865–866)
- **The key step in gastric acid secretion is extrusion of H⁺ into the lumen of the gastric gland in exchange for K⁺** (pp. 866)
- **Three secretagogues (acetylcholine, gastrin, and histamine) directly and indirectly induce acid secretion by parietal cells** (pp. 866–867)
- **The three acid secretagogues act through either Ca²⁺/diacylglycerol or cAMP** (p. 867)
- **Antral and duodenal G cells release gastrin, whereas ECL cells in the corpus release histamine** (pp. 867–868)
- **Gastric D cells release somatostatin, the central inhibitor of acid secretion** (pp. 868–870)
- **Several enteric hormones ("enterogastrone") and prostaglandins inhibit gastric acid secretion** (p. 870)
- **A meal triggers three phases of acid secretion** (pp. 870–872)

### Core claims — the parietal cell and the H-K pump
- **Canalicular recruitment**: in the resting cell, the H-K pump sits in **tubulovesicular membranes**. On stimulation, cytoskeletal rearrangement **fuses these tubulovesicles into the canalicular (apical) membrane**, raising apical surface area **50- to 100-fold** and creating microvilli. Fusion co-inserts the **H-K pumps plus K⁺ and Cl⁻ channels** into the canalicular membrane.
- **The gastric proton pump (H,K-ATPase)** is a **P-type ATPase** (same family as the ubiquitous Na,K-ATPase). It is an **α/β heterodimer**: catalysis resides in the **α subunit**; the **β subunit** targets the pump to the apical membrane.
- **Pump pharmacology / specificity**:
  - **Substituted benzimidazoles (PPIs, e.g., omeprazole)** bind **covalently to cysteines on the extracytoplasmic surface** → potent, essentially irreversible inhibition of the gastric H-K pump. Highly effective in normal subjects and hypersecretory states.
  - A second class are **competitive inhibitors of the K⁺-binding site** (e.g., experimental Schering 28080).
  - **Ouabain** (Na,K-ATPase inhibitor) does **not** block the gastric H-K pump, and **omeprazole does not block the Na,K-ATPase** — confirming the pumps are distinct.
- **Net transport at the canalicular membrane** (Fig. 42-4):
  - Apical **H,K-ATPase** extrudes **H⁺** into the lumen in **exchange for K⁺**; the K⁺ is **recycled** back to the lumen through apical **KvLQT1 K⁺ channels**.
  - **Cl⁻** exits to the lumen through apical **CFTR** (and possibly ClC) channels. Net apical product = **HCl**.
  - The H⁺ comes from **carbonic anhydrase (CA)**: as acid leaves, intracellular pH rises, CO₂ and H₂O are taken up and CA converts them to H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; H⁺ feeds the pump.
  - **HCO₃⁻ exits the basolateral membrane** via the **Cl-HCO₃ exchanger AE2 (SLC4A2)** — this efflux of base into the blood is the **"alkaline tide"** that accompanies a meal. AE2 also imports some of the **Cl⁻** needed for apical HCl. Additional basolateral Cl⁻ entry: **Na/K/Cl cotransporter NKCC1** and electrogenic **SLC26A7**.
  - Basolateral **Na,K-ATPase** maintains gradients; basolateral **Na-H exchanger** assists pH_i regulation, mainly in the basal state.

### Equations
- **Gastric proton-pump stoichiometry** (electroneutral H⁺/K⁺ exchange driven by ATP):

  $$\text{H}^+_{\text{cyt}} + \text{K}^+_{\text{lumen}} + \text{ATP} \;\rightleftharpoons\; \text{H}^+_{\text{lumen}} + \text{K}^+_{\text{cyt}} + \text{ADP} + \text{P}_i$$

  Two H⁺ are pumped per ATP in exchange for 2 K⁺ — an electroneutral, ouabain-insensitive P-type pump.

- **Net acid generation in the parietal cell** (CA supplies H⁺; HCO₃⁻ leaves basolaterally as the alkaline tide):

  $$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{CA}} \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$$

  apical: $\text{H}^+ + \text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{HCl (lumen)}$;  basolateral (AE2): $\text{HCO}_3^- \;\rightarrow\; \text{blood}$ (in exchange for Cl⁻).

- **Maintained H⁺ gradient** (Protection section, but a defining parietal-cell number): luminal pH ≈ 1 → [H⁺] ≳ 100 mM vs. cell pH ~7.2 ([H⁺] ≈ 60 nM):

  $$\frac{[\text{H}^+]_{\text{lumen}}}{[\text{H}^+]_{\text{cell}}} \approx \frac{10^{-1}}{10^{-7.2}} > 10^{6}$$

  i.e., a **>1,000,000-fold** transmembrane H⁺ gradient.

### Core claims — the three secretagogues converging on the parietal cell
All three bind **G protein–coupled receptors** on the parietal-cell basolateral membrane and act through **two parallel (partly redundant) routes**: a **direct** action on the parietal cell and an **indirect** action via histamine release from ECL cells (Figs. 42-5, 42-6).

| Secretagogue | Source | Parietal-cell receptor | G protein | Second messenger / effectors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Acetylcholine (ACh)** | vagal (CN X) postganglionic endings | **M₃ muscarinic** | Gα_q | PLC → IP₃ (↑Ca²⁺ via ER + a Ca²⁺ channel) + DAG → PKC; Ca²⁺/calmodulin kinase |
| **Gastrin** | antral & duodenal **G cells** | **CCK₂ (= CCK_B / "gastrin") receptor** | Gα_q | PLC → IP₃/DAG → ↑Ca²⁺ + PKC (same pathway as ACh) |
| **Histamine** | corpus **ECL cells** (paracrine) | **H₂ receptor** | Gα_s | **adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA** → phosphorylates parietal proteins incl. the H-K pump |

- **Histamine/H₂/cAMP is the central amplifier.** ACh and gastrin act not only directly on the parietal cell but **also stimulate ECL cells to release histamine** (the indirect pathway). This explains why **H₂ blockers (cimetidine, ranitidine)** suppress acid secretion stimulated by ACh and gastrin too — not just by histamine.
- **CCK receptor distinction**: **CCK₁ (CCK_A)** has equal affinity for gastrin and CCK; **CCK₂ (CCK_B)** binds CCK ~1000× more avidly than gastrin but is the **parietal-cell gastrin receptor**. Gastrin and CCK share an **identical C-terminal tetrapeptide** carrying full biological activity.
- **Gastrin forms** (Fig. 42-7): **G-17 ("little gastrin")** — antral, the main acid stimulant; **G-34 ("big gastrin")** — predominantly duodenal, slower-degraded. Post-translational **C-terminal amidation** and **N-terminal pyroglutamyl** modification make gastrin resistant to peptidases. Tyrosine may be **sulfated (gastrin II)** or not (**gastrin I**); both equally active. Gastrin's three effects: stimulate parietal acid, release ECL histamine, and trophic mucosal growth.

### Core claims — the somatostatin (D-cell) brake
- **Somatostatin** (predominant GI form **SS-28**; also SS-14) from **D cells** is the **central inhibitor** of acid secretion, acting by **direct + two indirect (paracrine)** routes (Fig. 42-8):
  - **Direct**: somatostatin binds a **Gα_i-coupled SST receptor** on the parietal cell → **inhibits adenylyl cyclase** → antagonizes histamine's cAMP signal.
  - **Indirect (corpus)**: D-cell somatostatin **inhibits histamine release from ECL cells**.
  - **Indirect (antrum)**: D-cell somatostatin **inhibits gastrin release from G cells**.
- **D-cell triggers differ by region**: **antral** D cells are stimulated by **low luminal pH** (the negative-feedback sensor); **corpus** D cells cannot sense luminal pH and are driven neurally/hormonally. **Gastrin stimulates somatostatin release** (a built-in negative feedback); **cholinergic (vagal/ACh) input inhibits** somatostatin release, thereby disinhibiting acid secretion.
- **Antral G cell is an "open-type" endocrine cell** with apical microvilli: it senses **luminal peptides/amino acids (peptones)** and basolateral **gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP)** from vagal/ENS endings. **Intact proteins have no effect**; only digested peptones release gastrin.

### Core claims — enterogastrones and prostaglandins (the duodenal brake)
- **Fat > acid > hyperosmolar** solutions in the duodenum/jejunum inhibit gastric acid (negative feedback). Candidate "enterogastrones" (Table 42-2): **CCK, secretin, VIP, GIP, neurotensin, peptide YY, somatostatin** — none singly proven as the sole mediator.
- **Secretin** (duodenal **S cells**, triggered by acid/fat) inhibits acid by three routes: ↓antral gastrin release, ↑somatostatin release, and direct downregulation of parietal H⁺ secretion.
- **GIP** (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) inhibits parietal acid directly and antral gastrin indirectly, and stimulates insulin release. **CCK** directly reduces parietal acid.
- **Prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂)** binds an **EP₃ receptor (Gα_i)** on the parietal cell → inhibits adenylyl cyclase (distal to the histamine receptor); also reduces ECL histamine and G-cell gastrin release. (PGE₂ is also a major mucosal-protective agent — see Section 4.)

### Core claims — the three phases of acid secretion
- **Basal/interdigestive**: low, circadian (lowest pre-dawn, highest evening); proportional to parietal-cell mass (men > women). Resting intragastric pH ranges 3–7.
- **Cephalic phase (~30% of meal-stimulated acid)**: smell/sight/taste/thought/swallowing → **dorsal motor nucleus of vagus** → four vagal effects: (1) ACh → parietal M₃ (direct); (2) ACh → ECL histamine; (3) GRP → antral G-cell gastrin; (4) vagal inhibition of D cells → ↓somatostatin (disinhibition). Occurs **before food enters the stomach**. **Vagotomy** (rarely used now) blocks this; side effects include delayed emptying and diarrhea.
- **Gastric phase (~50–60%)**: food entry → (1) **distention** activates a **vagovagal reflex** + local ENS reflexes (same four vagal pathways) and (2) **peptones** directly stimulate **antral G cells** → gastrin. Positive-feedback loop: acid → pepsin activation → more peptones → more gastrin. Low antral pH → D-cell somatostatin → ↓gastrin (the negative brake). Carbohydrate and lipid do **not** drive acid; wine/beer/coffee components do.
- **Intestinal phase (~5–10%)**: peptones in proximal small intestine → (1) **duodenal G-cell gastrin**, (2) release of putative **"entero-oxyntin,"** (3) absorbed amino acids. Enhanced after **portacaval shunt** (the intestinal signal bypasses hepatic clearance).

#### Figure 42-8 — Regulation of gastric acid secretion *(viewed)*
The chapter's master integration figure, split **corpus (top) / antrum (bottom)**, threaded by the **vagus nerve**.
- **Corpus**: vagal ACh acts (i) **directly** on the parietal cell (M₃); (ii) on **ECL cells** → **histamine** → parietal H₂ (amplification); and (iii) on **D cells**, *inhibiting* somatostatin release — which otherwise paracrine-inhibits both ECL histamine and parietal acid. So vagal input both pushes acid and lifts the somatostatin brake.
- **Antrum**: vagus stimulates **G cells via GRP** → **gastrin**, which raises acid both directly (parietal CCK₂) and indirectly (ECL histamine). Vagal ACh also **inhibits antral D cells** (↓somatostatin → disinhibits gastrin). Two feedback arms are explicit: **luminal H⁺ → antral D-cell somatostatin → ↓gastrin** (negative feedback); **peptones/amino acids → G cell → gastrin** (positive feedback).

## Section 3 — Pepsinogen secretion (pp. 872–874)

### Subsection headings
- **Chief cells, triggered by both cAMP and Ca²⁺ pathways, secrete multiple pepsinogens that initiate protein digestion** (pp. 872–873)
- **Low pH is required for both pepsinogen activation and pepsin activity** (pp. 873–874)

### Core claims
- **Pepsinogens** are **aspartic-proteinase zymogens** secreted by **chief cells** (and mucous neck cells). Classified as **group I** (corpus chief cells; predominate), **group II** (chief + mucous neck cells, cardia/corpus/antrum), and **cathepsin E**. Activated to **pepsins** (endopeptidases) by cleavage of an N-terminal **activation peptide**.
- **Two agonist classes** drive chief-cell secretion:
  - **cAMP arm** — secretin/VIP, **β-adrenergic**, and **EP₂ (PGE₂)** receptors → adenylyl cyclase. (At low concentration PGE₂ can also *inhibit* via another receptor.)
  - **Ca²⁺ arm** — **M₃ muscarinic (ACh)** and **CCK₁ (= CCK_A)** receptors → IP₃ → ↑[Ca²⁺]_i. Note the **switch vs. parietal cells**: acid is driven by the **CCK₂** receptor, but **pepsinogen by the CCK₁** receptor (higher affinity for CCK than gastrin).
- **ACh (vagal) is the single most important pepsinogen secretagogue**, conveniently coupling pepsinogen output to the acid output that activates it.
- **Compound exocytosis**: secretory granules fuse with both the plasma membrane and with one another → rapid, sustained release. Basal pepsinogen ≈ **20% of maximal**.
- **pH dependence** (Fig. 42-12):
  - Activation is **slow at pH 3–5**, **very rapid at pH < 3** (and **autocatalytic** — pepsin cleaves pepsinogen).
  - Pepsin **activity optimum ~pH 1.8–3.5**; **reversibly inactivated > pH 3.5**, **irreversibly inactivated > pH 7.2** — the basis of acid-suppression therapy and of mucus/HCO₃ protection.
- The peptones pepsin produces feed back to **stimulate the very acid secretion required for pepsin activation** (positive-feedback). Nitrogen balance is preserved after total gastrectomy, so gastric proteolysis is not essential.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Pepsins are endopeptidases that initiate the hydrolysis of ingested protein in the stomach." (p. 873)
- > "Release of pepsinogen across the apical membrane is the result of a novel process called compound exocytosis, in which secretory granules fuse with both the plasma membrane and other secretory granules." (p. 873)
- > "Unlike gastric acid secretion, which is stimulated by the CCK₂ receptor, pepsinogen secretion is stimulated by the CCK₁ receptor." (p. 873)
- > "This activation occurs by spontaneous cleavage of a small N-terminal peptide fragment (the activation peptide), but only at a pH that is <5.0 … extremely rapid at a pH that is <3.0." (p. 873)
- > "pH values >3.5 reversibly inactivate pepsin, and pH values >7.2 irreversibly inactivate the enzyme." (p. 873)

### Figures

#### Figure 42-12 — Activation of pepsinogens to pepsins *(listed)*
At pH 5→3 pepsinogen spontaneously sheds its N-terminal activation peptide to form pepsin; activation is far faster at pH < 3 and is **autocatalytic** (new pepsin activates more pepsinogen). Pepsins themselves are active only at pH < 3.5.

## Section 4 — Protection of the gastric surface epithelium and duodenal neutralization (pp. 874–877)

### Subsection headings
- **(intro) The gastric diffusion barrier** (p. 874)
- **Vagal stimulation and irritation stimulate gastric mucous cells to secrete mucins** (pp. 874–875)
- **Gastric surface cells secrete HCO₃, stimulated by acetylcholine, acids, and prostaglandins** (p. 875)
- **Mucus protects the gastric surface epithelium by trapping an HCO₃-rich fluid near the apical border of these cells** (pp. 875–876)
- **Acid entry into the duodenum induces S cells to release secretin, triggering the pancreas and duodenum to secrete HCO₃** (pp. 876–877)

### Core claims
- The epithelium must hold a **>10⁶-fold H⁺ gradient** (lumen pH 1 vs. cell pH 7.2) and a plasma-to-lumen **Na⁺ ratio up to ~30**. The **gastric diffusion barrier** has three components:
  1. **Apical-membrane + tight-junction impermeability to acid** in the gland.
  2. A **mucous gel layer** (50–200 µm thick) over the surface cells.
  3. An **HCO₃-rich microclimate** beneath the gel keeping local pH ~7 against a bulk luminal pH of 1–3.
- **Mucins** (MUC1, MUC5, MUC6) are high-MW glycoproteins, disulfide-linked oligomers; sulfated polysaccharide chains make the gel viscous and protective. Three sources: **surface mucous cells, mucous neck cells, glandular mucous cells**. Glandular mucus is **neutral**; surface/pit mucus is **neutral + acidic** glycoprotein. Mucus must be **continuously secreted** because acid precipitates and sloughs it. Stimuli: **vagal ACh (↑Ca²⁺)** and physical/chemical irritation; **cAMP is *not* a messenger for mucus** (unlike acid/pepsinogen).
- **Surface-cell HCO₃ secretion** (Fig. 42-13A): basolateral **electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransporter (NBC)** imports HCO₃⁻; apical **Cl-HCO₃ exchanger SLC26A9** exports it into the mucous layer. Stimulated by **vagal ACh (↑Ca²⁺)**, **luminal acid** (via neural reflexes + local **PGE₂**), and prostaglandins.
- **Viscous fingering** resolves the paradox of how strongly acidic gland output reaches the lumen without neutralizing the surface microclimate: pressurized acid **tunnels up as a "finger"** through the gel without spreading laterally.
- The mucus/HCO₃ layer also blocks **pepsin** (diffusion barrier + alkaline inactivation > pH 3.5/7.2), preventing autodigestion.
- **Duodenal neutralization**: duodenal pH below threshold **~4.5** → **S cells release secretin** → **pancreatic fluid + HCO₃** secretion (neutralizes the acid load); plus **duodenal HCO₃ secretion** (proximal duodenum, prostaglandin-dependent, via **Cl-HCO₃ exchange + CFTR**, mechanism like the pancreatic duct, Fig. 43-6). **Duodenal-ulcer patients** tend to have **↑acid + ↓duodenal HCO₃** → greater low-pH exposure.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The gastric epithelium must maintain an H⁺ concentration gradient of more than a million-fold." (p. 874)
- > "the diffusion barrier is both physiological and anatomical." (p. 874)
- > "This mucous gel layer provides protection against injury from noxious luminal substances, including acid, pepsins, bile acids, and ethanol." (p. 874)
- > "Unlike for acid and pepsinogen secretion, cAMP does not appear to be a second messenger for mucus secretion." (p. 875)
- > "the stream of acid apparently does not spread laterally, but rather rises to the surface as a 'finger'" (p. 876) — viscous fingering.
- > "Prostaglandins play a central role in maintaining mucosal integrity." (Box 42-2, p. 876)

### Figures

#### Figure 42-13 — Diffusion barrier in the surface of the gastric mucosa *(viewed)*
**A (NORMAL SURFACE EPITHELIUM)** — surface cell with a **mucous gel layer** above it. A **pH profile** runs from luminal **pH ~1–3 ([H⁺] ≈ 20 mM)** at the top to **~7.4** at the cell surface. Within the gel a **neutralization zone**: secreted **HCO₃⁻** (apical **SLC26A9** exchanger; basolateral **NBC** uptake) titrates H⁺ that diffuses in ($H^+ + HCO_3^- \to H_2CO_3 \to CO_2 + H_2O$), keeping the juxtamucosal **[H⁺] ≈ 0.0001 mM**. The alkaline layer also inactivates penetrating pepsin.
**B (DAMAGED MUCOSAL BARRIER)** — when H⁺ back-diffuses into the epithelium it damages **mast cells**, which release **histamine** and other mediators → inflammatory response. Mild insult → ↑blood flow supports mucus/HCO₃ repair; severe insult → ↓blood flow (ischemia) and cell injury.

## Section 5 — Filling and emptying of the stomach (pp. 877–878)

### Subsection headings
- **Gastric motor activity plays a role in filling, churning, and emptying** (pp. 876–877)
- **Filling of the stomach is facilitated by both receptive relaxation and gastric accommodation** (p. 877)
- **The stomach churns its contents until the particles are small enough to be gradually emptied into the duodenum** (pp. 877–878)

### Core claims
- **Three motor functions**: reservoir/storage (proximal stomach relaxes), churning/mixing (digestion), and controlled emptying of **chyme** (pyloric antrum + pylorus + proximal duodenum act as a unit).
- **Receptive relaxation** — even a dry swallow relaxes the LES and proximal stomach via a **vagovagal reflex** (efferent limb neither cholinergic nor adrenergic); volume rises without a pressure rise. **Gastric accommodation** — active fundic dilation in response to filling itself; ENS is the primary regulator, vagus modulatory. **Vagotomy** abolishes much accommodation (pressure rises faster).
- **Liquids vs. solids**: water/isotonic saline empty fastest; **acidic and caloric fluids slower; fat slowest**. **Solids must be triturated to < 2 mm** before emptying; achieved by **propulsion → grinding → retropulsion** cycles driven by the **gastric pacemaker** on the greater curvature. Particles > 2 mm are cleared later by **MMCs** during the interdigestive period.
- **Fasting vs. fed patterns**: fasting = **migrating myoelectric complex (MMC)**, terminated by eating → **fed pattern**.
- **Feedback on emptying** (almost always *delaying*): duodenal chemo-/mechanoreceptors sense **low pH, high calories, lipid, tryptophan, osmolarity** → neural + hormonal signals (**vagus, secretin, CCK, GIP**) → fundic relaxation, ↓antral motility, phasic pyloric contractions, altered intestinal motility.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Relaxation in the fundus is primarily regulated by a vagovagal reflex and has been called receptive relaxation." (p. 877)
- > "This phenomenon is the result of active dilation of the fundus, called gastric accommodation." (p. 877)
- > "Particles >2 mm do not leave the stomach during the immediate postprandial digestive period." (p. 877)
- > "most gastric contents are returned to the body of the stomach for pulverization and shearing of solid particles, a process known as retropulsion." (p. 878)

## Glossary

- **Oxyntic (fundic) gland mucosa** — corpus/fundus glands with parietal, chief, mucous neck, ECL cells; secretes acid, intrinsic factor, pepsinogen.
- **Pyloric (antral) gland mucosa** — no parietal cells; G cells (gastrin) + D cells (somatostatin).
- **Parietal (oxyntic) cell** — secretes HCl + intrinsic factor; tubulovesicle→canaliculus recruitment.
- **Chief (peptic) cell** — secretes pepsinogens via compound exocytosis.
- **ECL cell** — histamine source; central cAMP amplifier of acid.
- **G cell / D cell** — gastrin / somatostatin endocrine cells.
- **H,K-ATPase (gastric proton pump)** — P-type α/β ATPase; 2 H⁺ out / 2 K⁺ in per ATP; PPI target.
- **Alkaline tide** — basolateral HCO₃⁻ efflux (AE2/SLC4A2) into blood during acid secretion.
- **Apical K⁺ recycling (KvLQT1) and Cl⁻ conductance (CFTR)** — supply the pump and net HCl.
- **Three secretagogues** — ACh (M₃/Gq), gastrin (CCK₂/Gq), histamine (H₂/Gs→cAMP).
- **Somatostatin (SST, Gi)** — central brake; direct + paracrine inhibition.
- **CCK₁ vs. CCK₂** — pepsinogen (CCK₁) vs. acid (CCK₂) receptor split.
- **Cephalic / gastric / intestinal phases** — ~30% / ~50–60% / ~5–10% of meal acid.
- **Enterogastrone** — duodenal inhibitory hormones (CCK, secretin, GIP, VIP, neurotensin, PYY).
- **PGE₂ / EP₃ (parietal) and EP₂ (chief)** — prostaglandin mucosal protection + acid inhibition.
- **Gastric diffusion barrier** — apical impermeability + mucous gel + HCO₃ microclimate.
- **Mucins MUC1/MUC5/MUC6** — gel-forming glycoproteins.
- **Viscous fingering** — acid streams up through the gel without neutralizing the microclimate.
- **Intrinsic factor** — parietal-cell glycoprotein essential for ileal vitamin B₁₂ (cobalamin) absorption.
- **Pepsinogen → pepsin** — pH-dependent autocatalytic activation; pepsin active pH 1.8–3.5.
- **Receptive relaxation / gastric accommodation** — vagovagal + ENS storage reflexes.
- **Trituration; propulsion/grinding/retropulsion; MMC** — solid-emptying machinery.
- **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma)** — autonomous gastrin hypersecretion.
- **Helicobacter pylori** — gram-negative antral bacillus; cause of most peptic ulcers.
