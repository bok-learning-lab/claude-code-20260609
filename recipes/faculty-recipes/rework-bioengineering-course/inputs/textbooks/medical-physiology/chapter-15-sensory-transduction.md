## Section 1 — Sensory transduction: general principles (pp. 353–354)

### Subsection headings
- **Sensory receptors convert environmental energy into neural signals** (p. 353)
- **Sensory transduction uses adaptations of common molecular signaling mechanisms** (pp. 353–354)
- **Sensory transduction requires detection and amplification, usually followed by a local receptor potential** (p. 354)

### Core claims
- **Transduction** = conversion of environmental energy (photons, mechanical force, dissolved chemicals, temperature) into a graded electrical change in a sensory cell — the **receptor potential** — that then regulates either the gating of voltage-gated channels in the same cell, the firing of a primary sensory axon, or the Ca²⁺-dependent release of a transmitter onto an afferent.
- **Univariance** (Johannes Müller, early 19th century): the firing of a sensory afferent does not "know" what stimulated it; modality specificity is set by the receptor and the central projection target, not by the spike. Banging the eye produces phosphenes; cortical seizures in olfactory areas produce phantom smells.
- **Two broad transduction families** map onto signaling already used elsewhere in cells:
  - **GPCR-mediated** — vision (rhodopsin / cone opsins → transducin), olfaction (ORs → Gₒₗf), taste sweet/umami/bitter (T1R/T2R → gustducin → PLC). Use cyclic nucleotides (cGMP, cAMP) and IP₃/DAG as second messengers; CNG, TRPM5, TRPC channels as effectors.
  - **Channel-mediated** — mechanotransduction in hair cells, mechanoreceptive nerve endings (Piezo2, TRPC, TRPA1, TRPV4); thermosensation (TRPV1/2/3/4, TRPM8, TRPA1); some taste modalities (ENaC for Na⁺; TRPP3/PKD1L3 for H⁺); ASICs.
- **Amplification is mandatory** for low-input modalities (single photons, single odorant molecules) and is achieved by enzymatic cascades — one photoactivated rhodopsin → ~700 transducin → PDE → ~1400 cGMP hydrolyzed → ~230 CNG channels closed (Fig. 15-12 vicinity).
- **Receptor potential** is **graded and electrotonic**, not all-or-none. Most retinal neurons (except ganglion cells) and most taste/olfactory transducers communicate via graded potentials over short distances; action potentials are reserved for axonal output (ganglion cells, olfactory neurons, primary sensory afferents).

### Psychophysics (compact reference)

Two classical scaling laws relate stimulus intensity $I$ to perceived intensity $\psi$:

- **Weber–Fechner law** (logarithmic; valid for vision near threshold, audition in dB SPL):

  $$\psi = k \, \log\!\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)$$

- **Stevens' power law** (more generally accurate across modalities; exponent $n$ varies by modality — vision ~0.33, audition ~0.6, electric shock ~3.5):

  $$\psi = k \, I^n$$

The Weber fraction $\Delta I / I = \text{const.}$ is the just-noticeable-difference law from which Fechner derived the logarithmic form by integration.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Sensory transduction... uses energy from the environment to trigger electrochemical signals that can be transmitted to the brain." (p. 353)
- > "The sensory receptor and its subsequent neural circuits do not know what stimulated them—they give the same type of response regardless. Specificity for each modality is ensured by the structure and position of the sensory receptor." (p. 353)
- > "Some modalities (vision, olfaction, some types of taste, and other chemoreception) begin with integral membrane proteins that belong to the superfamily of G protein-coupled receptors." (p. 353)
- > "Other sensory systems (mechanoreceptors, including the hair cells of audition and the vestibular organs, as well as some taste cells) use modified membrane ion channels in the primary transduction process." (p. 353)
- > "The receptor potential is not an action potential but a graded electrotonic event that can either modulate the activity of other channels... or trigger action potentials in a different portion of the same cell." (p. 354)

## Section 2 — Chemoreception: taste and smell (pp. 354–359)

### Subsection headings
- **Chemoreceptors are ubiquitous, diverse, and evolutionarily ancient** (p. 354)
- **Taste receptors are modified epithelial cells, whereas olfactory receptors are neurons** (pp. 354–356)
- **Complex flavors are derived from a few basic types of taste receptors, with contributions from sensory receptors of smell, temperature, texture, and pain** (p. 356)
- **Taste transduction involves many types of molecular signaling systems** (pp. 356–358)
- **Olfactory transduction involves specific receptors, G protein-coupled signaling, and a cyclic nucleotide–gated ion channel** (pp. 358–359)

### Core claims

#### Anatomy
- **Taste**: receptors clustered in **taste buds** (50–150 cells each) inside **papillae** on the dorsal tongue — fungiform, foliate, circumvallate, and filiform shapes. Taste receptor cells are **modified epithelia** (not neurons) that synapse onto axons of cranial nerves VII, IX, X. Cells live ~2 weeks and turn over continuously; if the sensory nerve is cut, taste buds degenerate. Humans have ~2,000–5,000 taste buds.
- **Olfaction**: receptor cells **are** neurons — bipolar primary afferents with cilia extending into the nasal mucus and axons projecting through the cribriform plate into the **olfactory bulb** (CN I). Olfactory epithelium ≈ 10 cm² in humans (170+ cm² in some dogs). Olfactory receptor neurons are among the few mammalian neurons that **regularly regenerate** throughout life (4–8 week cycle).

#### Five primary taste qualities and transduction (Fig. 15-3)

| Quality | Receptor / channel | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Salty** (low [NaCl]) | **ENaC** (epithelial Na⁺ channel; amiloride-sensitive); plus an amiloride-insensitive cation channel | Na⁺ flows down its gradient through constitutively open channels → depolarization (graded). Transduction "depolarizes by increasing the Na⁺ gradient at a fixed Na⁺ permeability" (p. 357) — opposite of the AP, where depolarization comes from increased permeability at a fixed gradient. |
| **Sour** (H⁺) | **TRPP3/PKD1L3** nonselective cation channel complex; HCN, **ASIC** also implicated | Protons gate or block cation channels → depolarization. Carbonation (high CO₂) → CA IV → H⁺ → activation of sour cells (acetazolamide blunts the "fizz" perception). |
| **Sweet** | **T1R2 + T1R3** GPCR heterodimer → gustducin (Gα family) → **PLC** → **IP₃** → ER Ca²⁺ release → **TRPM5** opens → depolarization → ATP release via nonvesicular gap-junction-like channels onto gustatory axon | Broadly tuned; sweet cells do not co-express bitter/umami receptors |
| **Umami** | **T1R1 + T1R3** GPCR heterodimer (note shared T1R3) → same PLC/IP₃/TRPM5 cascade | L-glutamate, some L-amino acids; T1R1 knockout abolishes amino-acid taste but preserves sweet |
| **Bitter** | ~25 different **T2R** GPCRs (each bitter cell expresses most/all of them) → same PLC/IP₃/TRPM5 cascade | Coarse discrimination — we recognize "bitter" but not which bitter. Distinct cells/axons from sweet/umami despite shared signaling, hence no taste confusion. |

- **Output transmitters differ by modality**: sour and salty cells use Ca²⁺-triggered **vesicular** release of **serotonin**; sweet/bitter/umami cells use Ca²⁺-triggered **nonvesicular** release of **ATP** onto gustatory afferents.
- **Labeled-line coding**: mice in which a bitter receptor is genetically expressed in sweet cells become attracted to bitter tastants — proving that the receptor cell, not the receptor protein, encodes taste quality (p. 356).

#### Olfactory transduction cascade (Fig. 15-4)

1. Odorant binds an **olfactory receptor (OR)** protein — GPCR — in a cilium of the olfactory receptor neuron. OR family is the **largest gene family** in the mammalian genome: ~1,000 in rodents, ~350 functional in humans (Buck & Axel, 2004 Nobel).
2. OR activates **Gₒₗf** (heterotrimeric G protein, olfactory-specific Gα).
3. αₒₗf activates **adenylyl cyclase III (ACIII)** → cAMP.
4. cAMP binds and opens a **cyclic-nucleotide-gated (CNG) cation channel** (closely related to the photoreceptor cGMP-gated channel — common ancestor).
5. Na⁺, K⁺, **Ca²⁺** enter → depolarization.
6. Risen [Ca²⁺]ᵢ opens **Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channels (ANO2 / anoctamin 2)**; because olfactory neurons maintain a high [Cl⁻]ᵢ, Cl⁻ exits and **further depolarizes** the cell (unusual amplification step).
7. Receptor potential reaches threshold → AP fires in the soma → axon → olfactory bulb glomerulus.

- **One-receptor-per-neuron rule**: each olfactory neuron expresses only one of the ~1,000 OR genes; the bulb organizes ORN axons into glomeruli by receptor identity, so each glomerulus represents a single OR. CNS decodes odor by reading patterns across glomeruli.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Chemoreception is the oldest form of sensory transduction... every cell in the human body is chemosensitive." (p. 354)
- > "Olfactory receptors are neurons... Taste receptor cells are not neurons but rather modified epithelial cells that synapse onto the axons of sensory neurons." (p. 354)
- > "Salt-sensitive taste cells detect low [NaCl] using the epithelial Na⁺ channel ENaC..., which is blocked by the drug amiloride." (p. 357)
- > "These taste receptors are GPCRs, and all use the same basic second-messenger pathway." (p. 358)
- > "Opening TRPM5 depolarizes the taste cell, triggering the release of neurotransmitter onto the primary gustatory axon." (p. 358)
- > "Each receptor cell seems to express only a single gene of the 1000 different odorant receptor genes in rodents. Thus, 1000 different types of olfactory receptor cells are present, each identified by the one receptor gene that it expresses." (p. 359)
- > "The olfactory channel and the photoreceptor channel almost certainly evolved from one ancestral CNG channel, just as the olfactory receptor and photoreceptor proteins probably evolved from an ancestral receptor with seven membrane-spanning segments." (p. 359)

## Section 3 — Visual transduction (pp. 359–371)

### Subsection headings
- **The optical components of the eye collect light and focus it onto the retina** (pp. 360–363)
- **The retina is a small, displaced part of the CNS** (p. 363)
- **There are three primary types of photoreceptors: rods, cones, and intrinsically photosensitive ganglion cells** (pp. 363–365)
- **Rods and cones hyperpolarize in response to light** (pp. 365–366)
- **Rhodopsin is a G protein-coupled "receptor" for light** (pp. 367–368)
- **The eye uses a variety of mechanisms to adapt to a wide range of light levels** (pp. 368–369)
- **Color vision depends on the different spectral sensitivities of the three types of cones** (pp. 369–370)
- **The ipRGCs have unique properties and functions** (pp. 370–371)

### Core claims

#### Eye optics
- The eye is functionally a self-cleaning, auto-focusing, light-adapting camera. The optical path: tears → **cornea** → aqueous humor → **pupil/iris** → **lens** → vitreous humor → retina.
- **Refraction** is dominated by the **air–cornea interface** (Δn = 0.376; **+48.2 D**); the inner cornea contributes −5.9 D, and the **lens** adds the remaining net power up to ~60 D total in the relaxed eye.
- **Focal power** of one surface of a spherical refractor:

  $$D = \dfrac{n_2 - n_1}{r} \qquad (1\,\text{D} = 1\,\text{m}^{-1}, \quad \text{Equation 15-1})$$

- **Accommodation**: the **ciliary muscle** contracts → releases tension on the zonal fibers → lens rounds up → ↑ focal power → near vision (up to +12–14 D in children). Stiffening with age = **presbyopia** (near point → ~10 cm by 30, > arm's length by mid-40s).
- **Refractive disorders (Fig. 15-7)**: **myopia** (eye too long, distant images in front of retina, concave lens correction); **hyperopia** (too short, near images behind retina, convex correction); **astigmatism** (asymmetric corneal curvature).
- **Iris**: parasympathetic constrictor → **miosis** (Edinger-Westphal nucleus, CN III); sympathetic dilator → **mydriasis** (superior cervical ganglion). **Pupillary light reflex** is both **direct** and **consensual** — afferent: retina → pretectal nucleus → bilateral Edinger-Westphal → bilateral ciliary ganglia (Fig. 15-8). Range of pupillary modulation = ~16-fold.
- **Glaucoma**: blocked canal of Schlemm → ↑ intraocular pressure → ganglion cell axon death at optic disc.

#### Retinal organization (Fig. 15-9)

The retina is **histologically and embryologically CNS** — ~200 µm thick — and contains five neuronal classes:

| Cell | Location | Output | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rods / cones** | outer nuclear layer | graded → bipolar cells (glutamate) | hyperpolarize to light; 100×10⁶ rods + cones per eye |
| **Bipolar cells** | inner nuclear layer | graded → ganglion cells | ON vs. OFF; sign-inverting (mGluR6) for ON-bipolar |
| **Horizontal cells** | outer plexiform | lateral inhibition between photoreceptors/bipolar | wide-field |
| **Amacrine cells** | inner nuclear / inner plexiform | lateral processing between bipolars and ganglion | many subtypes (>20) |
| **Ganglion cells** | ganglion cell layer | spike output via optic nerve to LGN | only retinal cells that fire conventional APs; 1×10⁶ per eye → ~100:1 photoreceptor:ganglion convergence |

- **Inverted retina**: light passes through ganglion cells, inner layers, and only then reaches outer-segment photopigment. This counterintuitive arrangement places photoreceptors next to the pigment epithelium for waste recycling and absorption of stray photons.
- **Fovea**: 300–700 µm pit, **cone-only**, with **1:1:1** receptor:bipolar:ganglion ratio (Fig. 15-10A) for maximal acuity. Inner-retinal neurons are physically displaced laterally to clear the light path. Cone-cone center-to-center spacing is ~0.3 µm in the fovea.
- **Periphery**: ratio is much higher (many rods → one bipolar → one ganglion), enlarging receptive fields and increasing absolute sensitivity but reducing acuity (Fig. 15-10B).

#### Phototransduction cascade (Figs. 15-11, 15-12)

Vertebrate rods and cones are **hyperpolarizing receptors** — light **decreases** transmitter release.

| State | [cGMP]ᵢ | CNG channel | Outer-segment Na⁺/Ca²⁺ current | $V_m$ | Glutamate release |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dark | high | open | ~33 pA inward (≈11,000 open channels × 3 fA each) | ~ −40 mV | tonic, high |
| Light | low | closed | reduced | hyperpolarized (more negative) | reduced |

Cascade per absorbed photon:
1. **Photon → 11-cis retinal isomerizes to all-trans retinal** within ~1 ps inside the rhodopsin binding pocket.
2. **Rhodopsin → metarhodopsin II**, which activates **transducin (Gₜ)**: one R* turns over **~700 Gₜ** in ~100 ms.
3. **αₜ-GTP → cGMP phosphodiesterase (PDE)** → ~100-fold increase in cGMP hydrolysis rate; ~**1,400 cGMP molecules** hydrolyzed per absorbed photon → [cGMP]ᵢ drops ~8% locally.
4. **CNG channel closes** (steep Hill cooperativity — three cGMP bind to open). ~230 of the 11,000 dark-open channels close per photon → ~2% drop in dark current. Each channel passes only **3 fA** (one of the smallest known unitary conductances) — chosen by evolution to keep the single-channel shot noise small compared to the 33-pA dark current (signal-to-noise advantage).
5. Outer-segment current ↓ → cell hyperpolarizes from −40 mV toward $E_K$.
6. Synaptic terminal: voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels close → glutamate release drops.

Termination of the response:
- **Rhodopsin kinase** phosphorylates R*.
- **Arrestin** binds phosphorylated R* → blocks further Gₜ activation.
- αₜ has intrinsic GTPase; PDE turns off.
- **Guanylyl cyclase** resynthesizes cGMP from GTP. **Ca²⁺ negative feedback**: in light, [Ca²⁺]ᵢ falls (Na⁺/Ca²⁺-K⁺ exchanger NCKX1 / SLC24A1 keeps pumping); low Ca²⁺ **disinhibits guanylyl cyclase** and **inhibits PDE**, accelerating cGMP recovery.
- Bleached all-trans retinal recycles via the pigment epithelium back to 11-cis retinal.

Amplification gain per photon: ~10⁶ Na⁺ ions are prevented from entering the outer segment.

#### Adaptation

Operating range of the human eye spans **~10¹⁰** in luminance.

- **Pupillary aperture**: ~16-fold (small).
- **Receptor-mediated adaptation** (the dominant range):
  - **Rapid (~10 min) cone adaptation** — Ca²⁺-dependent feedback on rhodopsin, guanylyl cyclase, and the CNG channel itself.
  - **Slow (~30 min) rod adaptation** — rhodopsin regeneration. A fully dark-adapted retina (Fig. 15-13) has a **15,000-fold lower threshold** than the light-adapted retina; this is "two retinas in one" — cone retina for high light, rod retina for dim.
- **Hecht et al. (1942)**: only **5–7 photons**, each on a different rod, are enough to evoke a sensation of light in humans — rods operate near the physical floor.

#### Color vision

- **Trichromacy** (Young–Helmholtz, 1801; confirmed by microspectrophotometry in 1964). Three cone opsins with absorption peaks:

| Cone | Peak (nm) | Spectral region | Historical name |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | 420 | violet | "blue" |
| **M** | 530 | yellow-green | "green" |
| **L** | 560 | yellow-red | "red" |
| (rod rhodopsin) | 500 | blue-green | scotopic peak |

- **Single cones do not encode wavelength** — by univariance, a cone gives the same response to one wavelength near peak and a brighter wavelength far from peak. Color requires comparison **across cone types**.
- **Scotopic luminosity peak ≈ 500 nm** (rhodopsin); **photopic peak ≈ 560 nm** (L cone dominates because S sensitivity is only ~⅒ of L/M). The **Purkinje shift** is the perceptual blue-shift of luminosity as you dark-adapt.
- **Box 15-1 — Inherited color-vision defects**: X-linked **red-green color blindness** affects ~8% of white males and ~1% of white females. L and M opsin genes sit adjacent on the X chromosome and undergo homologous recombination — producing **dichromacy** (one pigment missing) or **anomalous trichromacy** (shifted spectrum from L–M hybrid pigments). True **monochromacy** (e.g., S-cone monochromacy with L and M both missing) is rare (<0.001%).

#### ipRGCs and melanopsin

- A small subset (~1%) of retinal ganglion cells expresses **melanopsin** (peak 475 nm) and is **intrinsically photosensitive (ipRGC)**.
- ipRGCs **depolarize** to light (opposite of rods/cones) via a G-protein pathway resembling invertebrate phototransduction: melanopsin → Gq family → PLC → IP₃/DAG → TRP channels open → depolarization. They generate action potentials.
- High threshold, **slow** (seconds), **sustained** response. Outputs:
  - **Pretectal nucleus** → Edinger-Westphal → **pupillary light reflex**.
  - **Suprachiasmatic nucleus** → **circadian entrainment**.
  - **Pineal melatonin** suppression by light.
  - Sleep-wake regulation.

## Section 4 — Vestibular and auditory transduction: hair cells (pp. 371–383)

### Subsection headings
- **Bending the stereovilli of hair cells along one axis causes cation channels to open or to close** (pp. 372–374)
- **The otolithic organs (saccule and utricle) detect the orientation and linear acceleration of the head** (pp. 374–375)
- **The semicircular canals detect the angular acceleration of the head** (pp. 375–376)
- **The outer and middle ears collect and condition air pressure waves for transduction within the inner ear** (pp. 376–377)
- **The cochlea is a spiral of three parallel, fluid-filled tubes** (pp. 377–378)
- **Inner hair cells transduce sound, whereas the active movements of outer hair cells amplify the signal** (pp. 378–380)
- **The frequency sensitivity of auditory hair cells depends on their position along the basilar membrane of the cochlea** (pp. 380–383)

### Core claims

#### The hair cell

- **Hair cells** are mechanoreceptive epithelial cells with an apical **hair bundle** of 50–150 actin-filled **stereovilli** (often called stereocilia) arranged in a graded staircase, plus (in vestibular cells, immature auditory cells) one true **kinocilium** at the tall end. Stereovilli are 0.2–0.8 µm wide × 4–10 µm tall.
- **Tip links**: very fine extracellular filaments between adjacent stereovilli, formed from two cadherins — **cadherin 23** (upper end) and **protocadherin 15** (lower end). Brief Ca²⁺-free exposure destroys tip links and abolishes transduction.
- **MET (mechanoelectrical transduction) channels**: nonselective cation channels (unitary conductance 150–300 pS) located near the **tips of the stereovilli**, **probably at the lower end of each tip link**. Each stereovillus has at most ~2 MET channels.
- **Two ionic compartments**:

| Compartment | Cation composition | Voltage |
|---|---|---|
| **Perilymph** (bathes hair-cell basolateral surface) | low K⁺, high Na⁺ — CSF-like | 0 mV |
| **Endolymph** (bathes stereovilli at apex) | **high K⁺ (~150 mM), low Na⁺** — cytoplasm-like | 0 mV (vestibular); **+80 mV (cochlear endocochlear potential)** |
| Hair cell basolateral resting $V_m$ | — | −40 mV |

  → Apical driving force for K⁺ entry through MET channels: ~40 mV (vestibular) or ~120 mV (cochlear) — the **endocochlear potential** of +80 mV is the single largest extracellular voltage in the body.

- **Stria vascularis** (Fig. 15-21) sets the endocochlear potential. Two-layered epithelium: **marginal cells** facing endolymph + **basal cells** separating the intrastrial fluid from the spiral-ligament interstitium. Fibrocytes scavenge K⁺ from perilymph; intermediate cells' **KCNJ10** K⁺ channel pumps K⁺ across the basal membrane down a steep K⁺ gradient (very high [K⁺]ᵢ + very low intrastrial [K⁺]) — this generates the +80 mV; **KCNQ1** in marginal cells then deposits K⁺ into the endolymph. Mutations in KCNQ1/KCNE1 → Jervell-Lange-Nielsen syndrome (deafness + long QT).

#### Mechanotransduction kinetics (Fig. 15-16)

- **Latency** < 40 µs — too fast for any second messenger; direct mechanical gating by tension on the tip link.
- **Threshold**: ~0.5 nm of bundle deflection (about the diameter of a large atom).
- **Saturation**: ~150 nm (the diameter of one stereovillus, or ~1° of angular deflection).
- **Directionality**: bending **toward the kinocilium / tallest stereovillus** = positive direction = depolarization; bending **away** = hyperpolarization. Off-axis (90°) bending is <10% as effective.
- **Resting tone**: at rest the MET channels have a small but nonzero open probability — a "leak" K⁺ current that lets the cell respond bidirectionally to small positive and negative deflections.

#### Vestibular system (Figs. 15-17B, 15-18)

- **Membranous labyrinth** contains 5 vestibular sensory organs on each side: 2 **otolithic organs (saccule, utricle)** + 3 **semicircular canals (anterior, posterior, lateral)**.
- **Saccule** (sagittal macula) and **utricle** (horizontal macula, when head tilted ~25° down) sense **gravity + linear acceleration**. Stereovilli are embedded in a gelatinous **otolithic membrane** studded with calcium-carbonate crystals (**otoconia / otoliths**, 1–5 µm). The otolithic membrane has greater inertia than endolymph, so head tilt/linear acceleration → relative shear → bundle deflection.
- **Reversal line**: kinocilia point **away** from the line in the saccule, **toward** the line in the utricle (Fig. 15-18D). This polarity tile gives the macula full directional coverage: any tilt depolarizes some cells and hyperpolarizes others. Push-pull is doubled by paired left/right organs.
- **Semicircular canals**: 3 nearly orthogonal canals encode **angular acceleration** about three head axes. Each canal terminates in an **ampulla** containing a sensory **crista ampullaris** whose hair cells project bundles into the gelatinous **cupula** that spans the canal lumen. **Cupula density = endolymph density**, so it does NOT respond to linear acceleration. With sudden head rotation, endolymph inertia bends the cupula (like wind on a sail) → bundle deflection → depolarization or hyperpolarization depending on direction.
- **Vestibular nerve afferents**: Scarpa's ganglion → ipsilateral vestibular nucleus in the brainstem.
- **Vestibulospinal** (postural) and **vestibulo-ocular** (image stabilization) reflexes; vestibular dysfunction → vertigo.

#### Outer + middle ear (Figs. 15-17A, 15-19)

- **Outer ear**: pinna + tragus + external auditory canal → emphasize and localize sound (vertical-plane localization). Auditory canal ~2.5 cm.
- **Middle ear**: air-filled chamber between tympanic membrane and oval window; connected to nasopharynx via **eustachian tube** (pressure equalization; route for otitis media).
- **Impedance matching**: sound traveling from air directly into cochlear perilymph would lose >97% by reflection due to acoustic impedance mismatch. The middle ear matches impedances by two mechanisms:
  1. **Area ratio** — tympanic membrane area / oval-window area ≈ 20.
  2. **Lever action** of malleus + incus → mechanical advantage on the stapes.
- **Acoustic reflex**: tensor tympani (CN V) + stapedius (CN VII) muscles dampen the ossicular chain at loud sounds and during self-vocalization.

#### Cochlea (Figs. 15-20, 15-22, 15-23)

- ~35 mm long, coiled 2.5 turns; ~1 × 10⁶ moving parts → most complex mechanical apparatus in the body.
- **Three parallel fluid-filled compartments** in each cross-section:

| Compartment | Fluid | Top boundary | Bottom boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scala vestibuli** | perilymph (CSF-like) | bone | **Reissner's membrane** |
| **Scala media** | endolymph (+80 mV) | Reissner's membrane | **basilar membrane** (carrying organ of Corti) |
| **Scala tympani** | perilymph | basilar membrane | bone |

- Scala vestibuli (oval window) and scala tympani (round window) communicate at the cochlear apex through the **helicotrema**.
- **Organ of Corti** (on the basilar membrane): four rows of hair cells — **1 row of ~3,500 inner hair cells (IHCs)** and **3 rows totaling ~16,000 outer hair cells (OHCs)** — supported by Deiters' cells and pillar cells. Inner hair cell stereovilli are free in endolymph; **outer hair cell stereovilli are embedded in the tectorial membrane** (hinged along one edge).

#### Sound transduction sequence (steps 1–13, p. 379)

When the stapes moves outward (sound rarefaction):

1. Oval window pulls outward → scala-vestibuli pressure drops.
2. Scala vestibuli pressure falls below scala tympani.
3. Basilar membrane **bows upward**.
4. Upward bowing shears the OHC stereovilli against the tectorial membrane.
5. OHC hair bundles tilt **toward** the tallest stereovillus.
6. MET channels open in OHCs → K⁺ entry → depolarization.
7. **Depolarization activates prestin** (named for the musical "presto") — a member of the SLC26 anion-transporter family that functions in OHCs as a **voltage-driven motor protein**. OHCs **shorten** when depolarized, **lengthen** when hyperpolarized, with up to ~5% length change, beginning within ~100 µs. Independent of ATP, Ca²⁺, microtubules, actin. **Electromotility** — "electrical-to-mechanical transduction."
8. OHC shortening amplifies the upward basilar-membrane motion locally → **cochlear amplifier**. Prestin knockout → deafness.
9. Endolymph displaced under the tectorial membrane.
10. The flow bends the **free-floating IHC stereovilli** toward the tall side.
11. IHC MET channels open → depolarization.
12. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels at the IHC basolateral pole open.
13. Glutamate release at IHC ribbon synapses → APs in spiral-ganglion afferents → cochlear nucleus.

- **IHCs do all the actual sound transduction for the brain** — 95% of the 30,000 sensory spiral-ganglion afferents (type I) contact IHCs.
- **OHCs are mostly motor cells** — the cochlear amplifier — innervated by only ~5% of afferents (type II) but receive heavy **medial olivocochlear efferent** input from the brainstem. ACh release onto OHCs activates α9α10 nicotinic receptors → Ca²⁺ entry → Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels → hyperpolarization → suppression of electromotility. This descending control gates auditory gain.

#### Place coding and tonotopy (Figs. 15-24, 15-25)

- The basilar membrane tapers in stiffness (and oppositely in width) along the cochlea:

| End | Width | Stiffness | Preferred frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** (near stapes) | narrow | ~100× stiffer | **high frequencies** (kHz–20 kHz) |
| **Apex** (helicotrema) | wide | floppy | **low frequencies** (~20 Hz) |

- A pure tone sets up a traveling wave whose envelope peaks at a specific basal-to-apical position — von Békésy's discovery (1961 Nobel).
- **Tonotopy** = the orderly map of sound frequency along the basilar membrane and onto the spiral ganglion, cochlear nucleus, and beyond. **Place coding** is the dominant frequency-encoding mechanism above ~5 kHz; below it, phase-locked spike timing in auditory afferents ("temporal/volley coding") also contributes.
- Sharp tuning of living cochleae (orange curve, Fig. 15-25) requires **active amplification by OHCs** — 20-fold above threshold, 40-fold above the passive von Békésy curve, falling off >100-fold within ~0.5 mm of the peak.

#### Otoacoustic emissions

- The cochlear amplifier runs **backwards** as well: cochlear vibrations generated by OHC electromotility leak back through the ossicular chain and produce **otoacoustic emissions** detectable by a microphone in the auditory canal. Used clinically as a non-invasive screen for OHC function (neonatal hearing screens).

#### Sound metrics

- **Decibel SPL** (Eq. 15-2):

  $$\text{dB SPL} = 20 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P}{P_{\text{ref}}}\right), \qquad P_{\text{ref}} = 20\,\mu\text{Pa}$$

  +6 dB = doubling of pressure; +20 dB = 10× pressure.

- Human hearing range: **20 Hz – 20 kHz** in the young; high-frequency loss with age (presbycusis). Threshold at 2 kHz ≈ 20 µPa (the reference). Pain threshold ≈ 130 dB SPL.

#### Box 15-2 — Cochlear implants

Sensorineural deafness from hair-cell loss can be partially restored by implanting an 8–22 electrode array in the basal end of the cochlea that drives the auditory nerve directly. The implant exploits **tonotopy** — basal electrodes evoke high-pitch percepts, apical evoke low. External components: microphone + speech processor + transcutaneous RF transmitter. Internal: receiver/decoder + electrode array. Outcomes depend strongly on age at implant; prelingually deaf children > prelingually deaf adults.

## Section 5 — Somatic sensory receptors, proprioception, and pain (pp. 383–389)

### Subsection headings
- **A variety of sensory endings in the skin transduce mechanical, thermal, and chemical stimuli** (p. 383)
- **Mechanoreceptors in the skin provide sensitivity to specific stimuli such as vibration and steady pressure** (pp. 383–386)
- **Separate thermoreceptors detect warmth and cold** (pp. 386–387)
- **Nociceptors are specialized sensory endings that transduce painful stimuli** (pp. 387–388)
- **Muscle spindles sense changes in the length of skeletal muscle fibers, whereas Golgi tendon organs gauge the muscle's force** (pp. 388–389)

### Core claims

#### Cutaneous mechanoreceptors

Glabrous (hairless: palms, fingertips, soles) and hairy skin both contain several specialized mechanoreceptive endings:

| Receptor | Location | Adaptation | Preferred stimulus | Receptive field | Channel/molecule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pacinian (Pacini) corpuscle** | subcutaneous (both skin types) | **rapidly adapting** | high-frequency vibration (200–300 Hz) | very large (Fig. 15-27A) | Piezo2 + capsule filters out steady pressure |
| **Meissner's corpuscle** | dermal papillae of glabrous skin | rapidly adapting | low-frequency "flutter" (~30–50 Hz), light touch | very small (Fig. 15-27B) | Piezo2 |
| **Merkel's disk** | dermal-epidermal junction of glabrous skin | **slowly adapting** | steady pressure, edges, fine spatial form | small | Piezo2 in Merkel cells |
| **Ruffini's corpuscle** | subcutaneous (both skin types) | slowly adapting | skin stretch | medium | unclear |
| **Krause's end bulb** | dry skin / mucous membrane borders (lips, genitalia) | rapidly adapting (?) | — | small | unclear |
| **Hair-follicle endings** | around hair follicles | rapidly or slowly adapting subtypes | hair displacement | depends on follicle | TRPC, Piezo2 |

- **The capsule shapes the response**: Loewenstein dissected the Pacinian capsule away and showed that the bare axon becomes slowly adapting + low-frequency sensitive. The capsule's viscous-elastic onion layers absorb sustained pressure but transmit transients — a mechanical band-pass filter.
- **Spatial discrimination** (Fig. 15-27C): two-point discrimination threshold depends on **receptor density** and **receptive-field size**. Fingertips ≈ 2 mm; lips < 5 mm; back ≈ 40 mm. Cortical magnification (S1 somatotopy) compounds the difference.
- **Channels of cutaneous mechanoreception**: **Piezo2** is the dominant rapidly-adapting mechanotransduction channel in mouse touch receptors (knockdown → light-touch deficits). TRPV4, TRPC3/6, TRPA1, two-pore K⁺ (KCNK), ENaC/ASIC family members also contribute in subsets. Mechanosensitivity often requires tethering to cytoskeletal (actin) or ECM elements; some channels (TRP, KCNK) respond directly to bilayer tension.

#### Thermoreceptors

- Spatially **separable** warmth and cold spots on the skin map (~1 mm dots) — separate submodalities.
- **Free nerve endings** of small-diameter axons:
  - **Warmth fibers**: unmyelinated C fibers; firing increases above ~30 °C, peaks at 44–46 °C, drops sharply above (pain mechanism takes over).
  - **Cold fibers**: small myelinated Aδ fibers; firing increases as skin cools, peaks at 24–28 °C, declines below ~10 °C (cold becomes a local anesthetic). They also have a **phasic component** — transient burst at the moment of cooling regardless of starting temperature (Fig. 15-28B).
- **Molecular thermometry — the TRP-channel temperature spectrum**:

| Channel | Activation threshold | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **TRPV1** | ≥ ~43 °C | Capsaicin receptor (chili "burn"); H⁺ also gates it; sensitized by inflammatory mediators (PKC, PKA via NGF, bradykinin) → hyperalgesia |
| **TRPV2** | ≥ ~50 °C | Noxious heat |
| **TRPV3** | warm (~33–39 °C) | innocuous warmth (keratinocytes) |
| **TRPV4** | warm (~27–35 °C) | innocuous warmth, also osmotic |
| **TRPM8** | ≤ ~27 °C, peak at 8 °C | **Menthol** receptor — menthol mimics "coolness" by gating TRPM8 |
| **TRPA1** | ≤ ~17 °C (noxious cold); also mustard oil / allyl isothiocyanate, garlic | Pungent irritant + noxious cold |

> The chili-pepper "hot" sensation is literally the activation of the same channel that heat itself opens. The menthol "cool" sensation is the parallel trick on TRPM8.

#### Nociceptors

- **Free nerve endings** distributed almost everywhere except brain parenchyma (meninges are sensitive).
- **Axon classes**:
  - **Aδ fibers**: thinly myelinated, fast (5–30 m/s) → sharp, well-localized "first pain."
  - **C fibers**: unmyelinated, slow (0.5–2 m/s) → dull, burning, poorly localized "second pain."
- **Functional categories**:
  - **Mechanical nociceptors** — sharp pressure; subset express **MrgprD** (genetic ablation → mechanical analgesia, thermal intact).
  - **Thermal nociceptors** — heat via TRPV1/TRPV2; noxious cold via TRPA1, TRPM8, **Nav1.8** (a cold-resistant Na⁺ channel that keeps APs firing at low temperatures).
  - **Chemical nociceptors** — H⁺, K⁺, histamine, bradykinin, capsaicin (TRPV1), mustard oil (TRPA1).
  - **Polymodal C nociceptors** — sensitive to all three.
- **Nav1.7** — peripheral-neuron Na⁺ channel essential for pain perception. Loss-of-function → **congenital insensitivity to pain** (repeated injuries). Gain-of-function → **inherited erythromelalgia** and **paroxysmal extreme pain disorder** (intense chronic pain).

#### Hyperalgesia and inflammation (Fig. 15-29)

- Damaged tissue releases an **"inflammatory soup"**: glutamate, ATP, serotonin, adenosine; bradykinin, substance P; prostaglandins (COX-2 products), endocannabinoids; NGF and other neurotrophins; cytokines (TNF-α); K⁺, H⁺.
- These mediators sensitize TRPV1, TRPA1, and ASICs → **primary hyperalgesia** at the injury site.
- **Axon reflex**: APs in one branch of a nociceptive axon antidromically invade collaterals, releasing substance P and CGRP at neighboring skin → vasodilation, plasma extravasation, mast-cell histamine release → spreading **secondary hyperalgesia** ("flare").
- **Central sensitization**: prolonged C-fiber activity → glutamate-driven LTP-like potentiation of dorsal-horn synapses via AMPA + NMDA receptors → chronic pain states.
- **Gate-control theory** (Melzack & Wall, 1965): co-activation of large-fiber Aβ mechanoreceptors recruits spinal GABA/glycine inhibitory interneurons that close the gate on small-fiber nociceptive transmission — the basis for rubbing-the-bumped-elbow phenomenology and for TENS.
- **Endogenous opioids** (endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins) act on µ, δ, κ opioid receptors at supraspinal, spinal, and peripheral sites to dampen pain.

#### Proprioception: muscle spindle + Golgi tendon organ (Fig. 15-30)

- **Muscle spindle**:
  - **Intrafusal fibers** (modified muscle fibers) arranged **in parallel** with the force-generating **extrafusal fibers**.
  - Two intrafusal fiber types: **bag fibers** (nuclei clumped) — dynamic length response; **chain fibers** (nuclei in a row) — static length response.
  - **Primary (group Ia) afferents** wrap around bag and chain fibers — encode **rate of change in length** (velocity).
  - **Secondary (group II) afferents** mainly contact chain fibers — encode **static length** (slowly adapting).
  - **γ motor neurons** innervate intrafusal fibers and maintain spindle sensitivity during α-motor-mediated extrafusal contraction (otherwise the spindle would go slack and lose dynamic range). This is **α-γ coactivation**.
  - Mechanotransduction channels in spindle endings: ENaC, **ASIC2** implicated.
- **Golgi tendon organ (GTO)**:
  - Bare endings of **group Ib axons** investing a collagen matrix at the **muscle-tendon junction**, **in series** with extrafusal fibers.
  - Reports **muscle force** (active contraction or passive stretch).
  - Drives autogenic inhibition (Ib reflex) — partial protection against tendon rupture.
- **Joint receptors**: Ruffini-, Golgi-, and Pacini-like endings in joint capsules and ligaments; report joint angle and rate.
- Proprioceptive estimate of limb position uses **convergent inputs** from spindles + GTOs + joint receptors + cutaneous stretch receptors; patients with prosthetic joints retain joint-angle sense from the surrounding tissues.

## Glossary

- **Receptor potential** — graded, electrotonic membrane-potential change produced by transduction.
- **Univariance** — a sensory cell's response identity is fixed by the cell, not by what stimulated it.
- **Weber–Fechner / Stevens law** — psychophysical scaling laws (log / power).
- **GPCR-mediated transduction** — rhodopsin/cone opsin (visual), olfactory receptors (Gₒₗf), T1R/T2R (taste), melanopsin (ipRGC).
- **CNG channel** — cyclic-nucleotide-gated cation channel; cGMP-gated in rods/cones, cAMP-gated in olfactory neurons.
- **Transducin (Gₜ)** — visual heterotrimeric G protein.
- **PDE6** — visual rod phosphodiesterase; sildenafil cross-reactivity.
- **NCKX1 (SLC24A1)** — Na⁺/(Ca²⁺-K⁺) exchanger that extrudes Ca²⁺ from rod outer segments.
- **TRPM5** — Ca²⁺-activated nonselective cation channel of sweet/umami/bitter taste cells.
- **ENaC** — amiloride-sensitive epithelial Na⁺ channel; salt taste.
- **T1R / T2R** — sweet+umami / bitter taste receptor GPCR families.
- **T1R2+T1R3** = sweet; **T1R1+T1R3** = umami; ~25 T2Rs = bitter (each bitter cell expresses many).
- **TRPP3 / PKD1L3** — sour taste channel complex.
- **ANO2 (anoctamin 2)** — Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channel in olfactory cilia; amplifies receptor potential by Cl⁻ efflux against unusual high [Cl⁻]ᵢ.
- **Olfactory receptor genes** — ~1,000 (rodent) / ~350 (human) functional GPCRs; one per neuron rule.
- **Rod / cone / ipRGC** — three retinal photoreceptor classes.
- **Fovea** — cone-only, 1:1:1 receptor:bipolar:ganglion, foveal pit with displaced inner layers.
- **Rhodopsin / opsin** — visual GPCR + 11-cis retinal chromophore.
- **Bleaching** — separation of all-trans retinal from opsin after isomerization.
- **Dark current** — tonic Na⁺/Ca²⁺ inflow through cGMP-gated channels in the dark.
- **Cochlear amplifier** — prestin-driven OHC electromotility that sharpens basilar-membrane tuning.
- **Prestin (SLC26A5)** — voltage-driven motor protein of OHCs; absence → deafness.
- **Endocochlear potential** — +80 mV in scala media, set by stria vascularis (KCNJ10, KCNQ1).
- **Tip link** — cadherin 23 + protocadherin 15 filament that gates MET channels.
- **MET channel** — hair-cell mechanoelectrical transduction channel; nonselective cation, ~150–300 pS.
- **Otoconia / otoliths** — CaCO₃ crystals weighing down the otolithic membrane of saccule/utricle.
- **Cupula** — gelatinous mass in the semicircular canal ampulla, density matched to endolymph; sensitive to angular acceleration only.
- **Tonotopy** — frequency-to-position mapping along the basilar membrane and downstream auditory pathway.
- **Place coding vs. temporal coding** — frequency encoding by which cochlear position fires vs. by phase-locked spike timing.
- **dB SPL** — log scale of sound pressure relative to 20 µPa.
- **Pacini, Meissner, Merkel, Ruffini, Krause** — cutaneous mechanoreceptors.
- **Piezo2** — light-touch mechanotransduction channel; key for Merkel and Meissner endings.
- **TRPV1 / TRPV2 / TRPV3 / TRPV4 / TRPM8 / TRPA1** — thermo-TRP family with overlapping temperature thresholds (and chemical agonists: capsaicin, menthol, mustard oil).
- **Nav1.7 / Nav1.8** — peripheral-neuron Na⁺ channels critical for nociception; Nav1.7 LOF = congenital insensitivity to pain.
- **Aδ vs. C fibers** — fast/sharp vs. slow/burning pain.
- **Hyperalgesia (primary / secondary)** — lowered nociceptive threshold within / around injury site.
- **Axon reflex** — antidromic AP propagation into nociceptor collaterals → neurogenic inflammation.
- **Gate-control theory** — Aβ co-activation suppresses Aδ/C transmission via spinal inhibitory interneurons.
- **Endorphins / enkephalins / dynorphins** — endogenous opioid peptides.
- **Muscle spindle (Ia, II), Golgi tendon organ (Ib)** — proprioceptors.
- **α-γ coactivation** — coordinated drive that preserves spindle dynamic range during contraction.
