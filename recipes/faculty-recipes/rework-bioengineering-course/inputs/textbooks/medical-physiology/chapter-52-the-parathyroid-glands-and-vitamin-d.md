## Section 1 — Calcium and phosphate balance (pp. 1054–1056)

### Subsection headings
- **The gut, kidneys, and bone regulate calcium balance** (pp. 1054–1056)
- **The gut, kidneys, and bone also regulate phosphate balance** (p. 1056)

### Core claims
- **Three controlling hormones** — **PTH** and **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol)** primarily, plus **calcitonin** to a lesser extent — act on **three target organs (bone, kidney, GI tract)** to set plasma Ca²⁺ and phosphate. A given hormone usually moves the two ions oppositely.
- **Plasma Ca²⁺ exists in three forms** (total normal 2.2–2.6 mM ≈ 8.8–10.6 mg/dL): ~45% **free ionized Ca²⁺** (the physiologically active, PTH-regulating form), ~45% **protein-bound** (mostly albumin), ~10% **complexed** with small anions (phosphate, citrate, oxalate). Ionized Ca²⁺ is tightly held between **1.0 and 1.3 mM (4.0–5.2 mg/dL)**.
- **Calcium distribution** (Fig. 52-1): ~99% of body Ca²⁺ (~1 kg) is in **bone**; the entire ECF pool is only ~1 g (1000 mg). Dietary intake ~800–1200 mg/day (dairy main source). The gut absorbs ~500 mg/day but secretes ~325 mg/day → **net intestinal uptake ~175 mg/day**. Bone deposits ~280 mg/day and resorbs an equal amount in steady state. Kidneys filter ~10,000 mg/day and reabsorb ~99%, so **urinary Ca²⁺ ≈ 175 mg/day = net GI absorption** in balance.
- **Phosphate is NOT tightly regulated** — total plasma phosphate 0.8–1.5 mM (reported as elemental phosphorus, 2.5–4.5 mg/dL), ~50% higher in children; levels swing after meals. Of circulating inorganic phosphate, 85–90% is filterable (50% ionized, 40% complexed, only 10–15% protein-bound).
- **Phosphate distribution** (Fig. 52-2): ~0.6 kg elemental P in bone; 0.1 kg in soft tissue (organic phosphates); ~500 mg in ECF. Intake ~1400 mg/day; net intestinal absorption ~900 mg/day; bone turnover ~210 mg/day; kidneys filter ~7000 mg/day, reabsorb ~6100 mg/day, so **renal excretion ~900 mg/day = net GI absorption**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The body tightly regulates the ionized form of Ca²⁺ between 1.0 and 1.3 mM (4.0 and 5.2 mg/dL)." (p. 1054)
- > "Most total-body calcium is located within bone, ~1 kg." (p. 1054)
- > "The kidneys reabsorb ~99% of this Ca²⁺, so that the net renal excretion of Ca²⁺ is ~1% of the filtered load." (p. 1054)
- > "Unlike calcium, the plasma phosphate concentration is not strictly regulated, and its levels fluctuate throughout the day, particularly after meals." (p. 1054)
- > "Calcium and phosphate are the principal components of hydroxyapatite crystals [Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂], which by far constitute the major portion of the mineral phase of bone." (p. 1054)
- > "These hormones act on three organ systems—bone, kidneys, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract—to control the levels of calcium and phosphate in plasma." (p. 1054)

### Equations
- **Plasma Ca²⁺ partition** (healthy adults):
  $$\text{Ca}^{2+}_{\text{total}} = \text{Ca}^{2+}_{\text{free}} + \text{Ca}^{2+}_{\text{protein-bound}} + \text{Ca}^{2+}_{\text{complexed}} \approx 0.45 + 0.45 + 0.10$$
- **Calcium steady-state balance** (net GI absorption = renal excretion):
  $$\underbrace{500 - 325}_{\text{net gut}\,\approx\,175} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{Urine}}_{\approx\,175}\ \text{mg/day}$$
- **Bone Ca²⁺ in steady state**: deposition (~280 mg/day) = resorption (~280 mg/day).
- **Hydroxyapatite** mineral formula: $\text{Ca}_{10}(\text{PO}_4)_6(\text{OH})_2$.

## Section 2 — Physiology of bone (pp. 1056–1059)

### Subsection headings
- **Dense cortical bone and the more reticulated trabecular bone are the two major bone types** (pp. 1056–1057)
- **The extracellular matrix forms the nidus for the nucleation of hydroxyapatite crystals** (p. 1057)
- **Bone remodeling depends on the closely coupled activities of osteoblasts and osteoclasts** (pp. 1057–1058)

### Core claims
- **Three bone cells**: **osteoblasts** form bone (and are the principal PTH target); **osteoclasts** resorb bone (multinucleated, from monocyte/macrophage lineage; activated by RANKL); **osteocytes** are osteoblasts entombed in matrix that sense mechanical load and secrete both stimulatory (osteocalcin, Wnt ligands) and inhibitory (sclerostin, dickkopf1 — Wnt antagonists) cues. Osteocytes mediate **osteocytic osteolysis**, moving Ca²⁺ from bone interior to surface via canaliculi/gap junctions.
- **Two bone tissues** (Fig. 52-3): **cortical (compact/lamellar) bone** ~80% of mass — the dense outer cortex, fundamental unit the **osteon** (haversian canal + concentric lamellae); **trabecular (cancellous) bone** ~20% — interior spicules, prominent in vertebrae, lined by osteoblasts/osteoclasts; **most remodeling occurs on trabecular surfaces**. When resorption chronically exceeds synthesis → **osteoporosis**.
- **Osteoid** = the protein matrix (mostly **type I collagen**, ~90%; triple helix of two α1 + one α2). Collagen organization gives **tensile strength** and acts as the **nidus for hydroxyapatite nucleation**; crystal long axes align with collagen fibers. **Osteocalcin** (6 kDa, vitamin K–dependent γ-carboxyglutamate residues, induced by 1,25-(OH)₂D) and **osteonectin** (35 kDa) bind hydroxyapatite and promote nucleation.
- **Mineralization**: osteoblasts exocytose Ca²⁺ and Pᵢ from vesicles, raising local concentrations above the bulk ECF to drive crystal nucleation/growth. New formation occurs at sites of prior osteoclastic resorption — resorption and synthesis are **spatially coupled in the basic multicellular unit (BMU)**; adults have 1–2 million active BMUs.
- **RANK / RANKL / OPG axis** (Figs. 52-4, 52-5): PTH/vitamin D stimulate osteoblasts to release **M-CSF** (proliferates osteoclast precursors) and to express **RANKL** (membrane mRANKL + soluble sRANKL). RANKL binds **RANK** on osteoclast precursors → fusion into mature osteoclasts and activation. **OPG** is a soluble decoy receptor (also osteoblast-made) that **scavenges RANKL**, limiting osteoclastogenesis. Estrogen deficiency or glucocorticoid excess ↑RANKL and ↓OPG → bone loss. **Denosumab** (anti-RANKL monoclonal, FDA-approved 2010) treats postmenopausal osteoporosis.
- **Osteoclast as a one-cell epithelium** (Fig. 52-5): seals to matrix via **integrins → vitronectin**; secretes acid (V-type H⁺ pump + ClC-7 Cl⁻ channel at the ruffled border; carbonic anhydrase supplies H⁺, Cl-HCO₃ exchanger removes basolateral HCO₃⁻) and acid proteases into the **lacuna**, dissolving mineral and digesting matrix.
- **Wnt signaling** drives osteoblastogenesis and ↑OPG; its antagonists sclerostin and dickkopf1 oppose it. New osteoporosis therapies target this pathway.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Cortical (also called compact or lamellar) bone represents ~80% of the total bone mass." (p. 1056)
- > "When the rate of bone resorption exceeds that of synthesis over time, the loss of bone mineral produces the disease osteoporosis." (p. 1057)
- > "Type I collagen accounts for ~90% of the protein mass of osteoid." (p. 1057)
- > "RANK ligand (RANKL) … appears to be a major stimulator of both the differentiation of preosteoclasts to osteoclasts and the activity of mature osteoclasts." (p. 1057)
- > "By scavenging RANKL, OPG limits osteoclastogenesis, thereby protecting bone from osteoclastic activity." (p. 1058)
- > "The osteoclast—in reality a one-cell epithelium—then secretes acid and proteases across its ruffled border membrane into a confined resorption space (the lacuna)." (p. 1057)

## Section 3 — Parathyroid hormone (pp. 1059–1064)

### Subsection headings
- **Plasma Ca²⁺ regulates the synthesis and secretion of PTH** (pp. 1059–1061)
- **High plasma [Ca²⁺] inhibits the synthesis and release of PTH** (pp. 1060–1061)
- **The PTH receptor couples via G proteins to either adenylyl cyclase or phospholipase C** (p. 1061)
- **In the kidney, PTH promotes Ca²⁺ reabsorption, phosphate loss, and 1-hydroxylation of 25-hydroxyvitamin D** (pp. 1061–1063)
- **In bone, PTH can promote net resorption or net deposition** (pp. 1063–1064)

### Core claims
- **Four parathyroid glands** (combined <500 mg) on the posterior thyroid; **chief cells** synthesize/secrete PTH. The dominant secretory regulator is **ionized plasma Ca²⁺**; vitamin D also inhibits. **High phosphate stimulates PTH release.**
- **Synthesis/processing** (Fig. 52-6): **prepro-PTH (115 aa)** → cleave 25-aa signal → **pro-PTH (90 aa)** → Golgi cleavage → mature **intact PTH (84 aa)** stored in granules. The active **N-terminal 1-34/1-36 fragment** carries all biological activity; the longer **C-terminal fragment is inactive** but long-lived (70–80% of circulating PTH peptide), which is why modern **2-site ELISAs measure intact 1-84 PTH**. Intact PTH half-life ~4 min; cleared mainly by liver and kidney.
- **CaSR controls secretion** (Fig. 52-7): the **calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR)** is a GPCR on the chief-cell membrane. Ca²⁺ binding → **Gq → phospholipase C → IP₃ + DAG**; IP₃ releases internal Ca²⁺, DAG activates PKC. **Paradoxically, the rise in [Ca²⁺]ᵢ and PKC INHIBIT PTH release** (opposite of most secretory cells; cf. juxtaglomerular renin cell). Net: **high plasma Ca²⁺ → less PTH; low Ca²⁺ → more PTH.** Vitamin D (1,25-(OH)₂D)–occupied VDR/RXR binds the PTH gene 5′ response element and **lowers PTH transcription**. The gland stores only a few hours' worth of PTH, so hypocalcemia also triggers new synthesis.
- **Set-point shift** (Box 52-1, **familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia, FHH**): a mutated CaSR shifts the PTH-vs-Ca²⁺ curve rightward (Fig. 52-7B) — plasma Ca²⁺ must rise higher to suppress PTH. Patients have **elevated Ca²⁺ with normal PTH** and **low urine Ca²⁺** (mutated CaSR in TAL/DCT fails to inhibit reabsorption). Autosomal dominant; homozygous neonatal form is severe/life-threatening. **Calcimimetics** (CaSR agonists) lower PTH and treat parathyroid cancer / secondary hyperparathyroidism of renal disease.
- **PTH1R signaling**: PTH acts via **PTH1R** (a GPCR; binds 1-34, 1-84, and **PTHrP**), most abundant in **kidney (proximal + distal convoluted tubule)** and **bone (osteoblast/preosteoblast)**. PTH1R couples to **Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA** and to **Gq → PLC → IP₃/DAG → Ca²⁺**. A second receptor (PTH2R) is PTH-selective; uncertain role in Ca²⁺ regulation.
- **Renal actions** — three:
  1. **↑Ca²⁺ reabsorption** in the **TAL and DCT** (basolateral PTH receptors), greatly cutting urinary Ca²⁺ and raising plasma Ca²⁺. (Filtered Ca²⁺ is reabsorbed ~65% proximal, ~25% TAL, 5–10% distal, ~0.5% excreted.)
  2. **↓Phosphate reabsorption** in the proximal tubule — PTH **internalizes NaPi-IIa and NaPi-IIc** cotransporters → **phosphaturia**, lowering plasma phosphate. **FGF23** (from osteocytes, ↑ by high Pᵢ) likewise inhibits NaPi cotransporters.
  3. **Stimulates 1α-hydroxylase** in proximal-tubule mitochondria → converts **25-OH-D → 1,25-(OH)₂-D** (the active metabolite). FGF23 and 1,25-(OH)₂D inhibit this step (feedback).
- **PTH-induced phosphaturia protects against precipitation**: at normal levels the **Ca×PO₄ ion product** nears its solubility product; by dumping phosphate in urine, PTH keeps the product down so mobilized bone Ca²⁺ does not precipitate as calcium-phosphate salts.
- **Bone actions are biphasic**: **persistent** PTH → net **resorption** (indirect, via osteoblast RANKL/M-CSF/IL-6 → osteoclasts; also ↓collagen synthesis + ↑proteases to clear unmineralized osteoid). **Intermittent** PTH → net **synthesis** (osteocytic osteolysis → mineralization; ↓sclerostin/dickkopf1; release of matrix growth factors IGF-1/2, FGF2, TGF-β; ↑OPG). Hence **PTH 1-34 (teriparatide) given once/twice daily builds bone**, especially trabecular/axial skeleton.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The major regulator of PTH secretion is ionized plasma Ca²⁺, although vitamin D also plays a role. Both inhibit the synthesis or release of PTH. In contrast, an increase in plasma phosphorus concentration stimulates PTH release." (p. 1059)
- > "Virtually all the known biological activity of PTH resides in the N-terminal fragment." (p. 1059)
- > "The major stimulus for PTH secretion is a decline in the concentration of Ca²⁺ in the blood (hypocalcemia) and ECF." (p. 1060)
- > "Unlike most endocrine tissues … in the parathyroid the rise in [Ca²⁺]ᵢ and activation of PKC inhibit hormone secretion." (p. 1061)
- > "A key action of PTH is to promote the reabsorption of Ca²⁺ in the thick ascending limb (TAL) and distal convoluted tubule (DCT) of the kidney." (p. 1062)
- > "PTH reduces phosphate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, producing a characteristic phosphaturia and decreasing plasma phosphate levels." (p. 1062)
- > "The net effect of persistent increases of PTH on bone is to stimulate bone resorption, thus increasing plasma [Ca²⁺]." (p. 1063)
- > "Intermittent increases in plasma [PTH] have predominately bone-synthetic effects." (p. 1063)

### Equations
- **CaSR signal flow** (negative regulation of secretion):
  $$\text{Ca}^{2+}_{\text{ext}} \;\to\; \text{CaSR} \;\to\; G_q \;\to\; \text{PLC} \;\to\; \text{IP}_3 + \text{DAG} \;\to\; [\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i\!\uparrow,\ \text{PKC}\!\uparrow \;\dashv\; \text{PTH release}$$
- **PTH1R dual signaling**:
  $$\text{PTH} \to \text{PTH1R} \to \begin{cases} G_s \to \text{AC} \to \text{cAMP} \to \text{PKA} \\ G_q \to \text{PLC} \to \text{IP}_3/\text{DAG} \to [\text{Ca}^{2+}]_i\uparrow \end{cases}$$
- **Solubility constraint** (why phosphaturia matters):
  $$[\text{Ca}^{2+}] \times [\text{PO}_4] \lesssim K_{sp} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{PTH lowers } [\text{PO}_4] \text{ to keep the product subsaturated}$$

## Section 4 — Vitamin D (pp. 1064–1068)

### Subsection headings
- **The active form of vitamin D is its 1,25-dihydroxy metabolite** (pp. 1064–1066)
- **Vitamin D, by acting on the small intestine and kidney, raises plasma [Ca²⁺] and thus promotes bone mineralization** (pp. 1066–1067)
- **Calcium ingestion lowers—whereas phosphate ingestion raises—levels of both PTH and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D** (pp. 1067–1068)

### Core claims
- **Two source forms**: **vitamin D₃ (cholecalciferol)** from skin **7-dehydrocholesterol** under UV light (also from animal foods); **vitamin D₂ (ergocalciferol)** from plant diet only. They differ only in the ring-D side chain. Both are fat-soluble, carried on a **vitamin D–binding protein** (52 kDa) or in chylomicrons; stored in body fat (only 1–2% turns over daily, so deficiency takes years).
- **Two hydroxylations** (Fig. 52-9): **liver 25-hydroxylase** (cytochrome P450, largely unregulated, substrate-dependent) → **25-OH-D**; then **renal proximal-tubule 1α-hydroxylase** (mitochondrial, tightly controlled) → **1,25-(OH)₂-D (calcitriol)**, the most active metabolite. **PTH stimulates** the 1α-hydroxylation; **FGF23 and 1,25-(OH)₂D itself inhibit** it (feedback).
- **Mechanism**: 1,25-(OH)₂D enters target cells, binds the nuclear **VDR**, which heterodimerizes with **RXR** and acts as a transcription factor. VDR affinity for 1,25-(OH)₂D is ~1000× that for 25-OH-D, but because circulating 25-OH-D is ~1000× more concentrated, both probably contribute.
- **Intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption** (Fig. 52-10A): two routes — **paracellular** (throughout small intestine, passive, NOT vitamin-D-regulated) and **transcellular** (duodenum only, three steps all stimulated by 1,25-(OH)₂D): apical entry via **TRPV6 Ca²⁺ channels** → cytosolic buffering by **calbindin** (and ER/mitochondria) → basolateral extrusion by **Ca²⁺ pump (PMCA)** and **Na-Ca exchanger**. PTH's effect on gut Ca²⁺ is **entirely indirect** — it works by raising renal 1,25-(OH)₂D.
- **Intestinal phosphate absorption** (Fig. 52-10B): apical **Na/Pᵢ cotransporter (NaPi)**, the rate-limiting step, upregulated by 1,25-(OH)₂D.
- **Renal action**: vitamin D acts **synergistically with PTH** to ↑Ca²⁺ reabsorption in the DCT (via Ca²⁺-binding proteins) and promotes phosphate reabsorption — but PTH is the more potent renal regulator. 1,25-(OH)₂D **directly inhibits its own 1α-hydroxylation** (negative feedback) and **diminishes PTH gene transcription**.
- **Bone action is dual**: mostly **indirect** — by raising gut and renal Ca²⁺/phosphate, vitamin D supplies the mineral that lets unmineralized osteoid mineralize (the **antirachitic** effect). **Direct** effects act on osteoblasts and osteoclast precursors (both bear VDR): normally vitamin D ↑both differentiation (↑turnover); in **excess** it favors **resorption** (osteoblasts make matrix-degrading enzymes — alkaline phosphatase, collagenase, plasminogen activator — and favor RANKL over OPG), mobilizing bone Ca²⁺.
- **Integrated meal responses**: ingesting **Ca²⁺** → ↑plasma Ca²⁺ → ↓PTH → ↓bone resorption, ↓renal Ca²⁺ reabsorption (calciuria), and over time ↓1α-hydroxylation/↓gut absorption. Ingesting **phosphate** in excess → ↑Ca×PO₄ product → mineral deposits in bone → ↓plasma Ca²⁺ → ↑PTH → phosphaturia (lowers plasma Pᵢ) + bone mobilization. Plasma phosphate is thus regulated **indirectly through PTH (responding to Ca²⁺)** and directly by **FGF23**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The principal active form of vitamin D is not vitamin D₂ or D₃, but rather a dihydroxylated metabolite of either." (p. 1064)
- > "Vitamin D₃ can be synthesized from the 7-dehydrocholesterol that is present in the skin, provided sufficient ultraviolet light is absorbed." (p. 1064)
- > "The second hydroxylation reaction occurs in the renal proximal tubule under the tight control of PTH, vitamin D itself, and FGF23." (p. 1064)
- > "The effect of PTH to stimulate intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption is thought to be entirely indirect and mediated by PTH's action to increase the renal formation of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D." (p. 1066)
- > "First, Ca²⁺ enters the cell across the apical membrane via TRPV6 Ca²⁺ channels … the entering Ca²⁺ binds to several high-affinity binding proteins, particularly calbindin." (p. 1066)
- > "The actions of vitamin D on the small intestine, bone, and kidney serve to prevent any abnormal decline or rise in plasma [Ca²⁺]." (p. 1066)

### Equations
- **Vitamin D₃ synthesis and activation cascade**:
  $$\text{7-dehydrocholesterol} \xrightarrow{\text{skin, UV}} \text{cholecalciferol (D}_3) \xrightarrow[\text{liver}]{\text{25-hydroxylase}} \text{25-OH-D} \xrightarrow[\text{kidney PT}]{\text{1}\alpha\text{-hydroxylase}} \text{1,25-(OH)}_2\text{-D}$$
- **1α-hydroxylase regulation**:
  $$\text{1}\alpha\text{-hydroxylase}:\quad \text{PTH}\ (+),\qquad \text{FGF23}\ (-),\qquad \text{1,25-(OH)}_2\text{D}\ (-)$$
- **VDR affinity vs. abundance** (both metabolites contribute):
  $$\frac{K_{a}^{1,25}}{K_{a}^{25}} \approx 10^{3} \quad\text{but}\quad \frac{[25\text{-OH-D}]}{[1,25\text{-(OH)}_2\text{D}]} \approx 10^{3}$$

- **Panel B — Vitamin D₂.** Shows **ergocalciferol (vitamin D₂)** with its plant-sterol side chain on ring D, differing from D₃ only in that side chain.

## Section 5 — Calcitonin and other hormones (pp. 1068–1071)

### Subsection headings
- **Calcitonin inhibits osteoclasts, but its effects are transitory** (pp. 1068–1069)
- **Sex steroid hormones promote bone deposition, whereas glucocorticoids promote resorption** (pp. 1069–1070)
- **PTHrP, encoded by a gene entirely distinct from that for PTH, can cause hypercalcemia in certain malignancies** (pp. 1070–1071)

### Core claims
- **Calcitonin** is a 32-aa peptide from thyroid **C cells (parafollicular cells, neural-crest–derived)**. Its gene yields **calcitonin** in C cells and **CGRP** (a neurotransmitter) in the CNS by **alternative splicing** (Fig. 52-11). Secretion is triggered by **rising** extracellular Ca²⁺ (opposite trigger to PTH).
- **Calcitonin lowers Ca²⁺ but is minor in humans**: total thyroidectomy leaves plasma Ca²⁺ normal, and calcitonin-secreting tumors (50–100× normal) keep normal Ca²⁺. Its **calcitonin receptor** (GPCR → AC or PLC) is on the **osteoclast** (which lacks PTH receptors); calcitonin **raises osteoclast cAMP and inhibits resorption** and osteocytic osteolysis → transient hypocalcemia. Osteoclasts **desensitize within hours** ("escape"), limiting clinical use. **Salmon calcitonin** is ~10× more potent than human; used for **Paget disease** and acute hypercalcemia. Mild renal phosphaturia/natriuresis/calciuresis also occur.
- **FGF23** (osteocyte/osteoblast-derived **phosphatonin**, ↑ by high plasma phosphate) lowers plasma phosphate: ↓intestinal phosphate absorption, ↓renal phosphate reabsorption (inhibits NaPi-IIa/IIc), and inhibits 1α-hydroxylase. **Excess FGF23 → hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets**; **FGF23 deficiency → hyperphosphatemia + ectopic calcification**.
- **Sex steroids and glucocorticoids**: testosterone/estradiol **maintain bone mass**; postmenopausal **estradiol decline → osteoporosis** (loss of cortical + trabecular bone). Osteoporosis is less common in men (greater peak mass, gradual testosterone decline). **Glucocorticoid excess → osteoporosis** (↓OPG, ↑RANKL).
- **PTHrP** (PTH-related protein) is from a **distinct gene**, made in many normal and malignant tissues; its first 13 aa are homologous to PTH so it binds **PTH1R** and **mimics all PTH renal/bone actions**. Physiologic roles: endochondral bone and mammary development; secreted in milk (mobilizes maternal bone Ca²⁺). Acts mostly **paracrine/autocrine**; not a day-to-day Ca²⁺ regulator. **Tumor PTHrP secretion** (squamous lung/head/neck, renal/bladder carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, lymphoma) causes **humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Calcitonin is a 32-amino-acid peptide hormone made by the clear or C cells of the thyroid gland." (p. 1068)
- > "Within bone, the osteoclast—which lacks PTH receptors—appears to be the principal target of calcitonin." (p. 1068)
- > "Within hours of exposure to high concentrations of calcitonin, osteoclasts desensitize. This 'escape' from the hypocalcemic effect of calcitonin has limited the use of calcitonin." (p. 1069)
- > "Another regulator of plasma [phosphate] is the protein fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) … secreted by osteocytes and osteoblasts in response to high plasma [phosphate]." (p. 1067)
- > "The PTH1R receptor in kidney and bone recognizes PTHrP with an affinity similar to that for intact PTH. PTHrP mimics each of the actions of PTH on kidney and bone." (p. 1070)
- > "The decline in estradiol that occurs postmenopausally exposes women to the risk of osteoporosis." (p. 1069)

## Glossary

- **Ionized Ca²⁺** — free, physiologically active form (~45% of plasma; 1.0–1.3 mM); regulates PTH and most Ca²⁺ actions.
- **Ca×PO₄ ion product** — solubility constraint; PTH-induced phosphaturia keeps it subsaturated.
- **Hydroxyapatite** — Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂, bone mineral phase.
- **Osteoid** — protein matrix (≈90% type I collagen) that nucleates mineralization.
- **Osteoblast / osteoclast / osteocyte** — bone-forming / bone-resorbing / matrix-embedded mechanosensing cells.
- **BMU (basic multicellular unit)** — site coupling resorption to formation; 1–2 million active in adults.
- **RANK / RANKL / OPG** — osteoclast receptor / osteoblast ligand / decoy receptor; estrogen/glucocorticoid modulate the RANKL:OPG ratio; denosumab = anti-RANKL.
- **Osteocytic osteolysis** — osteocyte-mediated Ca²⁺ transfer from bone interior to surface.
- **PTH** — 84-aa chief-cell hormone; N-terminal 1-34 is active; raises plasma Ca²⁺, lowers phosphate.
- **CaSR** — calcium-sensing receptor (GPCR, Gq/PLC); high Ca²⁺ → inhibits PTH; mutated in FHH.
- **PTH1R** — receptor for PTH (and PTHrP); Gs/AC and Gq/PLC; abundant in kidney and osteoblast.
- **1α-hydroxylase** — renal proximal-tubule enzyme making 1,25-(OH)₂D; PTH ↑, FGF23/calcitriol ↓.
- **25-OH-D / 1,25-(OH)₂-D (calcitriol)** — liver-hydroxylated storage form / kidney-hydroxylated active form.
- **VDR / RXR** — nuclear vitamin D receptor / retinoid X receptor heterodimer; transcription factor.
- **TRPV6 / calbindin** — apical Ca²⁺ channel / cytosolic Ca²⁺-binding protein in duodenal transcellular absorption.
- **NaPi-IIa / NaPi-IIc** — renal/intestinal Na-phosphate cotransporters; inhibited by PTH and FGF23.
- **Calcitonin** — C-cell peptide; inhibits osteoclasts; minor in humans; "escape" via desensitization.
- **FGF23** — osteocyte phosphatonin; lowers phosphate; inhibits 1α-hydroxylase.
- **PTHrP** — PTH-related protein; distinct gene; mimics PTH via PTH1R; humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy.
- **Rickets / osteomalacia / osteoporosis / renal osteodystrophy** — major bone disorders.
