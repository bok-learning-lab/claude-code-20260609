## Section 1 — Glomerular filtration and the clearance method (pp. 739–742)

### Subsection headings
- **A high glomerular filtration rate is essential for maintaining stable and optimal extracellular levels of solutes and water** (pp. 739–740)
- **The clearance of inulin is a measure of GFR** (pp. 740–741)
- **The clearance of creatinine is a useful clinical index of GFR** (pp. 741–742)

### Core claims
- **Normal GFR** for the two kidneys is ~125 mL/min ≈ 180 L/day. This exposes the entire ECF to the tubule epithelium >10 times/day, allowing rapid clearance of filtered wastes and toxins. A low clearance would (1) delay excretion of sudden toxic loads and (2) force very high steady-state plasma levels of filtration-dependent wastes.
- **GFR normalization**: GFR is proportional to body surface area; reported as ~125 mL/min per 1.73 m² in men, ~110 in women. GFR is very low in the newborn (incomplete glomerular development), normalizes by ~2 years, then declines with age as nephrons are lost.
- **Pitts' urea example**: a person with normal GFR (180 L/day) excretes 12 g/day urea nitrogen at a BUN of only ~6.7 mg/dL; an ESRD patient with GFR ~10% of normal (18 L/day) needs BUN ~67 mg/dL to excrete the same 12 g/day. Steady-state plasma waste level rises as GFR falls.
- **Ideal glomerular marker** (Table 34-1): freely filtered; neither reabsorbed nor secreted; not synthesized, degraded, or accumulated by the kidney; physiologically inert. **Inulin** (a 5000-Da fructose polymer from the Jerusalem artichoke) meets all criteria; its plasma clearance equals GFR.
- **Three demonstrations that inulin clearance = GFR**: (1) inulin excretion (Uin·V) is proportional to plasma [inulin], the slope being the clearance; (2) inulin clearance is independent of plasma [inulin]; (3) inulin clearance is independent of urine flow (Uin·V is fixed once filtered, so Uin·V/Pin is fixed). Richards (1941) showed Bowman's-space [inulin] = plasma [inulin] (freely filtered); Marsh and Frasier showed tubules neither secrete nor reabsorb it.
- **Inulin is impractical clinically** (needs IV infusion + demanding chemical assay). ¹²⁵I-iothalamate is a research alternative but also needs IV infusion.
- **Creatinine clearance (Ccr)** is the practical surrogate: creatinine is endogenous (from creatine-phosphate metabolism in muscle, ~20–25 mg/kg/day in men, ~1.5 g/day in a 70-kg man; ~15–20 mg/kg/day in women). At steady state, urinary excretion = production. Tubules secrete some creatinine → Ccr overestimates GFR by ~20% (worse as GFR falls), but it needs no injection.
- **Plasma creatinine (Pcr) as an instant index**: at steady state Pcr is inversely proportional to GFR — a rectangular hyperbola (Fig. 34-2). At GFR 100 mL/min, Pcr ≈ 1 mg/dL (product = 1 mg/min = production). If GFR halves to 50, Pcr doubles to 2; if GFR falls to one-fourth, Pcr rises to 4.

### Equations

- **Clearance form of GFR** (Eq. 34-1 / 34-2; identical to the Ch 33 clearance equation):

  $$ \text{GFR} = \frac{U_X \cdot \dot V}{P_X} \tag{34-2}$$

  with $P_X$ = plasma concentration of marker X, $U_X$ = urine concentration, $\dot V$ = urine flow. For X = inulin this is the GFR.

- **Plasma creatinine as inverse index of GFR** (Eq. 34-3):

  $$ P_{Cr} = \frac{U_{Cr} \cdot \dot V}{C_{Cr}} = \frac{\text{constant}}{\text{GFR}} \tag{34-3}$$

  At steady state $U_{Cr}\cdot\dot V$ (= production) is constant, so $P_{Cr} \propto 1/\text{GFR}$ — a rectangular hyperbola.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Such a large rate of filtrate formation is required to expose the entire extracellular fluid (ECF) frequently (>10 times a day) to the scrutiny of the renal-tubule epithelium." (p. 739)
- > "Thus, the plasma clearance of a glomerular marker is the GFR." (p. 740)
- > "Inulin is freely filtered at the glomerulus, but neither reabsorbed nor secreted by the renal tubules." (p. 740)
- > "Although the inulin clearance is the most reliable method for measuring GFR, it is not practical for clinical use." (p. 741)
- > "Tubules, to a variable degree, secrete creatinine, which, by itself, would lead to a ~20% overestimation of GFR in humans." (p. 741)
- > "Although Ccr may overestimate the absolute level of GFR, assessing changes in Ccr is extremely useful for monitoring relative changes in GFR in patients." (p. 741)

## Section 2 — The filtration barrier and Starling forces (pp. 742–745)

### Subsection headings
- **Molecular size and electrical charge determine the filterability of solutes across the glomerular filtration barrier** (pp. 741–744)
- **Hydrostatic pressure in glomerular capillaries favors glomerular ultrafiltration, whereas oncotic pressure in capillaries and hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's space oppose it** (pp. 744–745)

### Core claims
- **Four-layer filtration barrier**: (1) endothelial glycocalyx, (2) fenestrated endothelial cells, (3) glomerular basement membrane, (4) epithelial podocytes (with their slit diaphragms). Layers 1, 3, and 4 carry fixed negative charge from anionic proteoglycans. Mutations causing massive albuminuria (nephrotic syndrome) generally hit **slit-diaphragm proteins** → the podocyte junctions are the predominant macromolecular barrier.
- **Sieving coefficient** UFx/Px (Table 34-2) is the ratio of ultrafiltrate to plasma concentration. Measured by micropuncture of Bowman's space (valid for any solute) or by the **clearance ratio** Cx/Cin (valid only for solutes the kidney neither reabsorbs nor secretes).
- **Size selectivity**: small solutes (<~5500 Da, e.g. water, urea, glucose, inulin) have UFx/Px ≈ 1 (no sieving — convection carries them with water). Above ~14 kDa (e.g. lysozyme) restriction grows; large macromolecules are increasingly excluded.
- **Charge selectivity** (Fig. 34-4): for dextrans of equal effective radius, **cationic** > **neutral** > **anionic** clearance. Neutral dextrans <2 nm pass freely; restriction begins above ~2 nm and passage ceases at ~4.2 nm. The barrier carries a **net negative charge** that retards anions and favors cations. Removing barrier charge (nephrotoxic serum nephritis) raises anionic-dextran clearance.
- **Albumin** (radius ~3.5 nm): a neutral dextran of the same radius would have a clearance ratio ~0.1, but albumin's strong negative charge drops its ratio to nearly zero. Loss of barrier negative charge → **albuminuria**; heavy filtration → hypoalbuminemia + edema = **nephrotic syndrome**.
- **Starling-force GFR equation**: GFR = Kf × net Starling force. Two forces favor filtration (Pgc and π_BS); two oppose it (P_BS and π_GC).
- **Pressure values along the glomerular capillary**: $P_{GC} \approx 50$ mm Hg (twice that of typical capillaries; afferent + efferent arterioles flanking the bed keep it nearly constant along the length); $P_{BS} \approx 10$ mm Hg (constant); $\pi_{BS}$ ≈ 0; $\pi_{GC}$ starts at ~25 mm Hg and **rises along the capillary** as protein-free filtrate is removed and plasma protein concentrates.
- **Filtration equilibrium**: because π_GC climbs, the net driving force P_UF can reach zero before the end of the capillary — beyond that point no further filtration occurs (filtration equilibrium).
- **Kf = Lp · Sf** (hydraulic conductivity × filtration surface area). The glomerular Kf exceeds the combined Kf of all other systemic beds by >1 order of magnitude — hence ~180 L/day filtered in kidneys (20% of CO) vs. ~20 L/day in all other capillary beds (80% of CO). **Mesangial-cell contraction** (driven by ANG II, AVP, PTH) changes Sf and thus Kf.

### Equations

- **The GFR / Starling-force equation** (Eq. 34-4):

  $$ \text{GFR} = K_f \cdot \left[\,(P_{GC} - P_{BS}) - (\pi_{GC} - \pi_{BS})\,\right] \tag{34-4}$$

  with $K_f = L_p \cdot S_f$. The net ultrafiltration pressure at any point is $P_{UF} = (P_{GC} - P_{BS}) - (\pi_{GC} - \pi_{BS})$. Representative values: $P_{GC}\approx 50$, $P_{BS}\approx 10$, $\pi_{GC}$ rises from ~25, $\pi_{BS}\approx 0$ mm Hg.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The gene mutations that cause excessive urinary excretion of albumin (nephrotic syndrome) generally affect slit diaphragm proteins, which suggests that the junctions between adjacent podocytes are the predominant barrier to filtration of macromolecules." (p. 741)
- > "The previously discussed results suggest that the glomerular filtration barrier carries a net negative charge that restricts the movement of anions but enhances the movement of cations." (p. 743)
- > "Because albumin is highly negatively charged, its clearance ratio is nearly zero … and it is restricted from filtration." (p. 743)
- > "Glomerular ultrafiltration depends on the product of the ultrafiltration coefficient (Kf) and net Starling forces." (p. 743)
- > "Beyond this point, P_UF is zero and the system is said to be in filtration equilibrium (i.e., no further filtration)." (p. 744)
- > "The value of Kf of the glomerular filtration barrier exceeds—by more than an order of magnitude—the Kf of all other systemic capillary beds combined." (p. 744)

## Section 3 — Renal blood flow, arteriolar resistances, and peritubular capillaries (pp. 745–750)

### Subsection headings
- **Increased glomerular plasma flow leads to an increase in GFR** (pp. 745–746)
- **Afferent and efferent arteriolar resistances control both glomerular plasma flow and GFR** (pp. 746–748)
- **Peritubular capillaries provide tubules with nutrients and retrieve reabsorbed fluid** (pp. 747–749)
- **Blood flow in the renal cortex exceeds that in the renal medulla** (p. 749)
- **The clearance of para-aminohippurate is a measure of RPF** (pp. 749–750)

### Core claims
- **RBF** ≈ 1 L/min (20% of the 5-L/min cardiac output); per mass ~350 mL/min per 100 g — about 7× the normalized flow to the brain. **RPF** = (1 − Hct)·RBF ≈ 600 mL/min at Hct 0.40.
- **GFR depends on plasma flow** (Fig. 34-6): at low flow, filtration equilibrium occurs halfway down the capillary; at higher flow the P_UF profile stretches out (filtration disequilibrium) because delivered plasma outstrips the barrier's ability to concentrate protein, so π_GC rises more slowly. The equilibrium point shifts distally — more capillary length stays active, so previously "wasted" distal capillary is "in reserve." SNGFR (sum of filtration events ∝ yellow P_UF·length area) rises with flow, but **non-linearly**: GFR increases only moderately with rising RPF and falls steeply with falling RPF. Acute drops in renal perfusion cause abrupt GFR decline.
- **Filtration fraction** FF = GFR/RPF ≈ 125/600 ≈ 0.2. Because GFR saturates at high RPF, FF is larger at low plasma flows.
- **Two unique microvascular features**: (1) two resistance sites — afferent and efferent arterioles; (2) two capillary beds in series — glomerular then peritubular. Large pressure drops occur across both arterioles (Fig. 34-7); Pgc stays high; peritubular pressure stays low.
- **Reciprocal resistance change (constant total R, constant flow)**: constricting the afferent (relaxing efferent) lowers Pgc; constricting the efferent (relaxing afferent) raises Pgc.
- **Single-arteriole changes** (more realistic): constricting only the afferent (Fig. 34-8B) lowers both Pgc and RPF → GFR falls monotonically. Constricting only the efferent (Fig. 34-8C) raises Pgc but lowers RPF → GFR rises at low resistance (Pgc dominates) then falls at high resistance (declining RPF dominates) — biphasic.
- **Combined afferent + efferent constriction** (sympathetic stimulation, ANG II): RPF falls but GFR stays fairly constant because the opposing GFR effects cancel.
- **Clinical correlates**: loss of renal tissue (nephrectomy in a donor) → afferent dilation, large RPF rise, GFR in the remnant nearly doubles. CHF with high vasodilatory prostaglandins (PGE₂, PGI₂) → GFR depends on prostaglandin-mediated afferent dilation; **NSAIDs** can cause acute GFR fall. CHF with high angiotensin → GFR depends on efferent constriction; **ACE inhibitors** can cause abrupt GFR fall.
- **Peritubular capillaries** arise from efferent arterioles (superficial glomeruli → cortical network; juxtamedullary → vasa recta in medulla). Functions: deliver O₂/nutrients to tubule cells and take up reabsorbed fluid. Filtration concentrates plasma protein, so peritubular oncotic pressure π_pc ≈ 35 mm Hg while efferent resistance drops intravascular hydrostatic P_pc to ~20 mm Hg → a strong **net absorptive** force throughout the peritubular bed (~17 mm Hg at the arteriolar end falling to ~12 at the venular end). Interstitial π_i ≈ 4–8, P_i ≈ 6–10 mm Hg.
- **Volume expansion** (Fig. 34-10) inhibits renin-angiotensin → efferent resistance falls more than afferent → Pgc and RPF rise, FF falls → blood entering peritubular capillaries has lower π_pc and higher P_pc → less interstitial fluid uptake. Reverse occurs in volume contraction and chronic heart failure. Renal lymph (<1% of RPF) removes interstitial protein.
- **Cortex vs. medulla**: ~90% of efferent flow perfuses cortex; ~10% the medulla; only 1–2% the papilla. Low medullary flow (high vasa-recta resistance) minimizes washout of the hypertonic interstitium → needed for concentrated urine.
- **PAH clearance = RPF**: PAH is filtered (~20%) and avidly secreted by proximal tubules, so ~90% is cleared in one pass (the missing ~10% reflects the medullary blood that bypasses PAH-secreting tubules). Because almost none remains in renal venous blood, C_PAH ≈ RPF without renal-vein sampling. Example: P_PAH 0.1 mg/mL, RPF 600 → arterial load 60 mg/min; 12 filtered + 48 secreted = 60 excreted.

### Equations

- **Renal plasma flow from RBF** (Eq. 34-5):

  $$ \text{RPF} = (1 - \text{Hct}) \cdot \text{RBF} \tag{34-5}$$

  At Hct 0.40 and RBF ~1 L/min, RPF ≈ 600 mL/min.

- **Filtration fraction** (Eq. 34-6):

  $$ \text{FF} = \frac{\text{GFR}}{\text{RPF}} \approx \frac{125}{600} \approx 0.2 \tag{34-6}$$

- **Fick principle for RPF** (Eq. 34-7) — arterial input = venous output + urine output:

  $$ P_{a,X}\cdot\text{RPF}_a = P_{v,X}\cdot\text{RPF}_v + U_X\cdot \dot V \tag{34-7}$$

- **PAH mass balance** (Eq. 34-8): PAH excreted = PAH filtered + PAH secreted.

- **PAH clearance ≈ RPF** (Eqs. 34-9, 34-10) — because almost no PAH leaves in the renal vein:

  $$ C_{PAH} = \frac{U_{PAH}\cdot \dot V}{P_{PAH}} \approx \text{RPF} = \frac{60\ \text{mg/min}}{0.1\ \text{mg/mL}} = 600\ \text{mL/min} \tag{34-9, 34-10}$$

  (Venous blood from the arm is acceptable; skeletal muscle extracts negligible PAH.)

- > "The end of the capillary that is 'wasted' at low plasma flow rates really is 'in reserve' to contribute when blood flow is higher." (p. 746)
- > "Selective constriction or relaxation of the afferent and efferent arterioles allows for highly sensitive control of the hydrostatic pressure in the intervening glomerular capillary, and thus of glomerular filtration." (p. 746)

## Section 4 — Control of RBF and GFR (pp. 750–753)

### Subsection headings
- **Autoregulation keeps RBF and GFR relatively constant** (pp. 750–751)
- **Volume expansion and a high-protein diet increase GFR by reducing TGF** (pp. 751–752)
- **Four factors that modulate RBF and GFR play key roles in regulating effective circulating volume** (pp. 752–753)
- **Other vasoactive agents modulate RBF and GFR** (p. 753)

### Core claims
- **Autoregulation**: RBF and GFR stay within narrow limits as mean arterial pressure varies between ~80 and 170 mm Hg (also seen in brain and heart). It is intrinsic — independent of renal nerves and circulating hormones, persisting in isolated kidneys perfused with cell-free solution. It stabilizes the filtered load delivered to tubules and protects glomerular capillaries from pressure damage.
- **Mechanism**: a rise in arterial pressure is met by a proportional rise in **afferent** arteriolar resistance (efferent, capillary, and venous resistances change little). Over the low-pressure range (e.g. CHF), efferent resistance increases to minimize GFR loss. Two equally important mechanisms underlie autoregulation: the **myogenic response** and **tubuloglomerular feedback**.
- **Myogenic response**: afferent smooth muscle contracts when stretched — stretch-activated nonselective cation channels open → depolarization → Ca²⁺ influx → contraction (p. 481, p. 477).
- **Tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF)**: the **macula densa** cells (in the thick ascending limb, part of the juxtaglomerular apparatus) sense a rise in GFR via increased luminal NaCl delivery and translate it into afferent constriction → ↓ Pgc, ↓ RPF, ↓ GFR. Experimentally: a wax block isolates a tubule; an upstream pipette measures SNGFR while a downstream pipette perfuses the loop of Henle at set rates — SNGFR falls as imposed late-proximal perfusion (delivery to macula densa) rises (inverse relationship).
- **TGF signaling cascade**: ↑GFR → ↑NaCl delivery → macula-densa apical Na/K/Cl cotransporter raises intracellular [Na⁺]/[Cl⁻] (furosemide blocks this and interrupts TGF) → ↑[Cl⁻]ᵢ + basolateral Cl⁻ channel → depolarization → basolateral nonselective cation channel → Ca²⁺ entry → release of **adenosine and ATP** (ATP → adenosine) → adenosine on A₁ receptors of vascular smooth muscle → afferent contraction → ↑afferent resistance → ↓GFR (negative feedback).
- **TGF sensitivity** (Table 34-3): increased by volume contraction, adenosine, PGE₂, thromboxane, HETE, ANG II; decreased by volume expansion, ANP, NO, cAMP, PGI₂, high-protein diet. ANG II is a required cofactor for TGF.
- **High-protein diet** increases GFR/RPF by lowering TGF sensitivity: it enhances proximal NaCl reabsorption, so luminal [NaCl] at the macula densa falls; flow (and GFR) must rise to restore macula-densa [NaCl]. Over time, especially with intrinsic renal disease, the elevated Pgc can cause progressive glomerular damage.
- **Four effector pathways of effective circulating volume**: (1) renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis, (2) sympathetic nervous system, (3) AVP, (4) atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP).
  - **ANG II** (Table 34-4): mainly efferent constriction (Ca²⁺ from internal stores; insensitive to Ca²⁺-channel blockers) — maintains GFR when perfusion is reduced because prostaglandins offset afferent constriction. Also contracts mesangial cells (↓Kf), increases TGF sensitivity, reduces medullary flow. NSAIDs (blocking the protective prostaglandins) unmask afferent constriction → GFR falls.
  - **Sympathetic nerves**: moderate stimulation raises both afferent and efferent resistance (RPF falls more than GFR — preferential efferent constriction); maximal stimulation → afferent predominates → drastic RBF and GFR fall. Also stimulates renin release and proximal Na⁺ reabsorption.
  - **AVP**: principally promotes collecting-duct water reabsorption; modest vasoconstrictor with little net effect on total RBF/GFR, but can reduce medullary flow (preserving hypertonicity). Severe volume loss (shock) → massive nonosmotic AVP release → systemic vasoconstriction.
  - **ANP**: dilates afferent and efferent arterioles → ↑cortical and medullary flow, lowers TGF sensitivity → net ↑RPF and ↑GFR; also inhibits renin and aldosterone and directly inhibits inner-medullary-collecting-duct Na⁺ reabsorption → natriuresis.
- **Other vasoactive agents**: epinephrine (norepinephrine-like); dopamine (vasodilation + ↓Na⁺ reabsorption, opposite to catecholamines); endothelins (potent local vasoconstrictors, ↓Kf, ↓RBF and GFR); prostaglandins (mainly protective — prevent excess vasoconstriction during high sympathetic/ANG II tone, maintain flow during surgery/blood loss/salt depletion); leukotrienes (vasoconstrictors in inflammation); nitric oxide (NOS → NO from L-arginine; tonic vasodilation defending against ANG II/epinephrine; NOS inhibition constricts both arterioles and blunts TGF-mediated vasodilation).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "An important feature of the renal circulation is its remarkable ability to maintain RBF and GFR within narrow limits, although mean arterial pressure may vary between ~80 and 170 mm Hg." (p. 750)
- > "Autoregulation of the renal blood supply is independent of the influence of renal nerves and circulating hormones, and persists even when one perfuses isolated kidneys with erythrocyte-free solutions." (p. 750)
- > "Two basic mechanisms—equally important—underlie renal autoregulation: a myogenic response of the smooth muscle of the afferent arterioles and a tubuloglomerular feedback mechanism." (p. 751)
- > "Macula densa cells do not sense flow per se, but the higher luminal [Na⁺] or [Cl⁻] resulting from high flow." (p. 751)
- > "Under normal conditions, the effect of ANG II is primarily to mediate efferent arteriolar constriction, an effect that tends to maintain GFR when renal perfusion is reduced." (p. 752)
- > "Prostaglandins appear mainly to be protective and are important under conditions in which the integrity of the renal circulation is threatened." (p. 753)

## Glossary

- **GFR** — glomerular filtration rate; ~125 mL/min (180 L/day) for both kidneys; 125 (men) / 110 (women) mL/min per 1.73 m².
- **Clearance** — Cx = Ux·V̇/Px; for an ideal marker, clearance = GFR.
- **Inulin** — ideal GFR marker (5000-Da fructose polymer); freely filtered, not handled by tubules.
- **Creatinine clearance (Ccr)** — clinical GFR surrogate; overestimates GFR ~20% (tubular secretion).
- **Pcr–GFR hyperbola** — at steady state, Pcr ∝ 1/GFR.
- **Filtration barrier** — endothelial glycocalyx, fenestrated endothelium, GBM, podocyte slit diaphragm; net negatively charged.
- **Sieving coefficient (UFx/Px)** — ultrafiltrate-to-plasma concentration ratio; 1.0 for small solutes, →0 for large/anionic ones.
- **Clearance ratio** — Cx/Cin; used to estimate sieving for non-handled solutes.
- **Kf** — ultrafiltration coefficient = Lp·Sf; glomerular Kf >10× other beds combined.
- **Starling-force GFR equation** — GFR = Kf·[(Pgc − P_BS) − (π_GC − π_BS)].
- **Filtration equilibrium / disequilibrium** — P_UF reaches zero before the capillary end (low flow) vs. stays positive throughout (high flow).
- **RBF / RPF** — ~1 L/min; RPF = (1 − Hct)·RBF ≈ 600 mL/min.
- **Filtration fraction (FF)** — GFR/RPF ≈ 0.2.
- **PAH clearance** — ≈ RPF (filtered + nearly completely secreted; ~90% extracted per pass).
- **Peritubular capillaries / vasa recta** — efferent-derived; net absorptive Starling forces; high π_pc (~35) from upstream filtration.
- **Autoregulation** — RBF/GFR constant over MAP ~80–170 mm Hg via afferent resistance.
- **Myogenic response** — stretch-activated afferent constriction.
- **Tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF)** — macula densa senses luminal NaCl → adenosine/ATP → afferent constriction → ↓GFR.
- **Juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)** — macula densa + granular (renin-secreting) cells + afferent arteriole.
- **Effector pathways of effective circulating volume** — renin-angiotensin-aldosterone, sympathetic nerves, AVP, ANP.
