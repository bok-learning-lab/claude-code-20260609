## Section 1 — Water balance and overall renal handling of water (pp. 806–808)

### Subsection headings
- **The kidney can generate a urine as dilute as 40 mOsm or as concentrated as 1200 mOsm** (pp. 806–807)
- **Free-water clearance (C_H₂O) is positive if the kidney produces urine less concentrated than plasma and negative if more concentrated** (pp. 807–808)

### Core claims
- **Steady-state water balance**: intake = output. Inputs ≈ 2500 mL/day (ingested fluid ~1200, food water ~1000, metabolic water ~300). Outputs ≈ 2500 mL/day (urine ~1500, feces ~100, skin/sweat ~550, exhaled air ~350). See Table 38-1.
- **The kidney is the central regulator of water balance.** It adjusts water output to compensate for abnormal intake or non-renal losses.
- **Fixed solute load**: on a normal diet the kidney excretes ~600 milliosmoles/day **regardless of the water volume**. Therefore urine osmolality (U_osm) and urine flow (V) trade off reciprocally.
- **Concentrating range is asymmetric**: the kidney can dilute urine to ~40 mOsm (≈1/7 of plasma → ~15 L/day) but concentrate only to ~1200 mOsm (≈4× plasma → ~0.5 L/day). So ~7-fold dilution vs. ~4-fold concentration. Renal failure narrows both.
- **Two moieties of urine**: (1) the isosmotic volume needed to dissolve all excreted solutes at plasma osmolality (osmolal clearance, C_osm), plus (2) the free water that must be added (dilute urine) or subtracted (concentrated urine).

### Equations

- **Constant solute output** (Eq. 38-1):

  $$\text{osmoles excreted/day} = U_{osm} \cdot \dot V$$

  where $U_{osm}$ is urine osmolality and $\dot V$ is urine output/day.

- **Maximal dilution** (Eq. 38-3): with $U_{osm} \approx 40$ mOsm and a 600-milliosmole load, $\dot V \approx 15$ L/day.
- **Maximal concentration** (Eq. 38-4): with $U_{osm} \approx 1200$ mOsm, $\dot V \approx 0.5$ L/day.

- **Decomposition of urine flow** (Eq. 38-5):

  $$\dot V = C_{osm} + C_{H_2O}$$

- **Osmolal clearance** (Eq. 38-6) — computed like any clearance:

  $$C_{osm} = \frac{U_{osm} \cdot \dot V}{P_{osm}}$$

  the hypothetical volume of plasma fully cleared of osmoles per unit time. With solute excretion fixed at 600 milliosmoles/day and $P_{osm} = 300$ milliosmoles/L, $C_{osm} = 2$ L/day.

- **Free-water clearance** (Eq. 38-7) — obtained only by subtraction:

  $$C_{H_2O} = \dot V - C_{osm}$$

  Note (per N38-1) this is a "misnomer": $C_{H_2O} \neq (U_{H_2O}\cdot \dot V)/P_{H_2O}$, but the term is conventional.

- **Worked extremes**:
  - Isosmotic urine ($U_{osm}=P_{osm}$): $C_{osm}=\dot V$, so $C_{H_2O}=0$ (Eq. 38-8).
  - Maximal dilution: $C_{H_2O} = 15 - 2 = +13$ L/day (Eq. 38-9).
  - Maximal concentration: $C_{H_2O} = 0.5 - 2 = -1.5$ L/day (Eq. 38-10).

- > "Therefore, the kidney is capable of diluting the urine ~7-fold with respect to blood plasma, but it is capable of concentrating the urine only ~4-fold." (p. 807)

- > "This wide range of C_H₂O represents the kidneys' attempt to stabilize the osmolality of extracellular fluid in the face of changing loads of solutes or water." (p. 807)

## Section 2 — Water transport by different nephron segments (pp. 808–810)

### Subsection headings
- **The kidney concentrates urine by driving water via osmosis from the tubule lumen into a hyperosmotic interstitium** (pp. 808–809)
- **Tubule fluid is isosmotic in the proximal tubule, becomes dilute in the loop of Henle, and then either remains dilute or becomes concentrated by the end of the collecting duct** (pp. 808–809)

### Core claims
- **No water pumps exist** (pp. 127–128). The kidney concentrates urine entirely by **osmosis**: it builds a hyperosmotic interstitium in the confined renal medulla, then allows the collecting-duct lumen to equilibrate with it under regulated water permeability.
- **Two key elements of a concentrated urine**: (1) the hyperosmotic medullary interstitium that supplies the osmotic gradient, and (2) AVP, which raises the water permeability of the distal nephron.
- **Osmolality profile along the nephron** (Fig. 38-1):
  - **Proximal tubule** reabsorbs ~2/3 of filtered fluid **isosmotically** — tubule fluid osmolality unchanged regardless of final urine.
  - **TAL (the "diluting segment")** reabsorbs salt in excess of water, so tubule fluid leaving the loop and DCT is **hypo-osmotic** to plasma in all states.
  - **Collecting system (ICT, CCT, OMCD, IMCD)**: AVP regulates the variable fraction of water reabsorbed here, deciding whether final urine is dilute or concentrated.
- **Antidiuresis** (high AVP): from ICT onward, tubule fluid osmolality rises, equilibrating with cortical interstitium (~290 mOsm) by the end of the ICT, then rising sharply in the MCDs to as much as ~4× plasma at the papillary tip.
- **Water diuresis** (low AVP): distal water permeability stays low; continued NaCl reabsorption separates salt from water, so tubule fluid becomes **progressively more hypo-osmotic** from DCT through the rest of the nephron.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Instead, the kidney uses osmosis as the driving force to concentrate the contents of the tubule lumen." (p. 808)
- > "The proximal tubule, regardless of the final osmolality of the urine, reabsorbs two thirds of the filtered fluid isosmotically." (p. 808)
- > "The fluid exiting the DCT is hypo-osmotic with respect to plasma, regardless of the final urine osmolality." (p. 808)
- > "The two key elements in producing a concentrated urine are (1) the hyperosmotic medullary interstitium that provides the osmotic gradient, and (2) the AVP that raises the water permeability of the distal nephron." (p. 809)

## Section 3 — Generation of a hyperosmotic medulla and urine (pp. 810–817)

### Subsection headings
- **The renal medulla is hyperosmotic to blood plasma during both antidiuresis and water diuresis** (pp. 810–811)
- **NaCl transport generates only a ~200-mOsm gradient across the ascending limb, but countercurrent exchange can multiply this single effect to a 900-mOsm corticopapillary gradient** (pp. 811–812)
- **The single effect is passive NaCl reabsorption in the thin ascending limb and active NaCl reabsorption in the TAL** (pp. 812–813)
- **The IMCD reabsorbs urea, producing high inner-medullary interstitial urea** (pp. 813–815)
- **The vasa recta's countercurrent exchange and low blood flow minimize washout of medullary hyperosmolality** (pp. 814–816)
- **The MCD produces a concentrated urine by osmosis, driven by the gradient between interstitium and lumen** (pp. 816–817)

### Core claims — the countercurrent multiplier
- **The loop of Henle separates NaCl from water**, depositing NaCl in the medullary interstitium. It directly forms dilute urine (lumen left hypo-osmotic) and indirectly enables concentrated urine (interstitium made hyperosmotic).
- **Single effect**: at any transverse level the ascending limb can build only a ~**200-mOsm** gradient between lumen and interstitium. This alone cannot reach 1200 mOsm at the papilla.
- **Multiplication**: the **hairpin** geometry plus countercurrent flow multiplies the 200-mOsm single effect into a longitudinal **~900-mOsm** corticopapillary gradient. Each cycle = a single effect (NaCl moved out of the ascending limb) followed by an axial shift of fluid; iterating the cycle raises the papillary tip osmolality (in the worked model, ~300 → 600 mOsm over 7 steps; ~1200 mOsm after ~39 cycles).
- **Loop length sets the maximum**: among mammals, longer loops (relative to cortical thickness) → higher attainable medullary osmolality (desert rodents concentrate best).
- **Two limbs, two mechanisms of the single effect**:
  - **TAL (active)**: takes up Na⁺/K⁺/Cl⁻ via the apical **Na/K/Cl cotransporter (NKCC2)**, exports Na⁺ via basolateral Na-K pumps and Cl⁻ channels (transcellular), plus a lumen-positive voltage drives paracellular Na⁺ — together generating up to a 200-mOsm single effect across a water-impermeable wall.
  - **tALH (passive)**: thin cells with few mitochondria cannot do intensive active transport. NaCl is reabsorbed **passively** because luminal [NaCl] exceeds interstitial [NaCl]. The Cl⁻ channel **ClC-K1**, localized to the tALH, is pivotal (ClC-K1 knockout mice have a concentrating defect).
- **How luminal [NaCl] got high**: the work was done earlier in the **thin descending limb (tDLH)**, which (1) is highly water-permeable (**AQP1**), (2) has very low NaCl permeability and a finite urea permeability (**UT-A2**), and (3) sits in an interstitium with high [NaCl] and [urea] — so water leaves the tDLH and luminal NaCl is concentrated.
- **Interstitial composition** (Fig. 38-4): [Na⁺], [Cl⁻], and [urea] all rise cortex → papilla. In the **outer medulla**, the steep rise is dominated by NaCl from the TAL. In the **inner medulla**, [urea] rises steeply; at the papillary tip **urea and NaCl each contribute ~half** the interstitial osmolality, with [Na⁺] and [Cl⁻] each ~300 mM.

### Core claims — urea recycling
- **Urea handling is concentration-dependent**: filtered urea; ~half reabsorbed in the proximal tubule; net fractional excretion ranges from ~15% (minimal flow) to ~65%+ (maximal flow).
- **Three-step recycling loop** (Fig. 38-5): (1) **IMCD reabsorbs urea** into the inner-medullary interstitium via AVP-stimulated **UT-A1** (apical) and **UT-A3** (basolateral); (2) urea is **secreted** from the interstitium back into the thin limbs (into tDLH via **UT-A2**, into tALH via an unidentified transporter); (3) urea is carried up to the cortex and back down through TAL→IMCD. This recycling builds the high inner-medullary [urea].
- In the segments TAL→OMCD, urea permeability is low while water is reabsorbed (AVP present), so luminal [urea] climbs to **8–10× plasma** by the end of the OMCD. The IMCD then becomes urea-permeable (AVP-stimulated), releasing urea into the interstitium.
- **Protein diet**: more dietary protein → more urea → greater medullary hyperosmolality → greater concentrating ability.
- **Water diuresis (low AVP)**: IMCD permeability to urea and water falls, urea may be actively secreted by an apical Na-urea exchanger in the distal IMCD, interstitial [urea] is lower, and more urea appears in urine.

### Core claims — countercurrent exchange in the vasa recta
- **The washout problem**: a single straight permeable vessel (Fig. 38-6A) would equilibrate with the rising interstitial osmolality and carry the hyperosmolar solute away, washing out the gradient.
- **Two solutions**: (1) **low medullary blood flow** — only 5–10% of total renal plasma flow, a compromise between oxygen delivery and washout avoidance; (2) the **hairpin vasa recta** acting as a **countercurrent exchanger** (Fig. 38-6B). Vasa recta have no active transport.
- **Mechanism**: descending vasa recta gain solute (NaCl, plus urea via **UT-B1/UT-B2** facilitated diffusion) and lose water; ascending vasa recta lose solute and gain water. Solute recirculates ascending→interstitium→descending; water is short-circuited descending→interstitium→ascending. Net effect: solutes are **trapped in** and water **excluded from** the medulla, preserving the gradient.
- **Steady-state mass balance**: ascending vasa recta must carry out **more salt and more water** than the descending vasa recta bring in — removing the NaCl and urea continuously deposited by the tubules and the water given up by the tDLH and AVP-stimulated MCD.

### Core claims — the MCD as a straight-tube exchanger
- The MCD is an **unlooped/straight-tube exchanger**. Wall permeability properties: (1) without AVP, relatively impermeable to water, urea, and NaCl along its whole length; (2) AVP increases **water** permeability along its whole length; (3) AVP increases **urea** permeability only in the terminal IMCD.
- **NaCl vs. urea gradients** (Fig. 38-7): the [NaCl] gradient across the wall always favors water reabsorption; the [urea] gradient (low cortical interstitial urea) opposes it. Because ICT/CCT/OMCD are urea-impermeable, luminal [urea] rises there, opposing water uptake — but the NaCl gradient is large enough that net water reabsorption still occurs.
- **The IMCD's trick**: AVP raises IMCD urea permeability → low urea **reflection coefficient** (σ_urea ≈ 0.74) → any transepithelial [urea] difference produces only a small *effective* osmotic pressure. With high interstitial [NaCl] (σ_NaCl ≈ 1.0), water reabsorption continues even when luminal [urea] exceeds interstitial [urea]. The high urea permeability also raises interstitial [urea], further reducing the handicap.
- **Why evolve high luminal urea?** (1) high urinary [urea] lets the body excrete nitrogenous waste without large water loss; (2) the kidney exploits urea: high interstitial [urea] energizes luminal NaCl concentration in the tDLH → fuels the tALH single effect → high inner-medullary [NaCl] that directly concentrates urine.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The limiting NaCl concentration gradient that the tubule can develop at any point along its length is only ~200 mOsm, and this concentration alone could not explain the ability of the kidney to raise the osmolality of the papilla to 1200 mOsm." (p. 811)
- > "The hairpin loops of Henle create a countercurrent flow mechanism that multiplies the 'single' transverse gradient of 200 mOsm." (p. 811)
- > "Among mammals, the length of the loop of Henle—compared with the thickness of the renal cortex—determines the maximal osmolality of the medulla." (p. 813)
- > "At the tip of the papilla, urea and NaCl each contribute half of the interstitial osmolality." (p. 813)
- > "This urea recycling is responsible for the buildup of a high [urea] in the inner medulla." (p. 815)
- > "The net effect is that the countercurrent exchanger tends to trap solutes in and exclude water from the medulla, thereby minimizing dissipation of the corticomedullary osmolality gradient." (p. 815)
- > "The net effect of managing both solute and water balance in the medulla is that the ascending vasa recta carry out more salt and more water than the descending vasa recta carry in." (p. 816)

## Section 4 — Regulation by arginine vasopressin (pp. 817–820)

### Subsection headings
- **AVP increases water permeability in all nephron segments beyond the DCT** (pp. 817–819)
- **AVP, via cAMP, causes vesicles containing AQP2 to fuse with the apical membranes of principal cells** (pp. 818–819)
- **AVP increases NaCl reabsorption in the outer medulla and urea reabsorption in the IMCD, enhancing concentrating ability** (pp. 819–820)

### Core claims
- **Synthesis and release**: magnocellular neurons in the **paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei** of the hypothalamus synthesize AVP (a nonapeptide; = ADH), transport it down their axons to the **posterior pituitary**, and release it into the systemic circulation. Increased plasma osmolality and decreased effective circulating volume increase release (detailed in Ch 40).
- **Two target organs**: at high levels (e.g., hypovolemic shock) AVP causes vascular smooth-muscle **vasoconstriction** (raising blood pressure); more importantly it acts on the **kidney** as the major regulator of water excretion, increasing (1) collecting-duct water permeability, (2) TAL NaCl reabsorption, and (3) IMCD urea reabsorption.
- **Aquaporin map** (Fig. 38-8 and Box 38-2):
  - **AQP1** — constitutive, apical + basolateral, in proximal tubule and tDLH (highest water permeability; AVP-insensitive).
  - **tALH → connecting tubule** — constitutively very low water permeability.
  - **AQP2** — apical, principal cells of ICT/CCT/OMCD/IMCD; the **AVP-regulated** channel.
  - **AQP3 and AQP4** — basolateral, principal cells; provide the exit pathway to peritubular fluid; AVP-insensitive.
- **V₂-receptor signaling cascade** (Fig. 38-9): AVP binds basolateral **V₂ receptors** on principal cells → activates **Gₛ** → stimulates **adenylyl cyclase** → **cAMP** → **protein kinase A** → phosphorylates AQP2 and trafficking proteins → AQP2-containing vesicles fuse with the apical membrane by **exocytosis**, raising apical AQP2 **density** (not single-channel conductance). When AVP falls, **endocytosis** retrieves AQP2. AVP also regulates AQP2 **protein abundance** long-term (transcription of the AQP2 gene).
- **Modulators of the cascade**: high [Ca²⁺]ᵢ and [Li⁺] inhibit adenylyl cyclase → ↓cAMP → diuresis; colchicine disrupts the cytoskeleton → ↓AQP2 insertion; phosphodiesterase inhibitors (theophylline) raise cAMP → ↑water permeability.
- **AVP beyond water permeability**: in the outer medulla AVP (via cAMP) stimulates **apical Na/K/Cl cotransport (NKCC2)** and K⁺ recycling in the TAL → raises outer-medullary interstitial osmolality; stimulates ENaC-mediated Na⁺ reabsorption in the CCT (mostly rodent data, minor in humans). In the inner medulla, the same cAMP rise that inserts AQP2 also phosphorylates **UT-A1 (apical)** and **UT-A3 (basolateral)** → increased urea reabsorption and high interstitial [urea].

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Large-bodied neurons in the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus synthesize AVP, a nonapeptide also known as ADH." (p. 817)
- > "AVP dramatically increases the water permeabilities of the collecting tubules (ICT and CCT) and ducts (OMCD and IMCD) by causing AQP2 water channels to insert into the apical membrane." (p. 818)
- > "These water channels are AVP sensitive, not in the sense that AVP increases their single channel water conductance, but rather that it increases their density in the apical membrane." (p. 818)
- > "Apical AQP2 is the basis for AVP-regulated water permeability. AQP3 and AQP4 are present in the basolateral membrane of principal cells, where they provide an exit pathway for water." (p. 819, Box 38-2)
- > "AVP enhances the urea permeability of the terminal two thirds of the IMCD … leads to a phosphorylation of apical UT-A1 and basolateral UT-A3 urea transporters … increasing their activity." (pp. 819–820)

- **Table 38-2** — Factors that modulate urinary concentration and dilution:
  1. **Osmotic gradient of medullary interstitium** — (a) length of loops of Henle (long-looped desert rodents concentrate more); (b) rate of active NaCl reabsorption in the TAL (raised by high Na⁺ delivery / high GFR / Na-K pump turnover; reduced by loop diuretics); (c) dietary protein content (more urea → more inner-medullary hyperosmolality).
  2. **Medullary blood flow** — low flow preserves high interstitial osmolality; high flow washes out solutes.
  3. **Osmotic permeability of collecting tubules/ducts to water** — AVP enhances it.
  4. **Luminal flow in loop and collecting duct** — high flow (osmotic diuresis) reduces multiplier efficiency and equilibration time.
  5. **Pathophysiology** — central DI (low plasma AVP) vs. nephrogenic DI (renal unresponsiveness to AVP).

## Clinical boxes

- **Box 38-1 — Diabetes Insipidus (DI).** Two varieties: **central (neurogenic)** DI = failure of AVP secretion (hypothalamic or pituitary lesion: injury, tumor, infection, autoimmune; idiopathic or familial); **nephrogenic** DI (NDI) = renal unresponsiveness to normal/elevated AVP (90% of familial cases = X-linked **AVPR2** [V₂ receptor] mutations, 10% = **AQP2** mutations; acquired causes include K⁺ depletion, hypercalcemia, sickle-cell renal disease, Li⁺ salts, colchicine). Both present with **polyuria and polydipsia**; risk of hypernatremia/shock if water access is denied. Diagnosis: **fluid-deprivation test** (urine stays dilute); then exogenous AVP raises urine osmolality >50% in central DI but little in NDI. Treatment: **DDAVP** (desmopressin) for central DI; for NDI, treat the underlying disease, and a **thiazide diuretic + dietary Na⁺ restriction** (induce mild volume depletion → enhanced proximal reabsorption → less polyuria). Distinguish from **osmotic diuresis** (untreated diabetes mellitus, mannitol — high solute excretion) and **primary polydipsia** (water deprivation helps polydipsia but harms DI).
- **Box 38-2 — Role of Aquaporins in Renal Water Transport.** AQP1 (proximal tubule, tDLH); AQP2/3/4 in collecting-duct principal cells (apical AQP2 = AVP-regulated; basolateral AQP3/4 = exit). Short-term regulation = AQP2 vesicle trafficking; long-term = increased AQP2 transcription. AQP1 knockout → reduced proximal fluid absorption; AQP2 mutation → NDI. Third-trimester **vasopressinase** (placental aminopeptidase) can mimic central DI. Acquired AQP2 up-regulation accompanies fluid-retentive states (CHF, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, pregnancy, SIADH).
- **Box 38-3 — Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH Secretion (SIADH).** The opposite of DI: inappropriately high AVP given low plasma osmolality and no hypovolemia → inappropriately concentrated urine, inability to excrete water loads → ↑total-body water, hypo-osmolar blood, **hyponatremia**, cell swelling (headache, nausea, behavioral changes; severe → stupor, coma, seizures). Four major causes: (1) malignancies (bronchogenic carcinoma, sarcomas, lymphomas, leukemias) secreting AVP/AVP-like substances; (2) cranial disorders (trauma, meningitis, abscess); (3) nonmalignant pulmonary disease (TB, pneumonia, abscess) and positive-pressure ventilation; (4) drugs that stimulate AVP release (clofibrate, phenothiazines), sensitize tubules (chlorpropamide), or both (carbamazepine). Treatment: address the cause + **fluid restriction**; for severe symptomatic hyponatremia, **gradual** hyperosmotic Na⁺ infusion (rapid correction risks central pontine myelinolysis).

## Glossary

- **Osmolal clearance (C_osm)** — U_osm·V̇/P_osm; isosmotic moiety of urine flow.
- **Free-water clearance (C_H₂O)** — V̇ − C_osm; positive in dilute urine, negative in concentrated urine.
- **Countercurrent multiplier** — loop of Henle; iterates a ~200-mOsm single effect into a ~900-mOsm corticopapillary gradient.
- **Single effect** — the ~200-mOsm transverse lumen-to-interstitium gradient an ascending-limb level can generate.
- **TAL / diluting segment** — active NaCl reabsorption via apical NKCC2; water-impermeable; generates dilute tubule fluid.
- **NKCC2** — apical Na/K/Cl cotransporter of the TAL.
- **tALH** — thin ascending limb; passive NaCl reabsorption (ClC-K1 Cl⁻ channel).
- **tDLH** — thin descending limb; water-permeable (AQP1), low NaCl permeability; concentrates luminal NaCl.
- **Corticopapillary (corticomedullary) osmotic gradient** — interstitial osmolality rising cortex (~300) → papilla (~1200 mOsm).
- **Urea recycling** — IMCD reabsorption (UT-A1/A3) → thin-limb secretion (UT-A2) → cortical return; builds inner-medullary [urea].
- **UT-A1/A3** — AVP-stimulated IMCD urea transporters (apical/basolateral). **UT-A2** — tDLH urea transporter. **UT-B1/B2** — vasa recta urea transporters.
- **Countercurrent exchanger (vasa recta)** — hairpin vessels that trap solute and exclude water, preserving the gradient.
- **Reflection coefficient (σ)** — σ_NaCl ≈ 1.0, σ_urea ≈ 0.74 in the IMCD; low σ_urea lets water reabsorption continue despite luminal urea.
- **AQP1–4** — water channels; AQP1 (PT/tDLH), AQP2 (apical, AVP-regulated), AQP3/AQP4 (basolateral exit).
- **V₂ receptor (AVPR2)** — basolateral AVP receptor; Gₛ → cAMP → PKA → AQP2 trafficking.
- **Diabetes insipidus** — central (AVP-deficient) vs. nephrogenic (AVP-resistant; AVPR2/AQP2 mutations).
- **SIADH** — inappropriately high AVP → hyponatremia.
