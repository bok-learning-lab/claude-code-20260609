# Chapter 43 — Pancreatic and Salivary Glands

> Section VII · The Gastrointestinal System · pp. 879–898 · Authors: Christopher R. Marino & Fred S. Gorelick

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Overview of exocrine gland physiology** (pp. 879–882) — acinus + duct architecture; acinar cells (protein export) vs. duct cells (fluid/electrolyte transport); the two-population model of the final secretion; goblet cells and mucins.
2. **Pancreatic acinar cell** (pp. 882–885) — regulated enzyme secretion; M₃ and CCK₁ receptors; Ca²⁺/cAMP second messengers; isotonic NaCl-rich plasma-like fluid (Cl⁻-driven).
3. **Pancreatic duct cell** (pp. 885–888) — isotonic NaHCO₃ secretion; apical CFTR + SLC26 Cl-HCO₃ exchange; secretin (cAMP) vs. ACh (Ca²⁺); CFTR as the master effector; cystic fibrosis.
4. **Composition, function, and control of pancreatic secretion** (pp. 887–893) — protein-rich alkaline juice; flow-dependent HCO₃/Cl (two-component model); interdigestive cycling; cephalic/gastric/intestinal phases; CCK and secretin; mechanisms protecting against autodigestion / pancreatitis.
5. **Salivary acinar cell** (pp. 893–895) — serous vs. mucous acini; α-amylase, mucins, proline-rich proteins; autonomic (both sympathetic and parasympathetic) control; Ca²⁺ and cAMP.
6. **Salivary duct cell** (pp. 894–898) — hypotonic final saliva; ductal Na/Cl reabsorption exceeding K/HCO₃ secretion in a water-impermeable duct; aldosterone; myoepithelial cells.

## Section 1 — Overview of exocrine gland physiology (pp. 879–882)

### Subsection headings
- **The pancreas and major salivary glands are compound exocrine glands** (pp. 879–880)
- **Acinar cells are specialized protein-synthesizing cells** (pp. 879–881)
- **Duct cells are epithelial cells specialized for fluid and electrolyte transport** (pp. 881)
- **Goblet cells contribute to mucin production in exocrine glands** (pp. 881–882)

### Core claims
- The exocrine pancreas and major salivary glands are **compound exocrine glands**: branching ductular trees draining microscopic secretory units. The functional unit is an **acinus** (15–100 acinar cells) plus a small **intercalated duct**. Acini drain into intercalated → intralobular → interlobular → main ducts.
- **Two epithelial-cell populations** produce the final secretion: the **acinar cell** (protein-rich primary secretion) and the **duct cell** (modifies fluid and electrolyte composition). The final exocrine secretion is the combined product of both.
- **Acinar cells** are polarized, with extensive rough ER and a hallmark apical pool of electron-dense **secretory (zymogen) granules**. Pancreatic granules hold ~20 digestive zymogens + enzymes; salivary granules hold α-amylase (parotid) or mucins (sublingual).
- **Secretory pathway** (Fig. 43-2): rough ER → Golgi → condensing vacuoles/immature granules (acidic, lowest pH in the pathway) → mature zymogen granules. Lysosomal enzymes are sorted away via the mannose-6-phosphate receptor; the signal sorting digestive enzymes into the regulated pathway remains unclear.
- **Exocytosis**: granule moves to apical membrane, SNARE-mediated fusion, content release into the acinar lumen. Apical membrane area transiently increases up to ~30-fold, then is retrieved by endocytosis. Vacuolar H⁺ pumps acidify granules; parallel Cl⁻ entry yields net HCl uptake and water by osmosis, hydrating granule proteins.
- **Duct cells** are mitochondria-rich, transporter-laden epithelial cells with basolateral infolding; synthetic machinery is far less developed than in acinar cells. **Centroacinar cells** at the acinus-duct junction express high carbonic anhydrase and likely contribute to HCO₃ secretion.
- **Goblet cells** (mostly in large distal ducts) secrete high-molecular-weight **mucins** that hydrate to mucus — lubrication, hydration, mechanical protection, pathogen binding.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The final exocrine gland secretion represents the combined product of two distinct epithelial-cell populations, the acinar cell and the duct cell." (p. 879)
- > "The most characteristic feature of the acinar cell is the abundance of electron-dense secretory granules at the apical pole of the cell." (p. 879)
- > "In the pancreas, acinar cells secrete ~20 different digestive zymogens (inactive enzyme precursors) and enzymes." (p. 879)
- > "Pancreatic and salivary duct cells are polarized epithelial cells specialized for the transport of electrolytes across distinct apical and basolateral membrane domains." (p. 881)
- > "These cells [centroacinar] express very high levels of carbonic anhydrase and presumably play a role in HCO₃⁻ secretion." (p. 881)

## Section 2 — Pancreatic acinar cell (pp. 882–885)

### Subsection headings
- **The acinar cell secretes digestive proteins in response to stimulation** (pp. 882)
- **Acetylcholine and cholecystokinin mediate the regulated secretion of proteins by pancreatic acinar cells** (pp. 882–883)
- **Ca²⁺ is the major second messenger for the secretion of proteins by pancreatic acinar cells** (pp. 883–884)
- **In addition to proteins, the pancreatic acinar cell secretes a plasma-like fluid** (pp. 884–885)

### Core claims
- Resting acinar cells secrete low levels of protein via a **constitutive** pathway; neurohumoral agents drive a **regulated** pathway (5–10× more amylase over 30–60 min), releasing only ~10–20% of stored protein per stimulation. Amylase activity (secreted fully active) is the standard secretion marker.
- **Two dose-response patterns** (Fig. 43-3): **monophasic** (e.g., GRP — plateaus and stays maximal) and **biphasic** (CCK, carbachol — secretion peaks then falls at supramaximal doses). The biphasic falloff reflects high- and low-affinity receptors and is mechanistically linked to **acute pancreatitis**.
- **Two key receptors**, both basolateral, both Gq-linked, both signaling via PLC/Ca²⁺: the **M₃ muscarinic ACh receptor** and the **CCK₁ receptor** (high affinity for CCK ≫ gastrin; formerly CCK_A). The **CCK₂ receptor** (formerly CCK_B/gastrin receptor) binds CCK and gastrin about equally; its role on human acinar cells is unclear. Many other receptors (GRP, VIP, secretin, somatostatin, insulin) are present.
- **Ca²⁺ is the dominant second messenger.** Resting [Ca²⁺]ᵢ oscillates slowly; physiological CCK/ACh **raise oscillation frequency** (required for secretion). Supramaximal CCK/ACh produces a single large [Ca²⁺]ᵢ spike that halts oscillations and **inhibits** secretion (cytoskeletal disruption) — the pancreatitis-relevant signature (Fig. 43-4B).
- **cAMP** (secretin, VIP, CCK → PKA) potentiates Ca²⁺-driven secretion; simultaneous CCK + VIP is additive (Fig. 43-3B). ACh has little cAMP effect.
- **Isotonic, plasma-like NaCl fluid** (~25% of total pancreatic fluid): the primary event is **apical Cl⁻ secretion**. Basolateral Cl⁻ entry via the **Na/K/Cl cotransporter** (energized by the Na-K pump's Na⁺ gradient), with K⁺ recycled by basolateral K⁺ channels; apical Cl⁻ channels release Cl⁻; lumen-negative voltage drives paracellular Na⁺; water follows (paracellular + aquaporins). ACh and CCK (via Ca²⁺) stimulate this fluid secretion.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The most important [receptor] in regulating protein secretion is the M₃ muscarinic acetylcholine (ACh) receptor … located on the basolateral membrane." (p. 882)
- > "This increase in the frequency of [Ca²⁺]ᵢ oscillations is required for protein secretion by acinar cells." (p. 883)
- > "A supramaximal (i.e., hyperstimulatory) concentration of CCK or ACh generates a sudden [Ca²⁺]ᵢ spike … and eliminates further [Ca²⁺]ᵢ oscillations." (p. 883)
- > "This NaCl-rich fluid hydrates the dense, protein-rich material that the acinar cells secrete. The fundamental transport event is the secretion of Cl⁻ across the apical membrane." (p. 884)
- > "The net effect of these acinar cell transport processes is the production of an isotonic, NaCl-rich fluid that accounts for ~25% of total pancreatic fluid secretion." (p. 884)

## Section 3 — Pancreatic duct cell (pp. 885–888)

### Subsection headings
- **The pancreatic duct cell secretes isotonic NaHCO₃** (pp. 885)
- **Secretin (via cAMP) and ACh (via Ca²⁺) stimulate HCO₃⁻ secretion by pancreatic ducts** (pp. 886–887)
- **Apical membrane chloride channels are important sites of neurohumoral regulation** (pp. 887)
- **Pancreatic duct cells may also secrete glycoproteins** (pp. 887–888)

### Core claims
- The duct cell's job: secrete an **HCO₃⁻-rich fluid** that alkalinizes and hydrates the acinar primary secretion. It accounts for **~75% of total pancreatic fluid secretion** (acinar fluid ~25%).
- **Apical step**: a **SLC26 family Cl-HCO₃ exchanger (SLC26A6, modeled as Cl⁻/2HCO₃⁻)** secretes HCO₃⁻ in exchange for luminal Cl⁻. Luminal Cl⁻ is replenished by **CFTR** (the cAMP-activated apical Cl⁻ channel) — **"Cl⁻ recycling."** SLC26 and CFTR are physically/functionally coupled. Apical Cl⁻ channels (incl. CFTR) may also directly conduct HCO₃⁻. Some species also have an apical Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channel (CaCC).
- **Intracellular HCO₃⁻ supply** comes from (1) basolateral **electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransporter (NBCe1-B / SLC4A4)**, ~1 Na⁺ : 2 HCO₃⁻, and (2) cytoplasmic **carbonic anhydrase** generating HCO₃⁻ from CO₂ + OH⁻. The resulting intracellular **H⁺** is extruded basolaterally by the **Na-H exchanger** and an **ATP-dependent H⁺ pump**. Three basolateral transporters (NBC, NHE, H⁺ pump) feed the secretion.
- **Regulation**: **secretin is the most important humoral regulator** (→ adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates and opens **CFTR** plus stimulates basolateral NBC, without affecting NHE). **ACh** (parasympathetic, muscarinic) acts via **Ca²⁺** (PKC, CaM kinases); atropine-sensitive. **GRP** stimulates; **substance P inhibits** (probably distal to second messengers, by inhibiting the Cl-HCO₃ exchanger). Secretin alone gives less HCO₃ than a meal → it acts in concert with CCK and ACh.
- **CFTR is the central effector**: low-conductance apical Cl⁻ channel with nucleotide-binding domains and a PKA/PKC-phosphorylatable regulatory domain. PKA activation is the dominant regulator; PKC enhances PKA's effect.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The principal physiological function of the pancreatic duct cell is to secrete an HCO₃⁻-rich fluid that alkalinizes and hydrates the protein-rich primary secretions of the acinar cell." (p. 885)
- > "Anion channels on the apical membrane of the duct cell provide additional Cl⁻ to the lumen in a process called Cl⁻ recycling. The most important of these anion channels is the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR)." (p. 885)
- > "The pancreatic duct cell accounts for ~75% of total pancreatic fluid secretion." (p. 886)
- > "Secretin is the most important humoral regulator of ductal HCO₃⁻ secretion." (p. 886)
- > "Agents that activate PKA are the most important regulators of CFTR function." (p. 887)

### Figures

#### Figure 43-6 — HCO₃⁻ secretion by the cells of the pancreatic duct *(viewed)*
A single polarized duct cell, **lumen at left**, **interstitial space at right**.
- **Apical (luminal) membrane:** the **SLC26A6 Cl-HCO₃ exchanger** secretes HCO₃⁻ into the lumen (drawn as Cl⁻ in / 2 HCO₃⁻ out); the **CFTR Cl⁻ channel** and a **Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channel (CaCC)** with an associated **ATP/purinergic receptor** supply luminal Cl⁻ for recycling and may themselves conduct HCO₃⁻.
- **Basolateral membrane:** the **electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransporter** directly imports HCO₃⁻; cytoplasmic **carbonic anhydrase (CA)** makes more HCO₃⁻ from CO₂ + OH⁻; the OH⁻ comes from splitting H₂O, driven by H⁺ extrusion via a **Na-H exchanger** and an **H⁺ pump**.
- **Regulatory annotations:** **secretin → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA → phosphorylates CFTR** (the most powerful stimulus). **ACh → Gq → PLC → DAG (PKC) + IP₃ (Ca²⁺ release)**, also stimulating secretion. The lumen-negative voltage pulls **Na⁺** into the lumen paracellularly; water follows, yielding isotonic NaHCO₃.

> Vision note: Pair with Fig. 43-5. Together they encode the **two-cell / two-fluid** model: acinar Cl⁻-driven NaCl fluid (~25%) + ductal CFTR/SLC26-driven NaHCO₃ fluid (~75%).

#### Box 43-1 — Cystic Fibrosis *(clinical)*
Autosomal-recessive **CFTR** mutation (chromosome 7); most common lethal genetic disease in Europeans (~1/2000). Misfolded CFTR is degraded in the ER → no apical Cl⁻ for Cl-HCO₃ exchange → **reduced ductal HCO₃⁻ and water** → thickened protein-rich secretions, ductal obstruction, fibro-fatty replacement of parenchyma (hence "cystic fibrosis"), pancreatic enzyme insufficiency, **steatorrhea**. Lung disease (now the chief cause of death): thick viscous airway mucus from insufficient fluid secretion + excess ENaC absorption → impaired ciliary clearance → recurrent infection. Elevated sweat [NaCl] is diagnostic.

## Section 4 — Composition, function, and control of pancreatic secretion (pp. 887–893)

### Subsection headings
- **Pancreatic juice is a protein-rich, alkaline secretion** (pp. 887–888)
- **In the fasting state, levels of secreted pancreatic enzymes oscillate at low levels** (pp. 888–889)
- **CCK from duodenal I cells stimulates acinar enzyme secretion** (pp. 889)
- **Secretin from S cells stimulates HCO₃⁻ and fluid secretion by ducts** (pp. 889–890)
- **A meal triggers cephalic, gastric, and intestinal phases of pancreatic secretion** (pp. 890–892)
- **The pancreas has large reserves of digestive enzymes for carbohydrates and proteins, but not for lipids** (pp. 892)
- **Fat in the distal part of the small intestine inhibits pancreatic secretion** (pp. 892)
- **Several mechanisms protect the pancreas from autodigestion** (pp. 892–893)

### Core claims
- Humans make **~1.5 L/day** of pancreatic fluid; the pancreas has the **highest protein-synthesis/secretion rate of any organ**, delivering **15–100 g protein/day**. It secretes >20 proteins (Table 43-1): zymogens (trypsinogens, chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidases A/B) and active enzymes (α-amylase, lipase, carboxyl ester lipase, colipase, RNAase, DNAase), plus trypsin inhibitor, lithostathine, GP2.
- **Two-component model / flow dependence (Fig. 43-7):** at **low flow** the juice resembles plasma; as **secretin-driven flow rises**, apical Cl-HCO₃ exchange raises **[HCO₃⁻]** (to ~140 mEq/L, pH ~8.1) and lowers **[Cl⁻]** reciprocally, while **[Na⁺] and [K⁺] stay roughly constant** near plasma values. The HCO₃⁻ neutralizes gastric acid in the duodenum and aids micellar lipid solubilization.
- **Interdigestive cycling (Fig. 43-8):** basal secretion oscillates with the **migrating motor complexes** — minimal in phase I, rising in phase II, **maximal in phase III** (still only 10–20% of meal-stimulated). Predominantly **parasympathetic (M₁ muscarinic)** control; the M₁ antagonist telenzepine cuts phase II/III enzyme secretion by >85%. CCK and α-adrenergic tone also modulate (α-tone suppresses basal secretion).
- **CCK** (from duodenal **I cells**; rises 5–10× within 10–30 min of a meal): the chief stimulus is **luminal lipid**, then protein digestion products (peptones, amino acids); carbohydrate and acid have little effect. CCK-releasing factors (degraded by basal proteases, spared during a meal) tune the response. CCK acts **directly** (acinar CCK₁ receptor) and **indirectly** (vagovagal cholinergic pathway); in humans the **parasympathetic/vagal route dominates** the intestinal phase — vagal stimulation can drive near-maximal secretion; atropine blocks it.
- **Secretin** (from duodenal **S cells**): released when **duodenal pH falls below 4.5** (and by bile acids/lipids) → most potent stimulus of **ductal HCO₃⁻/fluid** secretion. Acts in concert with CCK and ACh. Islet hormones (insulin) reaching the exocrine pancreas via a portal system modulate enzyme composition and growth.
- **Three phases of a meal (Table 43-2):** **Cephalic** (sight/smell/taste/mastication; vagal; ~25% of max enzyme; atropine-sensitive). **Gastric** (gastric distention → vagovagal gastropancreatic reflex; gastrin; ~10–20%). **Intestinal** (amino/fatty acids → CCK + secretin + enteropancreatic vagovagal reflexes; **50–80%**, the dominant phase). Longer-chain fatty acids and essential amino acids (Phe, Val, Met) are the most potent intestinal stimulants.
- **Enzyme reserves**: large for carbohydrate and protein, **limited for lipid** — fat maldigestion appears only after **80–90% of the pancreas is lost**. Distal small-bowel fat **inhibits** secretion (peptide YY; somatostatin SS-28; glucagon) — feedback return to the interdigestive state.
- **Protection against autodigestion (Table 43-3):** (1) enzymes packaged as inactive **zymogens** — activated only in the gut, where **enterokinase converts trypsinogen → trypsin**, which then activates the rest of the cascade; (2) granule membrane impermeable to proteins; (3) co-packaged **trypsin inhibitor** (blocks <5% of potential trypsin); (4) low pH/condensation/ionic conditions limit activity; (5) prematurely active enzymes degraded (by other proteases or lysosomal mixing — crinophagy/autophagy). Failure → premature trypsin activation → **pancreatitis**.

### Equations / quantitative relations

The chapter has no numbered equations; the two-component model is stated qualitatively. Rendered relations:

- **Total pancreatic flow** is the sum of acinar (NaCl, flow-independent in composition) and ductal (NaHCO₃, secretin-driven) components:

  $$\dot V_{\text{total}} = \dot V_{\text{acinar}} + \dot V_{\text{duct}}, \qquad \dot V_{\text{acinar}} \approx 0.25\,\dot V_{\text{total}}, \quad \dot V_{\text{duct}} \approx 0.75\,\dot V_{\text{total}}$$

- **Flow-dependent anion composition** (Fig. 43-7): as flow $\dot V$ rises (driven by secretin),

  $$[\mathrm{HCO_3^-}] \uparrow \;\text{toward}\; \approx 140\ \mathrm{mEq/L}, \qquad [\mathrm{Cl^-}] \downarrow \;\;(\text{reciprocally}), \qquad [\mathrm{Na^+}],\,[\mathrm{K^+}] \approx \text{const} \approx \text{plasma}$$

  Anion sum is conserved (electroneutrality with constant cations): $[\mathrm{Cl^-}] + [\mathrm{HCO_3^-}] \approx \text{const}$.

- > "The [HCO₃⁻] in pancreatic juice increases with increases in the secretory flow rate … Concentrations of Na⁺ or K⁺, however, are not significantly altered by changes in flow." (p. 888)
- > "The most potent stimulator of CCK release from I cells is lipid in the duodenal lumen." (p. 889)
- > "To stimulate secretin secretion, duodenal pH must fall to <4.5." (p. 889)

#### Figure 43-7 — Flow dependence of the electrolyte composition of pancreatic fluid *(viewed)*
Plot: **[ion] (mEq/L) on the y-axis vs. secretory rate (mL/min) on the x-axis.** Increasing **secretin** raises both flow and alters composition. ## Section 5 — Salivary acinar cell (pp. 893–895)

### Subsection headings
- **Different salivary acinar cells secrete different proteins** (pp. 893–894)
- **Cholinergic and adrenergic neural pathways are the most important physiological activators of regulated secretion by salivary acinar cells** (pp. 894)
- **Both cAMP and Ca²⁺ mediate salivary acinar secretion** (pp. 894–895)

### Core claims
- Three main bilateral glands: **parotid** (serous, α-amylase-rich), **sublingual** (mucinous), **submandibular** (mixed serous + mucous). Acinar products: **α-amylase, mucins, proline-rich proteins**. (α-amylase begins starch digestion in the mouth; lingual lipase contributes to fat digestion.)
- **Autonomic control is unusual: both sympathetic and parasympathetic stimulate secretion** (unlike most organs where they oppose). Major agonists: **ACh** (parasympathetic, muscarinic **M₃**) and **norepinephrine** (sympathetic, α- and β-adrenergic). Substance P (NK₁), VIP, purinergic (P2X7), and others also present.
- **Two second-messenger arms (Table 43-4):** **cAMP** (β-adrenergic → PKA) is the most potent driver of **protein/amylase** secretion (β: protein > fluid). **Ca²⁺** (muscarinic M₃, α-adrenergic, substance P → PLC → IP₃/DAG → Ca²⁺ + PKC) drives mostly **fluid > protein** secretion. ATP co-released with NE activates P2X7 to admit Ca²⁺.
- **Fluid/electrolyte secretion** (~90% of stimulated salivary volume) uses the **same Cl⁻-driven mechanism as the pancreatic acinar cell** (Fig. 43-5): basolateral Na/K/Cl cotransporter + Na-K pump + K⁺ channels load Cl⁻; apical Cl⁻ and aquaporin channels secrete Cl⁻ and water; Na⁺ paracellular. The **primary salivary secretion is isotonic, plasma-like.** Some glands also run basolateral AE2 (Cl-HCO₃), NHE1/NHE4, and NBCe1-B.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Unlike the pancreas, in which hormones have an important role in stimulating secretion, the salivary glands are mostly controlled by the autonomic nervous system." (p. 894)
- > "Both cholinergic and adrenergic neurotransmitters can stimulate exocytosis by salivary acinar cells." (p. 894)
- > "Activation of cAMP via the β-adrenergic receptor is the most potent stimulator of amylase secretion in the rat parotid gland." (p. 894)
- > "Fluid and electrolyte secretion … accounts for ~90% of total salivary volume output under stimulatory conditions." (p. 894)
- > "The primary secretion of the salivary acinar cell is isotonic and results largely from the basolateral uptake of Cl⁻ through Na/K/Cl cotransporters." (p. 894)

## Section 6 — Salivary duct cell (pp. 894–898)

### Subsection headings
- **Salivary duct cells produce a hypotonic fluid that is poor in NaCl and rich in KHCO₃** (pp. 894–895)
- **Parasympathetic stimulation decreases Na⁺ absorption, whereas aldosterone increases Na⁺ absorption by duct cells** (pp. 895)
- **Salivary duct cells also secrete and take up proteins** (pp. 895–898)
- **(Myoepithelial cells and autonomic regulation of flow)** (pp. 898)

### Core claims
- The salivary duct modifies the isotonic primary secretion into **final hypotonic saliva**: ducts **reabsorb Na⁺ and Cl⁻** and, to a lesser extent, **secrete K⁺ and HCO₃⁻**. Because the duct epithelium is **poorly water-permeable**, net solute removal (reabsorption > secretion) makes the **lumen hypotonic** — the final saliva is poor in NaCl, relatively rich in KHCO₃.
- **Two-step Na⁺ reabsorption (Fig. 43-11):** apical **ENaC** entry → basolateral **Na-K pump** extrusion. Rising [Na⁺]ᵢ feedback-inhibits ENaC (via Nedd4). **Two-step Cl⁻ reabsorption:** apical entry via **Cl-HCO₃ exchanger** + **CFTR** (CFTR also recycles Cl⁻, supporting bidirectional movement) → basolateral exit via inwardly-rectifying **ClC-2** channels.
- **K⁺ and HCO₃⁻ secretion:** K⁺ enters basolaterally via the Na-K pump (apical exit mechanism not well defined); HCO₃⁻ secreted via apical Cl-HCO₃ exchanger + CFTR with basolateral Na/HCO₃ cotransport — as in pancreatic ducts.
- **Regulation:** **parasympathetic (ACh, M₃, Ca²⁺)** stimulation of the intact gland increases saliva mainly by **decreasing NaCl reabsorption** (the duct's role is limited, more "less absorption" than "more KHCO₃"). **β-adrenergic** raises cAMP and activates CFTR. **Aldosterone increases ductal Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ secretion** (↑ ENaC + Na-K pump) — the **only well-established hormonal regulation of salivary secretion**; Addison disease produces the opposite (Na⁺-rich, K⁺-poor saliva).
- **Duct-cell protein handling**: synthesize and secrete proteins (EGF, NGF, kallikrein; some α-amylase/RNAase — α-adrenergic/sympathetic-driven degranulation); transcytose **IgA** (polymeric IgA receptor → secretory component → apical release); and **endocytose/remove** luminal organic substances.
- **Myoepithelial cells** (around acini and intercalated ducts; sympathetically driven) contract to support acini, lower intercalated-duct resistance, facilitate proximal secretory flow, and minimize extravasation of secretory proteins during high flow.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In general, salivary duct cells absorb Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and, to a lesser extent, secrete K⁺ and HCO₃⁻. Because the epithelium is not very water permeable, the lumen thus becomes hypotonic." (p. 894)
- > "Reabsorption of Na⁺ by salivary duct cells is a two-step transcellular process. First, Na⁺ enters the cell from the lumen through apical epithelial Na⁺ channels (ENaCs) … Second, the basolateral Na-K pump extrudes this Na⁺." (p. 894)
- > "The role played by duct cells in the increased saliva production … may reflect decreased NaCl absorption more than increased KHCO₃ secretion." (p. 895)
- > "The mineralocorticoid effect represents the only well-established example of regulation of salivary secretion by a hormone." (p. 898)

### Figures

#### Figure 43-11 — Salivary duct transporters *(viewed)*
A single salivary (striated) duct cell, **lumen at left**, **interstitial space at right** — the cell **reabsorbs net solute**, opposite in net direction to the secreting cells.
- **Apical (luminal) membrane:** **ENaC** admits luminal **Na⁺**; an **SLC26A6 Cl-HCO₃ exchanger** and **CFTR** mediate **Cl⁻ reabsorption** (CFTR also recycling Cl⁻ and supporting HCO₃⁻ movement). K⁺ exits apically (mechanism uncertain).
- **Basolateral membrane:** the **Na-K pump** extrudes reabsorbed **Na⁺** (and loads K⁺ for secretion); **NBCe1-B (Na/HCO₃ cotransporter)** and **ClC-2 Cl⁻ channels** handle basolateral HCO₃⁻ and Cl⁻ exit.
- **Regulatory annotations:** **ACh (muscarinic M₃)** raises [Ca²⁺]ᵢ and decreases Na⁺ absorption (parasympathetic). Because water cannot follow the reabsorbed solute (low water permeability), the lumen is left **hypotonic** — the defining feature of final saliva.

> Vision note: Contrast directly with Fig. 43-6/43-5. Pancreatic acinar/duct cells **secrete** NaCl/NaHCO₃ (isotonic); salivary duct cells **reabsorb** Na⁺/Cl⁻ faster than they secrete K⁺/HCO₃⁻ in a water-tight epithelium → **hypotonic** saliva. This is the chapter's signature comparative point.

## Glossary

- **Acinus / acinar cell** — cluster of 15–100 protein-secreting cells; source of the protein-rich primary secretion.
- **Intercalated / intralobular / interlobular / main duct** — the merging ductal hierarchy.
- **Zymogen** — inactive enzyme precursor (trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidases).
- **Enterokinase** — duodenal enzyme that converts trypsinogen → trypsin, triggering the protease cascade.
- **Trypsin inhibitor (SPINK1)** — granule-co-packaged block on aberrant trypsin; mutations predispose to pancreatitis.
- **CFTR** — cAMP-activated apical Cl⁻ channel; supplies luminal Cl⁻ for Cl-HCO₃ exchange; defective in cystic fibrosis.
- **SLC26A6 (Cl-HCO₃ exchanger)** — apical HCO₃⁻ secretion (Cl⁻/2HCO₃⁻), coupled to CFTR.
- **NBCe1-B (SLC4A4)** — basolateral electrogenic Na/HCO₃ cotransporter (~1 Na⁺ : 2 HCO₃⁻).
- **Cl⁻ recycling** — CFTR returns Cl⁻ to the lumen to sustain Cl-HCO₃ exchange.
- **Two-component model** — acinar NaCl fluid (~25%, flow-independent composition) + ductal NaHCO₃ fluid (~75%, flow-dependent).
- **Flow dependence** — ↑flow (secretin) → ↑HCO₃⁻, ↓Cl⁻; Na⁺/K⁺ constant.
- **Secretin** — duct HCO₃⁻/fluid; released by duodenal S cells when pH < 4.5.
- **CCK** — acinar enzyme secretion; released by duodenal I cells (chiefly by luminal lipid).
- **Cephalic / gastric / intestinal phases** — sequential meal stimulation (vagal; gastrin/distention; CCK + secretin + reflexes).
- **Acute pancreatitis** — premature intracellular trypsin activation / autodigestion; supramaximal-CCK Ca²⁺-spike model.
- **Serous vs. mucous acini** — parotid (α-amylase) vs. sublingual (mucin); submandibular mixed.
- **Primary saliva** — isotonic, plasma-like acinar fluid.
- **Final saliva** — hypotonic; ductal Na⁺/Cl⁻ reabsorption > K⁺/HCO₃⁻ secretion in a water-impermeable duct.
- **Striated duct cell** — salivary duct cell with basolateral infoldings/mitochondria for active transport.
- **Myoepithelial cell** — contractile stellate cell aiding secretory flow.
