## Section 1 — Modes of intercellular communication (pp. 47–49)

### Subsection headings
- **Cells can communicate with one another via chemical signals** (p. 47)
- **Soluble chemical signals interact with target cells in one of four ways: endocrine, paracrine, autocrine, and juxtacrine** (pp. 47–48)

### Core claims
- Communication modes are distinguished by the **distance** between the signaling and target cell and the **route** the signal travels:
  - **Endocrine** — released into the bloodstream; reaches distant targets (e.g., insulin from pancreatic β cells acting on liver/muscle/adipose).
  - **Paracrine** — diffuses through the local interstitium to nearby cells (e.g., ACh at the neuromuscular junction).
  - **Autocrine** — acts back on the cell that released it (e.g., growth factors in tumor self-stimulation).
  - **Juxtacrine** — direct contact-dependent (membrane-bound ligand on one cell binds receptor on apposed cell).
- **Gap junctions** allow direct cytoplasmic exchange of small molecules and ions between cells without a receptor.
- The classification matters because the same molecule (e.g., norepinephrine, ACh) can serve in different modes depending on where it is released.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Cells can communicate with one another via chemical signals." (p. 47)
- > "Soluble chemical signals interact with target cells in one of four ways: endocrine, paracrine, autocrine, and juxtacrine." (p. 47)
- > "An endocrine signal is released into the bloodstream and acts on distant target cells." (p. 47)
- > "Paracrine signals act on nearby cells … the released signal molecule has only a brief life because it is rapidly taken up by neighboring cells or degraded." (p. 48)
- > "Autocrine signaling occurs when the released chemical signal acts on the cell that originated it." (p. 48)
- > "the release of ACh at the neuromuscular junction is one of paracrine signaling because the nerve terminal releases a chemical (i.e., ACh) that acts on a neighboring cell (i.e., the muscle)." (Fig. 3-2 caption, p. 48)

## Section 2 — The five major receptor classes (pp. 49–50)

### Subsection headings
- **Signaling events initiated by membrane-associated receptors generally involve multiple steps** (p. 48)
- **Receptors fall into five major classes** (p. 49)

### Core claims
The classes are distinguished by **how the receptor converts ligand binding into downstream effect**:

1. **Ligand-gated ion channels** — receptor itself is a channel; ligand binding opens or closes it on a millisecond timescale.
2. **G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)** — seven-transmembrane (7TM) receptors that activate heterotrimeric G proteins on their cytoplasmic face. Slower than channels (seconds) but enormously branchpoint-rich. Largest single class in the genome.
3. **Catalytic receptors** — single-pass transmembrane proteins with enzymatic activity (or directly coupled to one) on their cytoplasmic tail. Subfamilies: receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), receptor serine/threonine kinases, receptor tyrosine phosphatases, receptor guanylyl cyclases, JAK-associated cytokine receptors.
4. **Nuclear receptors** — soluble lipid-permeable ligands (steroids, thyroid hormone, vitamin D, retinoids) cross the membrane and bind cytosolic/nuclear receptors that act as ligand-gated transcription factors.
5. **Receptors that undergo cleavage** — Notch, SREBP/SCAP; signal is transmitted by **regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP)** releasing a cytoplasmic fragment that traffics to the nucleus.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Receptors fall into five major classes." (p. 49)
- > "Ligand-gated ion channels. Integral membrane proteins, these hybrid receptors/channels are involved in signaling between electrically excitable cells. The binding of a neurotransmitter such as ACh to its receptor — which in fact is merely part of the channel — results in transient opening of the channel and thus alters the ion permeability of the cell." (p. 49)
- > "G protein-coupled receptors. These integral plasma-membrane proteins work indirectly — through multiple intermediates — to activate or inactivate downstream effectors, such as membrane-associated enzymes or channels." (p. 49)
- > "Catalytic receptors. When activated by a ligand, these integral plasma-membrane receptors are either enzymes themselves or part of an enzymatic complex. In many cases, these receptors are either kinases that add phosphate groups to their substrates or phosphatases that remove substrate phosphate groups." (p. 49)
- > "Nuclear receptors. These proteins, located in the cytosol or nucleus, are ligand-activated transcription factors. These receptors link extracellular signals to gene transcription." (p. 49)
- > "Receptors that undergo cleavage. In response to ligand binding, some transmembrane proteins undergo regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP)." (p. 49)

## Section 3 — Receptors that are ion channels (pp. 50–51)

### Core claims
- The receptor and the ion-permeable pore are **the same protein**.
- The nicotinic AChR is the prototype: a pentamer (2α + β + γ + δ in muscle, or 2α + 3β in many neuronal subtypes) forming a central cation-permeable pore.
- Binding two ACh molecules at the α subunits triggers a conformational change that opens the channel; ACh dissociation + receptor desensitization close it.
- Other ligand-gated channels include **GABA_A** (Cl⁻ in; inhibitory; benzodiazepine and barbiturate target), **glycine** (Cl⁻ in; inhibitory; strychnine target), **NMDA, AMPA, kainate** (glutamate; cation; excitatory; NMDA additionally voltage-gated by Mg²⁺ block), **5-HT₃** (cation), **P2X** (ATP-gated cation).
- Functional consequences treated in Chs 7–8.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The receptor and the channel are part of the same molecule." (p. 50)
- > "Binding of a ligand to its receptor results in transient opening of the channel and thus alters the ion permeability of the cell." (p. 49)

## Section 4 — Receptors coupled to G proteins (pp. 51–60)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **GPCRs comprise the largest family of receptors on the cell surface** (p. 51)
- **G proteins are heterotrimers that exist in many combinations of different α, β, and γ subunits** (pp. 52)
- **Small GTP-binding proteins are involved in a vast number of cellular processes** (p. 56)
- **G-protein second messengers: cyclic nucleotides** (p. 56)
- **cAMP usually exerts its effect by increasing the activity of protein kinase A** (pp. 57–58)
- **G-protein second messengers: products of phosphoinositide breakdown** (pp. 58–60)
- **G-protein second messengers: products of arachidonic acid** (pp. 60–63)

### Core claims — GPCRs

- **Architecture (Fig. 3-3):** All GPCRs share a single polypeptide chain crossing the membrane **seven** times as α helices, with the N-terminus extracellular, the C-terminus intracellular, and a ligand-binding pocket usually within the helical bundle (small-molecule ligands) or in the extracellular loops/N-terminus (peptide ligands).
- **Cycle:** ligand binding → conformational change → GDP/GTP exchange on Gα → α–GTP dissociates from βγ → both halves engage downstream effectors → intrinsic GTPase on Gα hydrolyzes GTP → α–GDP rebinds βγ → cycle resets.
- **GTPase rate** = intrinsic timer; many cells use **RGS proteins** (regulators of G-protein signaling) to accelerate it and shorten signal duration.

### Core claims — heterotrimeric G-protein families (Table 3-2)

| Family | Cα subtype | Key effector | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gs** | αs | adenylyl cyclase ↑ | ↑ cAMP → PKA |
| **Gi/o** | αi, αo | adenylyl cyclase ↓; GIRK channel ↑; voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel ↓ | ↓ cAMP; ↑ K⁺ efflux; ↓ Ca²⁺ influx |
| **Gq/11** | αq, α11 | PLCβ ↑ | ↑ IP₃ + DAG → Ca²⁺ + PKC |
| **G12/13** | α12, α13 | RhoGEF → Rho | cytoskeletal regulation |
| **Gt (transducin)** | αt | cGMP phosphodiesterase ↑ | photoreceptor: ↓ cGMP |
| **Golf** | αolf | adenylyl cyclase ↑ | olfactory cilia: ↑ cAMP |

The **βγ dimer** is itself an effector — opens GIRK K⁺ channels, inhibits voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, recruits PI3Kγ and GRK kinases.

### Core claims — small GTPases (Ras superfamily)

- Monomeric (~20–30 kDa) GTPases distinct from heterotrimeric G proteins; switch between GTP-bound "on" and GDP-bound "off."
- **GEFs** (guanine nucleotide exchange factors) load GTP; **GAPs** (GTPase-activating proteins) accelerate hydrolysis.
- Subfamilies and roles:
  - **Ras** — mitogenic signaling from RTKs (Ras → Raf → MEK → ERK).
  - **Rho/Rac/Cdc42** — cytoskeletal dynamics, cell migration.
  - **Rab** — vesicle docking / compartment identity (introduced Ch 2).
  - **Ran** — nucleocytoplasmic transport directionality.
  - **Arf** — coat-protein recruitment in vesicle budding.

### Core claims — cAMP / PKA cascade

- **Activation:** Gαs → adenylyl cyclase → ATP → cAMP.
- **PKA** (cAMP-dependent protein kinase) holoenzyme is a tetramer (2 regulatory + 2 catalytic). cAMP binds the regulatory subunits, releasing the catalytic subunits, which phosphorylate Ser/Thr residues on target proteins (canonical site `R-R-X-S/T`).
- Targets: CREB transcription factor (long-term gene-expression effects), L-type Ca²⁺ channels, ryanodine receptors, phospholamban, lipolytic enzymes, glycogen phosphorylase kinase, ion channels.
- **Termination:** cAMP hydrolyzed to 5′-AMP by **phosphodiesterases (PDEs)** — methylxanthines (caffeine, theophylline) are nonselective PDE inhibitors.
- Reversal of phosphorylation by **protein phosphatases (PP1, PP2A, PP2B/calcineurin)**.

### Core claims — cGMP / PKG cascade

- Two activation routes:
  - **Particulate (membrane) guanylyl cyclase** activated by ANP/BNP (cardiac peptide hormones; vasodilation, natriuresis).
  - **Soluble guanylyl cyclase** activated by **NO** — NO is a gaseous paracrine messenger generated by NO synthase (NOS); causes vasodilation, platelet inhibition.
- cGMP activates PKG and modulates cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels (photoreceptors, olfactory cilia).

### Core claims — PLCβ → IP₃ / DAG → Ca²⁺ + PKC

- Gαq activates **phospholipase Cβ (PLCβ)**, which hydrolyzes membrane PIP₂ into **IP₃** (soluble, cytoplasmic) and **DAG** (membrane-tethered).
- **IP₃** diffuses to the ER, binds IP₃ receptor (a Ca²⁺-permeable channel) and releases Ca²⁺ from the ER store into the cytosol.
- Released Ca²⁺ binds **calmodulin (CaM)**, which activates **CaMKII/IV** (Ser/Thr kinases) and other CaM-effector enzymes (myosin light chain kinase, calcineurin).
- **DAG** activates conventional and novel isoforms of **PKC** (the conventional isoforms additionally require Ca²⁺).
- Termination: IP₃ is dephosphorylated; cytosolic Ca²⁺ pumped back into ER (SERCA) or out of cell (PMCA, NCX); DAG is cleaved or phosphorylated.

### Core claims — arachidonic acid pathways

- **PLA₂** cleaves arachidonic acid (AA) from membrane phospholipids (Fig. 3-10).
- AA is metabolized by three enzyme branches:
  - **Cyclooxygenase (COX-1, COX-2)** → prostaglandins, prostacyclin, thromboxanes. Aspirin and NSAIDs act here.
  - **5-Lipoxygenase** → leukotrienes (LTB₄, LTC₄, LTD₄, LTE₄). Inflammation, bronchoconstriction (asthma).
  - **Epoxygenase (CYP450)** → epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs) and HETEs. Vascular tone, ion channel regulation.

## Section 5 — Catalytic receptors (pp. 60–67)

### Subsection headings
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases produce phosphotyrosines that recruit SH2/PTB-domain proteins** (pp. 60–63)
- **The Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK MAP-kinase cascade transmits RTK signals to the nucleus** (pp. 62–63)
- **The PI3K-Akt pathway is the principal anti-apoptotic and growth-promoting arm downstream of RTKs** (pp. 63–64)
- **Receptor tyrosine phosphatases reverse RTK signals** (p. 64)
- **Receptor serine/threonine kinases (TGF-β family) signal via SMADs** (pp. 64–65)
- **Receptor guanylyl cyclases generate cGMP from membrane ANP/BNP receptors** (p. 65)
- **Cytokine receptors signal via associated JAK kinases that phosphorylate STAT transcription factors** (pp. 65–67)

### Core claims
- **RTK activation**: ligand binding → receptor **dimerization** → trans-autophosphorylation on multiple Tyr residues → docking sites for SH2 / PTB domain-containing adaptors and effectors (Grb2, Shc, PLCγ, PI3K p85, STATs).
- **Ras pathway** (mitogenic): Grb2-SOS recruits SOS → Ras-GEF → Ras-GTP → Raf → MEK → ERK1/2 → nuclear translocation → ELK1/CREB/Fos transcription → cell-cycle entry.
- **PI3K pathway** (survival/metabolism): PI3K phosphorylates PIP₂ → PIP₃ → recruits PDK1 and Akt via PH domains → Akt phosphorylated and activated → phosphorylates BAD, FOXO, GSK3, TSC2 (mTOR pathway) → anti-apoptosis, glucose uptake, protein synthesis.
- **PLCγ pathway**: hydrolyzes PIP₂ to IP₃ + DAG (parallel route to Gq pathway, but triggered downstream of RTKs).
- **TGF-β family**: ligand binds **type II R-Ser/Thr kinase** → recruits and phosphorylates **type I R-Ser/Thr kinase** → phosphorylates **R-SMAD** (SMAD2/3 for TGF-β; SMAD1/5/8 for BMP) → co-SMAD (SMAD4) binding → nuclear translocation → target-gene transcription. **I-SMADs** (SMAD6/7) are negative regulators.
- **Receptor guanylyl cyclases**: ANP/BNP bind a single-TM receptor whose cytoplasmic domain is a guanylyl cyclase → cGMP → vasodilation and natriuresis.
- **JAK/STAT**: cytokine binds receptor → receptor dimerization → associated JAK kinases trans-phosphorylate → phosphorylate receptor Tyr residues → STAT SH2 docking → STAT phosphorylation → STAT dimerization → nuclear translocation → gene expression. EPO, GH, prolactin, many ILs use this route.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Receptor tyrosine kinases are themselves enzymes. The binding of an extracellular ligand causes the receptors to dimerize. They then phosphorylate each other on multiple tyrosine residues, generating phosphotyrosine docking sites for downstream signaling molecules." (p. 60)
- > "The Ras signaling pathway is one of the most important mitogenic cascades and is implicated in a wide variety of human cancers." (p. 62)
- > "Receptor tyrosine kinases activate phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase, which generates the membrane lipid PIP₃." (p. 63)
- > "Cytokines control gene transcription through the JAK-STAT pathway." (p. 65)
- > "TGF-β family ligands signal through receptor serine/threonine kinases and SMAD transcription factors." (p. 64)

## Section 6 — Nuclear receptors (pp. 67–70)

### Subsection headings
- **Steroid and thyroid hormones cross the plasma membrane and bind intracellular receptors** (pp. 67–68)
- **Nuclear receptors are ligand-activated transcription factors with conserved DNA-binding (zinc-finger) and ligand-binding domains** (pp. 68–70)

### Core claims
- Lipid-soluble ligands (cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, estradiol, T3, vitamin D₃, retinoic acid, fatty-acid PPAR ligands) diffuse through the plasma membrane.
- Classic action: ligand binds cytoplasmic receptor (e.g., GR) → receptor releases HSP90 chaperone → dimerizes → translocates to nucleus → binds **hormone response elements (HREs)** in target-gene promoters → recruits coactivators/corepressors → modulates transcription. Some receptors (TR, VDR, RAR) are already nuclear and DNA-bound, repressing transcription in the unliganded state and activating it upon ligand binding.
- Architecture: N-terminal AF-1 (transactivation), DNA-binding domain (DBD; two zinc fingers), hinge, ligand-binding domain (LBD; AF-2).
- Distinct pharmacological logic from GPCR/RTK: slow (hours), persistent (hours–days), and the ligand class is lipid-soluble.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Nuclear receptors. These proteins, located in the cytosol or nucleus, are ligand-activated transcription factors." (p. 49)
- > "[The receptor] is composed of two 'zinc finger' structures … receptor-ligand complexes bind to a particular response element [HRE] in target-gene promoters." (p. 67–68)
- > "Lipid-soluble hormones such as steroids and thyroid hormone can cross the plasma membrane and bind to intracellular receptors that act as transcription factors." (p. 67)

## Section 7 — Receptors that undergo cleavage (pp. 70–72)

### Core claims
- A subset of receptors signal by **regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP)**: ligand binding triggers two proteolytic cuts that release a cytoplasmic fragment, which travels to the nucleus to regulate gene expression.
- **Notch**: ligand (Delta/Jagged on neighboring cell) → ADAM-family metalloproteinase cleaves Notch in the extracellular juxtamembrane region → γ-secretase cleaves within the transmembrane domain → Notch intracellular domain (NICD) translocates to nucleus → binds CSL transcription factor → activates HES/HEY target genes. Critical for lateral inhibition in development, T-cell fate, intestinal stem cells.
- **SREBP/SCAP**: ER membrane-bound transcription factor SREBP is escorted by SCAP. When ER cholesterol is low, the SCAP-SREBP complex moves to the Golgi, where two proteases (S1P and S2P) release the soluble transcription-factor domain, which enters the nucleus and activates lipogenic genes (LDLR, HMG-CoA reductase). The statin drug class works because it raises LDLR expression via this loop.
- **APP (amyloid precursor protein)** is cleaved by β- and γ-secretase to generate Aβ peptide (Alzheimer pathology) — same γ-secretase as Notch; an unsolved selectivity problem in Alzheimer drug design.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In response to ligand binding, some transmembrane proteins undergo regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP)." (p. 49)
- > "Notch is the prototype of a receptor that signals via regulated intramembrane proteolysis." (p. 70)

## Equations and quantitative anchors

- **Ligand-receptor mass-action binding** (used throughout the chapter and forward into Chs 4 and 47):

  $$R + L \rightleftharpoons RL \qquad K_d = \frac{[R][L]}{[RL]}$$

  $$[RL] = R_T \cdot \frac{[L]}{K_d + [L]} \quad \text{(saturation)}$$

- **Hill equation** for cooperative binding (e.g., ligand-gated channels requiring two ACh):

  $$\frac{[RL]}{R_T} = \frac{[L]^n}{K_d^n + [L]^n}$$

- **First-order signal decay** for second-messenger turnover (e.g., cAMP):

  $$\frac{d[\text{cAMP}]}{dt} = V_{AC} - k_{PDE}[\text{cAMP}]$$

  Steady-state $[\text{cAMP}]_{ss} = V_{AC}/k_{PDE}$ — explains why PDE inhibition raises cAMP set-point.

- **G-protein cycle as a GTPase timer**: signal duration $\tau \sim 1/k_{GTPase}$; RGS proteins reduce $\tau$.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Endocrine / paracrine / autocrine / juxtacrine** — distance-based classes of soluble signaling.
- **GPCR** — seven-TM receptor; activates heterotrimeric G protein.
- **Heterotrimeric G protein** — αβγ; α binds GDP/GTP and gates downstream signaling.
- **Gs / Gi / Gq / G12 / Gt / Golf** — Gα subfamilies with different effector specificity.
- **RGS** — regulator of G-protein signaling; GAP for Gα.
- **Adenylyl cyclase** — converts ATP → cAMP.
- **cAMP** — diffusible second messenger; activates PKA.
- **PKA** — cAMP-dependent kinase; R₂C₂ holoenzyme.
- **PDE (phosphodiesterase)** — hydrolyzes cAMP/cGMP.
- **PLCβ / PLCγ** — phospholipase C isoforms (Gq vs RTK activated).
- **PIP₂ → IP₃ + DAG** — phosphoinositide hydrolysis.
- **IP₃R / RyR** — ER Ca²⁺-release channels.
- **Calmodulin / CaMK** — Ca²⁺ sensor / Ca²⁺-CaM-activated kinase.
- **PKC** — DAG + Ca²⁺ (conventional) activated kinase.
- **SERCA / PMCA / NCX / MCU** — Ca²⁺ removal pumps/exchangers.
- **RTK** — receptor tyrosine kinase.
- **SH2 / PTB domain** — phosphotyrosine recognition modules.
- **Ras / Raf / MEK / ERK** — MAPK mitogenic cascade.
- **PI3K / PIP₃ / Akt / mTOR** — survival and metabolism cascade.
- **Cytokine receptor / JAK / STAT** — non-RTK tyrosine-kinase signaling.
- **TGF-β R / SMAD** — receptor Ser/Thr kinase pathway.
- **Receptor guanylyl cyclase / ANP / cGMP / PKG** — natriuretic and NO-related second-messenger cascade.
- **Nuclear receptor / HRE / zinc finger** — ligand-activated transcription factors.
- **Notch / SREBP / RIP / γ-secretase** — cleavage-signaling receptors.
- **Cholera / pertussis toxin** — bacterial ADP-ribosyltransferases targeting Gαs and Gαi/o.
- **COX / LOX / CYP epoxygenase** — three eicosanoid branches downstream of PLA₂.
- **NO / NOS** — gaseous paracrine signaling, soluble guanylyl cyclase activation.
