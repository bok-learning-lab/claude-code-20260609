# Chapter 40 — Integration of Salt and Water Balance

> Section VI · The Urinary System · pp. 836–851 · Authors: Gerhard Giebisch, Erich E. Windhager, Peter S. Aronson

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Two control systems: volume vs. osmolality** (pp. 836–837) — the central dichotomy. Na⁺ content sets ECF volume; total-body water sets osmolality. Different sensors, transducers, effectors (Table 40-1).
2. **Control of ECF volume** (pp. 837–843) — effective circulating volume; the four parallel effector pathways for Na⁺ excretion (RAAS, sympathetic nerves, AVP, ANP); renin release control; ANG II and aldosterone actions.
3. **Pressure natriuresis / hemodynamic control** (p. 843) — Na⁺ excretion driven directly by arterial pressure, independent of effective circulating volume.
4. **Control of water content (osmolality)** (pp. 844–851) — hypothalamic osmoreceptors → AVP + thirst; AVP synthesis/release; nonosmotic stimuli; the osmolality–volume interaction; priority of volume defense over osmolality.

## The central dichotomy — two interrelated control systems (pp. 836–837)

### Core claims
- **Two separate but coupled control systems** regulate the ECF: one for **volume/blood pressure** (sensed signal = effective circulating volume; effector = renal **Na⁺** excretion) and one for **osmolality** (sensed signal = plasma osmolality; effector = renal **free-water** excretion + thirst). Both share one feature: some effectors are in the kidney.
- **Why Na⁺ content sets ECF volume**: Na⁺ with its anions (Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻) is the dominant extracellular osmotic constituent; "when Na salts move, water must follow." Because osmolality is held near ~290 mOsm, whole-body Na⁺ content is the major determinant of ECF volume.
- **Why total-body water sets osmolality**: total-body osmoles are dominated by the (larger, tightly regulated) intracellular compartment and change little except in growth or disease (e.g., diabetes mellitus). Only by controlling water independently of Na⁺ can the body control osmolality.
- **Sensitivity illustration**: adding 145 mmol of "dry" NaCl to the ECF obligates ~1 L of water; small changes in Na⁺ excretion produce large ECF-volume changes — hence precise control is required.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "These two homeostatic mechanisms—for ECF volume and osmolality—use different sensors, different hormonal transducers, and different effectors (Table 40-1)." (p. 836)
- > "Na⁺, with its associated anions, Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻, is the main osmotic constituent of the ECF volume; when Na salts move, water must follow." (p. 836)
- > "Only by controlling water independent of Na⁺ control can the body control osmolality." (p. 837)
- > "Total-body osmolality is defined as the ratio of total-body osmoles to total-body water." (p. 837)

### Equations
- **Total-body osmolality** (definitional):

  $$\text{Osmolality} = \frac{\text{total-body osmoles}}{\text{total-body water}}$$

- **Na⁺ steady-state balance** (Eq. 40-1):

  $$\text{Oral Na}^+\text{ intake} = \text{Renal Na}^+\text{ output} + \text{Extrarenal Na}^+\text{ output}$$

  Extrarenal Na⁺ output is normally negligible; it becomes large with vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, or burns.

## Section 1 — Control of extracellular fluid volume (pp. 837–843)

### Subsection headings
- **In the steady state, Na⁺ intake equals Na⁺ output** (pp. 837–838)
- **The kidneys increase Na⁺ excretion in response to an increase in ECF volume, not to an increase in extracellular Na⁺ concentration** (pp. 838–839)
- **It is not the ECF volume as a whole, but the effective circulating volume, that regulates Na⁺ excretion** (pp. 838–839)
- **Decreases in effective circulating volume trigger four parallel effector pathways to decrease renal Na⁺ excretion** (pp. 839–840)
- **Increased activity of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis is the first of four parallel pathways** (pp. 841–842)
- **Increased sympathetic nerve activity, increased AVP, and decreased ANP are the other three parallel pathways** (pp. 842–843)

### Core claims
- **Volume, not concentration, is the controlled signal**: renal Na⁺ excretion depends on the *amount* of Na⁺ in the body, not on plasma [Na⁺]. Because [Na⁺] is clamped by the osmoregulatory system, ECF volume is the effective signal.
- **Step-change experiment (Fig. 40-1A)**: abruptly raising intake from 10 → 150 mmol/day produces a transient **positive Na⁺ balance** (output lags intake ~5 days), cumulative retention ~140 mmol, accompanied by ~1 kg weight gain (1 L isotonic saline) — all extracellular. Reducing intake back produces a transient **negative Na⁺ balance**. [Na⁺] does not change during either transient — confirming volume (not [Na⁺]) is the signal.
- **The control system is exquisitely sensitive**: in a 70-kg subject with ECF ~17 L, expanding ECF by 1 L (~6%) drives a ~15-fold rise in steady-state Na⁺ excretion (10 → 150 mmol/day).
- **Effective circulating volume (ECV)** — *not* total ECF volume — is the regulated parameter. ECV is a functional blood volume reflecting tissue perfusion / fullness of blood-filled compartments. Normally it tracks total ECF, but the two **dissociate** in congestive heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, and cirrhosis: total ECF is grossly expanded (edema, ascites) while ECV is low, driving inappropriate Na⁺ retention.
- **ECV sensors (Table 40-2)** are baroreceptors in **high-pressure** sites (carotid sinus, aortic arch, JGA / renal afferent arteriole) and **low-pressure** sites (cardiac atria, pulmonary vasculature), plus less-important sensors in the CNS and liver. **Renal perfusion pressure is the most important for long-term regulation** of Na⁺ excretion and blood pressure.
- **Four parallel efferent effector pathways** correct a low ECV by reducing Na⁺ excretion (Fig. 40-2):
  1. **RAAS** (renin → ANG II → aldosterone) — kidney directly senses low ECV and activates this hormonal axis; increased renal perfusion pressure can also raise Na⁺ excretion independently.
  2. **Sympathetic nerves** — baroreceptor → brainstem → ↑ renal sympathetic outflow; ↓ renal blood flow and direct ↑ tubular Na⁺ reabsorption.
  3. **AVP** — posterior pituitary ↑ AVP → water conservation (activates only after large ECV declines, e.g., hemorrhage).
  4. **ANP** — the only effector that corrects low ECV by *decreasing* its activity: low ECV → ↓ atrial stretch → ↓ ANP → ↓ Na⁺ excretion.
- **Two-way redundancy**: efferent pathways converge on single effectors (e.g., sympathetic + physical factors both act on proximal tubule), and one pathway acts at multiple sites (ANG II stimulates apical Na-H exchange *and* lowers renal plasma flow).
- An **increase** in ECV promotes Na⁺ excretion (lowering ECF volume); a **decrease** inhibits it (raising ECF volume).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In contrast to many other renal mechanisms of electrolyte excretion, the renal excretion of Na⁺ depends on the amount of Na⁺ in the body and not on the Na⁺ concentration in ECF." (p. 838)
- > "It is actually the volume of ECF that acts as the signal for Na⁺ homeostasis." (p. 838)
- > "The critical parameter that the body recognizes is the effective circulating volume … not something that we can identify anatomically." (p. 838)
- > "In all three cases, total ECF volume is grossly expanded … In contrast, the effective circulating volume is low, resulting in Na⁺ retention." (pp. 838–839)
- > "Of the pressures at these sites, it is renal perfusion pressure that is most important for long-term regulation of Na⁺ excretion." (p. 839)
- > "All four parallel effector pathways correct the primary change in effective circulating blood volume." (p. 840)

#### Figure 40-3 — Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis *(viewed)*

### Equations / data — renin release control
- **Three signals stimulate renin release from JGA granular cells** when ECV falls:
  1. **↓ systemic blood pressure** → ↑ sympathetic outflow to JGA → ↑ renin (blocked by renal denervation or β-blockers, e.g., propranolol).
  2. **↓ NaCl at the macula densa** — low ECV raises filtration fraction → ↑ proximal reabsorption → ↓ flow & ↓ luminal [NaCl] at the macula densa → ↑ renin.
  3. **↓ renal perfusion pressure (renal baroreceptor)** — reduced afferent-arteriole stretch lowers granular-cell **[Ca²⁺]ᵢ**, which (atypically) *increases* renin release. Increased stretch (high volume) inhibits renin.
- **Note the inverted Ca²⁺ signaling**: unlike most secretory cells, ↓[Ca²⁺]ᵢ stimulates renin (a parallel to PTH secretion by parathyroid chief cells). cAMP/PKA also promotes renin release.
- **Modulators**: PGE₂, PGI₂, and endothelin *activate* renin; ANG II (short feedback loop), AVP, thromboxane A₂, high plasma K⁺, and NO *blunt* it.
- **ANG II actions**: (1) stimulates aldosterone release; (2) vasoconstriction altering renal hemodynamics (Fig. 40-4); (3) enhanced tubuloglomerular feedback; (4) enhanced apical Na-H exchange (proximal tubule, TAL, initial collecting tubule); (5) renal-tubule hypertrophy over time; (6) stimulated thirst + AVP release (the intersection of the volume and osmolality systems).

### Citation-anchor quotes (RAAS)
- > "The principal factor controlling plasma ANG II levels is renin release from JGA granular cells." (p. 841)
- > "Renin … is a protease that cleaves a peptide bond near the C terminus of angiotensinogen, releasing the decapeptide angiotensin I (ANG I)." (p. 841)
- > "ACE is present on the luminal surface of vascular endothelia throughout the body and is abundantly present in the endothelium-rich lungs." (p. 841)
- > "This decreased stretch lowers [Ca²⁺]ᵢ, which increases renin release." (p. 841)
- > "This ANG II effect represents an intersection between the systems for regulating effective circulating volume and osmolality." (p. 842)

#### Figure 40-4 — Hemodynamic actions of ANG II on Na⁺ reabsorption *(listed)*

## Section 2 — Pressure natriuresis: hemodynamic control independent of ECV (p. 843)

### Subsection heading
- **High arterial pressure raises Na⁺ excretion by hemodynamic mechanisms, independent of changes in effective circulating volume** (p. 843)

### Core claims
- The kidney modulates Na⁺ excretion in response to **purely hemodynamic** changes, beyond the four ECV pathways.
- **Large acute fall in arterial pressure**: if glomerulotubular (GT) balance were perfect, ↓GFR would lower Na⁺ excretion *linearly* (Fig. 40-5, blue line). Instead, acutely lowering GFR (aortic clamping) causes a **steep, nonlinear** fall in Na⁺ excretion (red curve) — the classical distal tubule keeps reabsorbing Na⁺ avidly despite reduced delivery, so very little Na⁺ escapes.
- **Large rise in arterial pressure (pressure natriuresis / "pressure diuresis")**: hypertension (e.g., primary hyperaldosteronism, Liddle disease) drives natriuresis by ≥4 mechanisms beyond simply ↑GFR (↑filtered load):
  1. ↑ ECV **inhibits RAAS** → ↓ Na⁺ reabsorption.
  2. ↑ vasa recta flow **washes out medullary solutes** → ↓ medullary hypertonicity → ↓ passive Na⁺ reabsorption in thin ascending limb.
  3. **↓ number of apical Na-H exchangers** in the proximal tubule (mechanism unknown; rapidly reversible on normalizing BP).
  4. ↑ peritubular-capillary pressure (**physical factors**) → ↓ proximal reabsorption.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Acutely lowering GFR by partial clamping of the aorta causes a steep, nonlinear decrease in urinary Na⁺ excretion." (p. 843)
- > "One reason for this pressure diuresis is that hypertension increases GFR, increasing the filtered load of Na⁺." (p. 843)
- > "An increase in arterial pressure leads, by an unknown mechanism, to prompt reduction in the number of apical Na-H exchangers in the proximal tubule." (p. 843)

#### Figure 40-5 — Effect of changes in GFR on urinary Na⁺ excretion *(listed)*
Percent of Na⁺ excretion (y) vs. percent of control GFR (x). The **blue line** = ideal GT balance (linear, constant fractional excretion). The **red curve** (dog data, Thompson & Pitts) is sigmoidal: as GFR falls, Na⁺ excretion falls *even faster*; as GFR rises, Na⁺ excretion rises *very rapidly* ("pressure diuresis"). GFR was lowered by an aortic balloon and raised by carotid compression.

## Section 3 — Control of water content / osmolality (pp. 844–851)

### Subsection headings
- **Increased plasma osmolality stimulates hypothalamic osmoreceptors that trigger the release of AVP, inhibiting water excretion** (pp. 844–845)
- **Hypothalamic neurons synthesize AVP and transport it along their axons to the posterior pituitary** (pp. 844–845)
- **Increased osmolality stimulates a second group of osmoreceptors that trigger thirst, which promotes water intake** (pp. 845–846)
- **Several nonosmotic stimuli also enhance AVP secretion** (pp. 846–848)
- **Decreased effective circulating volume and low arterial pressure also trigger thirst** (pp. 848–849)
- **Defense of the effective circulating volume usually has priority over defense of osmolality** (p. 849)

### Core claims
- Water content (and thus whole-body osmolality) is set by two effectors, both feeding back through the **hypothalamus**: (1) the **kidney** (water excretion, via AVP) and (2) **thirst** (oral water intake). Osmolality deviations of ±15% cause severe CNS dysfunction.
- **AVP osmoreceptor system**: ↑ECF osmolality is the *primary* signal for AVP release from the posterior pituitary. Verney's classic dog experiments (Fig. 40-6) showed that hyperosmotic NaCl infused into the **carotid** artery (but not peripherally) abruptly stops a water diuresis — the osmosensitive site is **intracranial**; removing the posterior pituitary abolishes the effect.
- **AVP threshold and steepness (Fig. 40-7)**: plasma osmolality is normally ~290 mOsm; the **AVP release threshold ≈ 280 mOsm**. A rise of only ~1% above threshold produces a detectable [AVP] increase; [AVP] then rises **steeply** with further osmolality increase.
- **Solute specificity**: NaCl and mannitol stimulate AVP (effective osmoles that shrink cells); an equal osmotic rise from **urea has little effect** because urea permeates membranes and is a poorly effective (ineffective) osmole.
- **AVP synthesis/transport**: osmoreceptors sit in two circumventricular organs that breach the blood-brain barrier — the **OVLT** and the **SFO**. Elevated osmolality opens mechanosensitive cation channels → depolarization → ↑ firing; these project to **magnocellular neurons** of the **supraoptic (SON)** and **paraventricular (PVN)** nuclei, which synthesize AVP, package it into granules, and transport it down their axons to the **posterior pituitary** for release into the circulation.
- **AVP biochemistry**: encoded by mRNA for **preproneurophysin II**; the prohormone yields **AVP** (9 amino acids, disulfide bridge), **neurophysin II (NpII)**, and a glycopeptide. NpII mutations impair AVP secretion. AVP half-life ≈ **18 minutes**; degraded by **liver and kidney**. Liver/kidney disease (e.g., heart failure congestion) impairs degradation → inappropriately high AVP; pregnancy's placental vasopressinase accelerates degradation.
- **Thirst osmoreceptors** are *distinct* neurons (also in OVLT and SFO) that respond to hyperosmolar cell shrinkage. Hyperosmolality thus triggers **two parallel loops** with a common end point — ↑ whole-body free water: (1) AVP → AQP2 insertion in collecting duct → ↑ water reabsorption → ↓ free-water excretion; (2) thirst → ↑ free-water intake.
- **Nonosmotic AVP stimuli**: decreased ECV / arterial pressure (volume threshold higher — needs **5%–10%** drop vs. **1%** for osmolality, but then rises steeply); pregnancy; pain, nausea; drugs (morphine, nicotine, high-dose barbiturates). **Inhibitors**: alcohol, opiate antagonists; volume expansion.
- **Osmolality–volume interaction (Fig. 40-7 family of curves)**: at fixed osmolality, **volume contraction shifts the AVP-vs-osmolality curve leftward and steepens it** — low osmolality (e.g., 280 mOsm) that would normally suppress AVP still permits secretion. **Volume expansion shifts it rightward and flattens it** (reduced osmoreceptor sensitivity).
- **Three pathways link low ECV / low pressure to AVP release** (Fig. 40-9): (1) ↓ left atrial pressure → ↓ vagal afferent firing → NTS → magnocellular AVP release (AVP varies inversely with left atrial pressure); (2) ↓ECV → JGA renin → ANG II → acts on OVLT/SFO → AVP; (3) most importantly, ↓ arterial pressure → carotid sinus baroreceptors → AVP.
- **Priority rule**: under normal conditions volume and osmolality are regulated independently, but in extremes **the body defends volume at the expense of osmolality**. Severe hemorrhage / hypovolemic shock and low-ECV edematous states (CHF, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis) drive AVP-mediated water retention → **hyponatremia** (hypo-osmolality) as the price of preserving blood volume.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "An increase in the osmolality of the ECF is the primary signal for the secretion of AVP from the posterior pituitary gland." (p. 844)
- > "The threshold for AVP release is somewhat lower, ~280 mOsm … Increasing the osmolality by only 1% higher than this level is sufficient to produce a detectable increase in plasma [AVP]." (p. 844)
- > "An equivalent increase in extracellular osmolality by urea has little effect on plasma AVP levels … urea readily permeates cell membranes and hence exerts a low effective osmolality or tonicity." (pp. 844–845)
- > "Osmoreceptors of the CNS appear to be located in two areas that breech the blood-brain barrier: the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis (OVLT) and the subfornical organ (SFO)." (pp. 844–845)
- > "The half-life of AVP in the circulation is 18 minutes." (p. 845)
- > "Fairly large reductions in effective circulating volume (5% to 10%) are required to stimulate AVP release of similar amounts." (p. 846)
- > "In general, the body defends volume at the expense of osmolality." (p. 849)

### Equations
- **AVP–osmolality threshold relation** (qualitative, from Fig. 40-7):

  $$[\text{AVP}]_{\text{plasma}} \approx S \cdot (\text{Osm}_{\text{plasma}} - \text{Osm}_{\text{threshold}}), \quad \text{Osm}_{\text{threshold}} \approx 280\ \text{mOsm}$$

  where slope $S$ and threshold both shift with effective circulating volume: volume contraction lowers $\text{Osm}_{\text{threshold}}$ and raises $S$; volume expansion does the opposite.

## Glossary

- **Effective circulating volume (ECV)** — functional blood volume reflecting tissue perfusion / vascular fullness; the regulated signal for Na⁺ excretion. Dissociates from total ECF in CHF, nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis.
- **Four parallel effector pathways** — RAAS, sympathetic nerves, AVP, ANP (the last works by *decreasing* its activity).
- **Renin** — protease from JGA granular cells; cleaves angiotensinogen → ANG I. Released by ↓BP (sympathetic), ↓macula densa [NaCl], ↓afferent stretch (↓[Ca²⁺]ᵢ).
- **ACE** — converts ANG I → ANG II; abundant in lung endothelium, also renal.
- **ANG II** — vasoconstriction; stimulates aldosterone, Na-H exchange, tubuloglomerular feedback, thirst + AVP (volume–osmolality intersection).
- **Aldosterone** — adrenal glomerulosa mineralocorticoid; ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption in distal/collecting tubules.
- **ANP** — atrial natriuretic peptide; atrial-stretch-released; receptor guanylyl cyclase; ↑ GFR and natriuresis; ↓ renin, aldosterone, AVP.
- **Pressure natriuresis / pressure diuresis** — ↑ arterial pressure → ↑ Na⁺ excretion independent of ECV (multiple mechanisms).
- **AVP / ADH (arginine vasopressin)** — nonapeptide; ↑ collecting-duct AQP2 → water retention; threshold ~280 mOsm; half-life 18 min.
- **Osmoreceptors** — OVLT and SFO (circumventricular, breach BBB); separate neuron populations for AVP and thirst.
- **OVLT / SFO** — organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis / subfornical organ.
- **Magnocellular neurons** — SON and PVN; synthesize and release AVP.
- **Effective vs. ineffective osmole** — NaCl/mannitol stimulate AVP (shrink cells); urea does not (permeates membranes).
- **Volume-defense priority** — body defends volume over osmolality in extremes → hyponatremia of hemorrhage, CHF, cirrhosis.
- **Goldblatt hypertension** — one- and two-kidney renal-artery-clip models; renal baroreceptor as long-term BP sensor.
- **Diuretic vs. aquaretic** — natriuretic (Na⁺ loss) vs. pure water loss (VRAs).
