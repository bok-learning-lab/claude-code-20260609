## 1. What is physiology? (p. 2)

### Core claims
- Physiology is the dynamic, functional study of life — distinct from but inseparable from structural disciplines (anatomy, histology, structural biology).
- It scales across levels of resolution: whole-person (e.g. exercise physiology), organ-system (cardiovascular, respiratory, GI), and cellular/molecular ("general physiology," now renamed **cellular and molecular physiology**).
- **Comparative physiology** treats differences across species at every level from molecule to organism.
- **Medical physiology** specifically integrates molecule → cell → organ → whole human body.
- Physiology is the parent discipline of biochemistry, biophysics, and neuroscience.
- Historical trajectory: increasingly **quantitative** — many leading physiologists trained as chemists, physicists, mathematicians, or engineers.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Physiology is the dynamic study of life." (p. 2)
- > "structure and function are inextricably linked because the living structures perform the functions." (p. 2)
- > "medical physiology takes a global view of the human body; but in doing so, it requires an integrated understanding of events at the level of molecules, cells, and organs." (p. 2)
- > "Physiology is the mother of several biological sciences, having given birth to the disciplines of biochemistry, biophysics, and neuroscience…" (p. 2)

### Terminology
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| general physiology | older name for cellular/molecular principles common to all tissues |
| cellular and molecular physiology | contemporary replacement |
| comparative physiology | cross-species physiology at any scale |
| medical physiology | integrated study of the human body |

## 2. Physiological genomics: the link between organ and gene (pp. 2–3)

### Core claims
- Organ systems must share information; communication between cells and organs is **almost always molecular** (ions like H⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺; small chemicals; neurotransmitters; long-range hormones).
- The **genome (with epigenetic modifications)** is the "grand organizer" that controls molecules, cells, and organs.
- Historically physiology stopped at the cell; molecular biology took over below that line. Modern physiology now reaches into DNA because DNA encodes the proteins physiologists care about.
- **Physiological genomics** (= **functional genomics**) is the new branch devoted to the physiological roles of genes. It "closes the circle" between organ physiology and molecular biology.
- The **knockout mouse** is the paradigmatic tool: knocking out a supposedly essential gene may produce no effect or unexpected effects, and the physiologist must explain why. Understanding a knockout requires re-evaluating the whole mouse's physiology.
- For polygenic parameters (e.g. **blood pressure**), epidemiologists identify candidate polymorphisms statistically; the physiologist then must (1) show the variant *can* modulate the parameter under controlled conditions and (2) determine the mechanism.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "the sharing of information between organs and between cells takes place at the level of atoms or molecules." (p. 2)
- > "The grand organizer — the master that controls the molecules, the cells, and the organs and the way they interact — is the genome with its epigenetic modifications." (p. 2)
- > "Physiology is unique among the basic medical sciences in that it is both broad in its scope … and integrative in its outlook." (p. 3)

### Workflow diagram (concept, no figure in source)

```
GWAS / epidemiology → candidate polymorphism
       ↓
Physiologist: controlled experiments
       ↓
(1) Does the variant modulate the parameter?
(2) What is the mechanism?
```

## 3. Cells live in a highly protected *milieu intérieur* (pp. 3–4)

### Historical anchor
**Claude Bernard, 1878** — *Leçons sur les phénomènes de la vie communs aux animaux et aux végétaux*. The chapter pivots around Bernard's framework.

### Core claims
- Animals have **two environments**: the *milieu extérieur* (the external world surrounding the organism) and the *milieu intérieur* (the controlled internal fluid bathing tissues and cells).
- The *milieu intérieur* is the **extracellular fluid (ECF)** — Bernard described it as "the organic liquid that circulates and bathes all the anatomic elements of the tissues, the lymph or the plasma."
- Bernard's greenhouse metaphor: the *milieu intérieur* isolates organs from environmental vagaries, so physiological functions proceed indifferent to external conditions.
- Topological insight: fluids inside the lumen of GI tract, sweat ducts, and renal tubules are **not really "inside" the body** — they are continuous with the *milieu extérieur*.
- Higher organisms differentiate organs precisely so each organ can "compensate and equilibrate" against external change. The cardiovascular, respiratory, urinary, GI, and endocrine systems (Sections IV–VIII of the book) collectively create and maintain the constant internal environment.
- **"Fixité du milieu intérieur"** is, in Bernard's phrasing, "the condition of free, independent life."

### Four properties of a living organism (p. 3, end of section)
1. Exchanges matter and energy with the environment.
2. Receives signals from the environment and reacts.
3. Has a life cycle of growth and reproduction.
4. Adapts to changing circumstances.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "the milieu extérieur that physically surrounds the whole organism; and the milieu intérieur, in which the tissues and cells of the organism live." (p. 3)
- > "this internal environment is what we today call the extracellular fluid." (p. 3)
- > "the contents of the gastrointestinal tract, sweat ducts, and renal tubules are all outside the body. They are all continuous with the milieu extérieur." (p. 3)
- > "the 'fixité du milieu intérieur' … is the condition of 'free, independent life.'" (p. 3, Bernard quoted)

## 4. Homeostasis and feedback control (pp. 4–5)

### Definition
**Homeostasis** = the control of a vital parameter. The body controls many such parameters at two scales:

| Scale | Examples |
|---|---|
| Whole body | arterial pressure, blood volume |
| *Milieu intérieur* (ECF) | body core temperature; plasma O₂, glucose, K⁺, Ca²⁺, H⁺ |
| Single cell | volume, intracellular ions (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, H⁺), energy charge (ATP) |

### The four required elements of a negative-feedback loop (p. 4)
The textbook lays out a control-theoretic recipe — useful for the AI application as a canonical schema:

1. **Sensor** — measures the vital parameter (or a proxy).
2. **Comparator** — compares input signal to an internal **set-point**; outputs an **error signal** (difference signal).
3. **Gain stage** — multiplies the error by a proportionality factor to produce an **output signal** (e.g., insulin release).
4. **Effector** — acts to oppose the input perturbation and return the parameter toward set-point.

### Negative-feedback schematic (concept, source has no figure)

```
disturbance
    │
    ▼
[parameter] ──► [sensor] ──► [comparator vs. set-point] ──► error
                                                              │
                                                       (× gain)
                                                              ▼
                                          [effector] ◄── output signal
                                              │
                                              └─► acts on parameter (opposes disturbance)
```

### Loop interactions and hierarchy
- Loops can act **synergistically** or **antagonistically** (e.g., insulin lowers blood glucose; epinephrine and cortisol raise it).
- Loops are organized **hierarchically** — example: hypothalamus → anterior pituitary → adrenal cortex → cortisol → blood glucose.
- **Positive feedback** is also used physiologically (margin Note €) N1-1 referenced in source — examples developed later in the book).

### Redundancy
The more vital the parameter, the more parallel systems regulate it. Redundancy explains why some gene knockouts produce no observed phenotype: backup loops mask the loss.

### Steady state vs. equilibrium (a key conceptual distinction)
- **Equilibrium**: no energy consumption; no net flux; parameter is constant because forces balance with no work being done.
- **Steady state**: parameter is held constant *by active matching* of opposing fluxes; energy is continuously consumed.
- Homeostasis is a **steady state**, not equilibrium.

This distinction is one of the most useful framings in the chapter for a physiology-RAG application — flag it.

### Adaptation and priority resolution
- Feedback loops are **flexible** — the same stimulus produces different responses depending on the system's **state** (history-dependent).
- **Acclimatization example**: at sea level, low inspired O₂ → modest ↑ in breathing. After high-altitude acclimatization, same low O₂ → much greater ↑ in breathing.
- **Genetic adaptation**: long-resident high-altitude populations tolerate hypoxia better than acclimatized lowlanders.
- **Priority conflicts**: during exercise, thermoregulation (sweating) reduces blood volume. The body prioritizes blood volume over core temperature → sweating is reduced even though it sacrifices cooling. If exercise continues, the consequence is **heat stroke**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Homeostasis is the control of a vital parameter." (p. 4)
- > "Negative feedback requires at least four elements." (p. 4)
- > "homeostasis occurs at a price: energy." (p. 4)
- > "Equilibrium is a state that does not involve energy consumption. Instead, a well-regulated parameter is generally in a steady state." (p. 4)
- > "each cell or organ must respect the needs of the body as a whole and not run amok for its own greedy interests." (p. 4)

## 5. Medicine as "physiology gone awry" (pp. 4–5)

### Core claims
- Medicine borrows its physicochemical principles from physiology and uses healthy physiology as a **reference state** against which disease is read.
- Disease is a chain reaction: a primary malfunction → primary pathological effect → secondary effects that are themselves *appropriate* feedback responses to the primary lesion.
  - Worked example (textbook): heart failure → ↓ cardiac output → fluid overload (a feedback-loop response that becomes part of the pathology).
- Clinician-physiologists have discovered much of physiology by studying disease. The textbook uses **clinical boxes** throughout to anchor concepts.
- Many **clinical functional tests** are direct technology transfers from the physiology lab — cardiac monitoring, pulmonary function tests, renal clearance tests, plasma ion/gas/hormone assays. Technology flows both ways between bench and clinic.
- The book's evidence base draws from human, other mammalian, and even invertebrate (squid, slime mold) experiments, but **the ultimate focus is the human body**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Medicine also uses physiology as a reference state: it is essential to know how organs and systems function in the healthy person to grasp which components may be malfunctioning in a patient." (pp. 4–5)
- > "the exchange of information between medicine and physiology is a two-way street." (p. 5)
- > "our ultimate focus is on the human body." (p. 5)

## Key terms introduced (glossary for downstream chunking)

- **Physiology** — dynamic functional study of life.
- **Comparative physiology** — cross-species physiology.
- **Medical physiology** — integrated human physiology (molecule → cell → organ → body).
- **Cellular and molecular physiology** — modern term for what was once "general physiology."
- **Physiological / functional genomics** — physiological roles of genes.
- ***Milieu intérieur*** (Bernard, 1878) — the controlled internal fluid environment = the **extracellular fluid (ECF)**.
- ***Milieu extérieur*** — the external environment (and, topologically, the contents of GI tract, sweat ducts, and renal tubule lumens).
- **Homeostasis** — control of a vital parameter.
- **Set-point**, **error/difference signal**, **gain**, **effector** — the four functional elements of a negative-feedback loop.
- **Negative feedback** — output opposes the perturbing input.
- **Positive feedback** — output reinforces the input (used selectively in physiology).
- **Equilibrium** vs. **steady state** — both produce constancy; only steady state requires energy.
- **Redundancy** — multiple parallel loops regulate the most vital parameters.
- **Acclimatization** — within-lifetime physiological adjustment (state-dependence).
- **Adaptation** — between-generation / evolutionary adjustment.
- **Reference state** — healthy physiology used as the standard against which disease deviation is interpreted.
