## Section 1 — Motor units and muscle function (pp. 1204–1208)

### Subsection headings
- **The motor unit is the functional element of muscle contraction** (pp. 1204–1205)
- **Muscle force rises with the recruitment of motor units and an increase in their firing frequency** (pp. 1205–1206)
- **Compared with type I motor units, type II units are faster and stronger but more fatigable** (pp. 1205–1207)
- **As external forces stretch muscle, series elastic elements contribute a larger fraction of total tension** (pp. 1206–1207)
- **The action of a muscle depends on the axis of its fibers and its origin and insertion on the skeleton** (pp. 1207–1208)
- **Fluid and energetically efficient movements require learning** (pp. 1207–1208)
- **Strength versus endurance training differentially alters the properties of motor units** (p. 1208)

### Core claims
- Skeletal muscle is ~30–50% of body mass; contractile energy demand can rise >100-fold over rest. Muscle converts only ~25% of substrate energy to mechanical work; the rest is heat — coupling exercise to thermoregulation.
- **Motor unit** = one motor neuron + all the fibers it innervates. A biceps brachii is served by ~750 somatic motor neurons. Every fiber fires when its neuron fires; one fiber has exactly one neuron, but a 100-fiber volume of muscle is innervated by ~50 different neurons (units intermingle). Finer control ⇔ smaller innervation ratio.
- **Size principle**: motor units recruit smallest → largest (weakest → strongest). A small soma has higher input resistance, so a given synaptic current $I_{syn}$ drives it to threshold sooner. Small units innervate few **slow-twitch type I** fibers (fatigue-resistant, recruited first); large units innervate many **fast-twitch type II** fibers (type IIx last, for peak force).
- **Grading force**: below the upper recruitment limit, force is graded by both adding units and **rate coding** (firing frequency). Once all units are recruited, further force is purely rate coding. Recruitment can be complete by ~50% of max force in some muscles, ~90% in others.
- **Brainstem neuromodulation**: descending serotonergic/noradrenergic input sets the gain of a motor-neuron pool; it can sustain self-firing for posture, and its withdrawal in sleep relaxes muscle.
- **Fiber-type physiology** (see fiber table below): max force per cross-sectional area is constant across types (~25 N/cm²), so a unit's force ∝ number and diameter of its fibers. Slow type I units fire at 10–50 Hz and fuse to tetanus at lower frequencies; fast units fire 30 to >100 Hz.
- **Series elastic elements** (tendon, intramuscular connective tissue) behave as a spring; passive tension rises exponentially with length. In the stretch–shorten cycle of running, landing pre-stretches the calf SEE so the next push-off reaches higher total tension with a smaller active-tension increment.
- **Work modes**: *concentric* — cross-bridge force > load, muscle shortens, does positive work (peak power at ~30–40% of isometric tension and ~30–40% of max shortening velocity). *Isometric* — length fixed, sarcomeres shorten by stretching SEE. *Eccentric* — force < load, muscle lengthens, does negative work (a brake); eccentric tension can exceed isometric because passive SEE tension adds, but eccentric contractions most readily damage fibers/tendon (e.g., Achilles rupture).
- **Anatomy of action**: pennation — few long parallel fibers (sartorius) shorten fast; many short angled fibers (gastrocnemius) develop more force. *Torque* = (perpendicular component of muscle force) × D = total force × **moment arm**; moment arm changes through range of motion (brachialis: max torque ~60° flexion).
- **Training specificity**: a motor neuron's long-term firing pattern dictates its fibers' properties (proved by cross-innervation: a fast nerve onto slow muscle makes it faster/more fatigable, and vice versa). Only **recruited** units adapt. Endurance/aerobic training → greater oxidative capacity (↑O₂ delivery, capillaries, mitochondria) and fatigue resistance (the slender build of distance runners). Strength/anaerobic training → ↑contractile protein in type IIx units (hypertrophy of sprinters/weightlifters).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "This intrinsic behavior of motor-unit recruitment is known as the size principle and reflects inherent differences in the biophysical properties of respective motor neurons." (p. 1205)
- > "Within a given motor unit, each muscle fiber is of the same muscle fiber type." (p. 1205)
- > "The maximal force that can develop per cross-sectional area of muscle tissue is constant across fiber types (~25 N/cm²)." (p. 1206)
- > "Thus, eccentric contractions are much more likely than isometric or isotonic contractions to damage muscle fibers and connective tissue, as occurs with the common injury of a ruptured Achilles tendon." (p. 1207)
- > "A corollary of this principle is that physical activity leads to adaptation only in those motor units that are actually recruited during the activity." (p. 1208)
- > "The firing pattern of the α motor neuron—over time—ultimately determines the contractile and metabolic properties of the muscle fibers in the corresponding motor unit." (p. 1208)

## Section 2 — Conversion of chemical energy to mechanical work (pp. 1208–1212)

### Subsection headings
- **ATP and PCr provide immediate but limited energy** (pp. 1208–1209)
- **Anaerobic glycolysis provides a rapid but self-limited source of ATP** (pp. 1209–1210)
- **Oxidation of glucose, lactate, and fatty acids provides a slower but long-term source of ATP** (pp. 1210–1212)

### Core claims
- **Three energy systems matched to event duration** (Fig. 60-4): immediate ATP+PCr for power events (≤ a few seconds); nonoxidative (anaerobic) glycolysis of muscle glycogen for spurts of seconds to ~1 min; oxidative phosphorylation of circulating glucose and fat for activity ≥ ~2 min. Muscle holds [ATP]/[ADP] nearly constant despite >100-fold turnover swings.
- **Phosphagens**: resting muscle holds ~5–6 mmol/kg ATP but ~25–30 mmol/kg PCr (~5× more energy). Creatine kinase regenerates ATP from PCr and buffers [ATP] so tightly that [ATP] barely moves — but the products of hydrolysis (Pᵢ, ADP, AMP) are the metabolic signals. Adenylate kinase (myokinase) salvages ATP from 2 ADP but is limited by the small ADP pool. Phosphagens last only several seconds.
- **Anaerobic glycolysis**: glycogenolysis (phosphorylase, ↑ by sympathetic epinephrine via β-receptors) → glucose-1-P → G6P; GLUT4 can also import blood glucose (hexokinase → G6P). Yields only **2 ATP/glucose** and is self-limiting — H⁺ from lactic acid drops pHᵢ from ~7.1 toward ~6.2, inhibiting glycolysis and contraction; intense effort limited to ~1 min.
- **Oxidative metabolism**: cardiovascular/respiratory systems need 1–2 min to raise O₂ delivery (endurance training speeds this), so early effort leans on phosphagens + glycolysis. Aerobic glucose oxidation yields ~**30 ATP/glucose** (~15× the anaerobic yield, and the nonoxidative path itself liberates ~100× the immediate ATP+PCr release). Most oxidized glucose comes from circulation, replenished by hepatic glycogenolysis + gluconeogenesis so blood glucose is held stable; exercising muscle ↑ glucose uptake 7–40× via insulin-independent (AMPK-driven) GLUT4 translocation — relevant to diabetes management.
- **Lactate shuttle**: lactate is produced even in fully aerobic muscle; oxidative (I, IIa) fibers reconvert it to pyruvate, glycolytic (IIx) fibers export it to neighbors, heart, or liver (gluconeogenesis). The **Cori cycle** (lactate → hepatic glucose) and **glucose–alanine cycle** redistribute glycogen from resting to exercising muscle. Above ~65% VO₂max, lactate production outruns clearance → exponential rise in blood [lactate] (the lactate/anaerobic threshold); training raises clearance. Oxidation removes ~75% of lactate, gluconeogenesis the rest.
- **Fat**: adipose triacylglycerol (~132,000 kcal in a 70-kg person) is mobilized by the sympathetic system (+ growth hormone after 30–40 min). With adequate O₂, fatty acids supply up to **60%** of muscle fuel in prolonged exercise; palmitate oxidation yields **106 ATP**.
- **Fuel selection / crossover**: at rest and low–moderate intensity with ample O₂, fat dominates (e.g., at 50% VO₂max fat > half of energy); as intensity rises there is a **crossover** to carbohydrate (intramuscular glycogen + blood glucose), which yields slightly more ATP per liter O₂. Endurance training shifts the mix toward fat (glycogen sparing) via more capillaries and mitochondria. In carbohydrate deprivation, starvation, or ultra-endurance, muscle also oxidizes ketone bodies.

### Equations
- **ATP hydrolysis** (Eq. 60-1), ΔG ≈ −11.5 kcal/mol under physiological conditions:
  $$\text{ATP} \rightarrow \text{ADP}^- + \text{P}_i + \text{H}^+ \qquad \Delta G$$
- **Creatine kinase** buffering of ATP (Eq. 60-2):
  $$\text{ADP} + \text{PCr} \xrightarrow{\text{creatine kinase}} \text{ATP} + \text{creatine}$$
- **Adenylate kinase / myokinase** (Eq. 60-3):
  $$\text{ADP} + \text{ADP} \xrightarrow{\text{adenylate kinase}} \text{ATP} + \text{AMP}$$
- **Anaerobic glycolysis to lactate** (Eq. 60-4) — 2 ATP/glucose:
  $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 2\,\text{ADP} + 2\,\text{P}_i \rightarrow 2\,\text{C}_3\text{H}_5\text{O}_3^- + 2\,\text{ATP} + 2\,\text{H}^+ + \text{heat}$$
- **Aerobic glucose oxidation** (Eq. 60-5) — 30 ATP/glucose:
  $$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\,\text{O}_2 + 30\,\text{ADP} + 30\,\text{P}_i \rightarrow 6\,\text{CO}_2 + 6\,\text{H}_2\text{O} + 30\,\text{ATP} + \text{heat}$$
- **Palmitate oxidation** (Eq. 60-6) — 106 ATP:
  $$\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{31}\text{COOH} + 23\,\text{O}_2 + 106\,\text{ADP} + 106\,\text{P}_i \rightarrow 16\,\text{CO}_2 + 16\,\text{H}_2\text{O} + 106\,\text{ATP} + \text{heat}$$

- > "These two energy stores are sufficient to support intense contractile activity for only a few seconds." (p. 1209)
- > "However, oxidative metabolism of the same amount of glucose provides 15-fold more energy than the nonoxidative metabolism of glucose." (p. 1210)
- > "In the presence of adequate O₂, fatty acids provide up to 60% of the oxidized fuel supply of muscle during prolonged exercise." (p. 1211)

## Section 3 — Muscle fatigue (pp. 1212–1213)

### Subsection headings
- **Fatigued muscle produces less force and has a reduced velocity of shortening** (pp. 1212–1213)
- **Changes in the CNS produce central fatigue** (p. 1213)
- **Impaired excitability and impaired Ca²⁺ release can produce peripheral fatigue** (p. 1213)
- **Fatigue can result from ATP depletion, lactic acid accumulation, and glycogen depletion** (p. 1213)

### Core claims
- **Definition**: fatigue = inability to maintain a desired power output, with declines in both force and shortening velocity, from fewer active cross-bridges and less force per cross-bridge. Force usually falls before velocity. Fatigue is **reversible with rest** — distinguishing it from muscle damage (e.g., eccentric injury, days to recover). Fatigue may be protective.
- **Central fatigue**: CNS-level — altered muscle-afferent input, reduced excitatory drive to motor centers, changed α/γ motor-neuron excitability. Minor in trained athletes who pace themselves; larger in novices and during boring/repetitive tasks. External input (shouting, cheering) can transiently boost output, showing supraspinal pathways can oppose central fatigue.
- **Peripheral fatigue** — at the fiber, not the neuromuscular junction (NMJ transmission rarely blocks):
  - **High-frequency fatigue** (type II, intense exercise): sustained firing makes Na⁺/K⁺ fluxes exceed the Na-K pump, so [K⁺]ₒ and [Na⁺]ᵢ rise and resting Vₘ depolarizes 10–20 mV, inactivating Na⁺ channels and impairing AP initiation/propagation and T-tubule L-type-channel coupling to SR Ca²⁺ release. Recovers within ~30 min.
  - **Low-frequency fatigue** (type I, prolonged moderate exercise): SR Ca²⁺ release falls (release-channel changes + SERCA inhibition + lower SR Ca²⁺ stores), shrinking the [Ca²⁺]ᵢ transient. Effect is worst at low stimulation frequencies, where unfused tetani leave [Ca²⁺]ᵢ below troponin-C saturation; high-frequency fused tetani mask it. Recovery takes several hours.
- **Metabolic causes**: **ATP depletion** — [ATP] can fall from ~5 mM to <2 mM locally at cross-bridges and pumps; rising Pᵢ, ADP, Mg²⁺, lactate, H⁺ impair SERCA and ryanodine-receptor Ca²⁺ release. **Lactic acid** — pHᵢ to ~6.2 inhibits myosin ATPase, Ca²⁺–troponin binding, the Na-K pump, and phosphofructokinase (glycolysis's rate-limiting step); low pH plus high Pᵢ together impair the weak→strong cross-bridge transition and lower Ca²⁺ sensitivity. **Glycogen depletion** — in prolonged ~50% effort with good O₂ delivery, oxidative-fiber glycogen runs out → "hitting the wall"; endurance, training, and carbohydrate loading postpone it.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Muscle fatigue is defined as the inability to maintain a desired power output—resulting from muscle contraction against a load—with a decline in both force and velocity of shortening." (p. 1212)
- > "Muscle fatigue is reversible with rest, which contrasts with muscle damage or weakness." (p. 1212)
- > "Fatigue resulting from impaired membrane excitability is particularly apparent at high frequencies of stimulation during recruitment of type-II motor units—high-frequency fatigue." (p. 1213)
- > "Fatigue resulting from impaired Ca²⁺ release is thus particularly apparent at low frequencies of stimulation during recruitment of type I motor units—low-frequency fatigue." (p. 1213)
- > "Long-distance runners describe this phenomenon as 'hitting the wall.'" (p. 1213)

## Section 4 — Determinants of maximal O₂ uptake and consumption (pp. 1213–1216)

### Subsection headings
- **Maximal O₂ uptake by the lungs can exceed resting O₂ uptake by more than 20-fold** (pp. 1213–1214)
- **O₂ uptake by muscle is the product of muscle blood flow and O₂ extraction** (p. 1214)
- **O₂ delivery by the cardiovascular system is the limiting step for maximal O₂ utilization** (pp. 1214–1216)
- **Effective circulating volume takes priority over cutaneous blood flow for thermoregulation** (p. 1216)

### Core claims
- **Three transport steps**, atmosphere → mitochondria: (1) pulmonary uptake (depends on ventilation); (2) delivery (blood flow × O₂ content); (3) extraction (O₂ delivery and the blood–mitochondria PO₂ gradient).
- **VO₂ and VO₂max**: resting VO₂ ≈ 250 mL/min (≈ 3.6 mL O₂·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹). As power rises, VO₂ plateaus at **VO₂max** — the objective index of aerobic capacity. Ranges: 10–20 mL·min⁻¹·kg⁻¹ in severe cardiopulmonary disease; 30–40 in mildly active middle-aged adults (3-month aerobic program raises it >20%); 80–90 (>20× resting) in elite endurance athletes. Hemorrhage/altitude lower it; transfusion/conditioning raise it.
- **Measurement**: incremental cycle-ergometer or treadmill test; criteria for VO₂max = (1) can't sustain the prescribed power, (2) VO₂ plateaus as power rises, (3) respiratory exchange ratio (V̇CO₂/V̇O₂) > 1.15 (a transient non-steady-state value reflecting hyperventilation that blows off CO₂ stores, not a true respiratory quotient).
- **Fick principle for muscle** (Eq. 60-8): VO₂ = blood flow × arteriovenous O₂ difference. Onset of exercise: rising mitochondrial respiration drops intracellular PO₂, steepening the diffusion gradient and ↑ extraction; anticipatory CNS hyperventilation slightly raises CaO₂ (e.g., 20.0 → 20.4 mL O₂/mL blood) and lowers PCO₂.
- **The respiratory response** is folded into O₂ uptake: alveolar ventilation rises to keep alveolar PO₂ high enough to fully saturate arterial blood. Anticipatory ventilation (before metabolic need) is driven by joint/muscle mechanoreceptors, descending cortical input, and chemoreceptor resetting, and is enhanced in trained athletes. The body's total O₂ store (~1 L, mostly Hb-bound) would support only ~10–30 s of exercise, so continuous matched transport is mandatory.
- **What limits VO₂max** — three competing models: (a) **lung limitation** — alveolar diffusion fails to fully saturate arterial blood in a subset of elite athletes (and racehorses), causing exercise-induced arterial desaturation, possibly from V/Q mismatch at very high cardiac output; (b) **cardiovascular O₂ delivery (prevailing view)** — maximal cardiac output, hence convective O₂ delivery, is the limiting step; training raises both max cardiac output/muscle flow and VO₂max roughly in parallel (Fig. 60-7); (c) **muscle O₂ extraction (diffusive model)** — kinetics of O₂ diffusion from Hb to mitochondrial matrix limits, set by muscle diffusing capacity and the Hb–mitochondria PO₂ gradient.
- **Effective circulating volume vs. skin flow**: exercise in heat pits skin flow (cooling) against muscle flow. Effective circulating volume falls for three reasons (Fig. 60-8): (1) plasma water shifts to interstitium (↑ capillary hydrostatic pressure as arterioles dilate; ↑ intracellular osmolality) — up to >500 mL (~15% of plasma) when intensity > 40% VO₂max; (2) sweat loss (>3% body weight → early heat illness, dehydration); (3) blood pooling in compliant cutaneous veins. Low-pressure cardiopulmonary baroreceptors trigger sympathetic compensation: ↑ splanchnic resistance, and partial offset of thermoregulatory vasodilation to skin and to active muscle — protecting blood pressure at the cost of reduced cooling (heat storage → heat stroke) and possibly reduced muscle O₂ delivery.

### Equations
- **Fick principle for muscle O₂ uptake** (Eq. 60-8):
  $$\dot V_{O_2} = F \cdot (C_{aO_2} - C_{\bar v O_2})$$
  $F$ = muscle blood flow; $C_{aO_2}$, $C_{\bar v O_2}$ = arterial and mixed-venous O₂ content.
- **Convective arterial O₂-delivery rate** (Eq. 60-7) — cardiac output × arterial O₂ content:
  $$\text{O}_2\text{ delivery} = (\text{HR} \times \text{SV}) \cdot C_{aO_2}$$
- **Whole-body VO₂max as an extension** (used in Section 6, Eq. 60-9):
  $$\dot V_{O_2,\text{max}} = \text{HR}_{opt} \cdot \text{SV}_{opt} \cdot (C_{aO_2} - C_{\bar v O_2})_{\text{max}}$$
  i.e., VO₂max = (optimal maximal cardiac output) × (maximal a–v O₂ difference).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "According to the Fick equation (see p. 423), VO₂ is the product of blood flow to muscle (F) and the arteriovenous (a-v) difference for O₂." (p. 1214)
- > "Maximal oxygen uptake measured at the lungs (VO₂max) is an objective index of the functional capacity of the body's ability to generate aerobic power." (p. 1214)
- > "According to the prevalent view, a limitation in O₂ transport by the cardiovascular system determines VO₂max." (p. 1214)
- > "The body's total store of O₂ is ~1 L (mainly in the form of O₂ bound to hemoglobin), a volume that … could support moderate aerobic exercise for 30 seconds at best." (p. 1214)
- > "When we exercise in the heat, our circulatory systems must simultaneously support a large blood flow to both the skin and contracting muscles." (p. 1216)

## Section 5 — Sweating (pp. 1216–1219)

### Subsection headings
- **Eccrine, but not apocrine, sweat glands contribute to temperature regulation** (pp. 1216–1217)
- **Eccrine sweat glands are tubules comprising a secretory coiled gland and a reabsorptive duct** (pp. 1217–1218)
- **The NaCl content of sweat increases with the rate of secretion but decreases with acclimatization to heat** (pp. 1218–1219)
- **The hyperthermia of exercise stimulates eccrine sweat glands** (p. 1219)

### Core claims
- **Two gland types**: **Apocrine** — few (~100,000), large (2–3 mm), in axillary/anogenital skin, empty into hair follicles, secrete lipid/carbohydrate-rich odorous fluid (possible pheromones), **no thermoregulatory role**. **Eccrine** — millions, small (50–100 µm), over most of the body (densest on palms/soles), present at birth, are the thermoregulatory effector. Women sweat less than men (less output per gland, not fewer glands).
- **Eccrine structure/function**: a coiled secretory gland (deep dermis) + a reabsorptive duct, wrapped in microvasculature, innervated by **cholinergic postganglionic sympathetic** fibers. ACh → muscarinic receptors → PLC → PKC and ↑[Ca²⁺]ᵢ → **primary secretion** (isotonic, protein-free-plasma-like) by the general Cl⁻-secretion mechanism (basolateral Na/K/Cl cotransporter loads Cl⁻; apical Cl⁻ channel; lumen-negative voltage drives paracellular Na⁺; NaCl/urea/lactate osmotically pull water). Myoepithelial cells may expel sweat in pulses, but glandular hydrostatic pressure can exceed 500 mm Hg.
- **Ductal reabsorption**: duct cells reabsorb Na⁺ (apical **ENaC**) and Cl⁻ (apical **CFTR**), with basolateral Na-K pump and Cl⁻ channel; low water permeability means little water follows, so final sweat is **hypotonic**. Because sweat is hypotonic, sweating loses solute-free water > salt → ECF contracts and becomes hyperosmolar, drawing water out of cells; the free-water loss ultimately comes from all body compartments.
- **Flow dependence** (Fig. 60-10): at low flow the duct reabsorbs nearly all Na⁺/Cl⁻ (sweat [Cl⁻] as low as ~10–20 mM); at high flow the load outruns reabsorption (Na⁺/Cl⁻ ~60–70 mM). Sweat [K⁺] stays ~5–10 mM, flow-independent.
- **Cystic fibrosis**: defective apical CFTR in duct cells → normal acinar secretion but failed Cl⁻ (and hence Na⁺) reabsorption → salty sweat (the diagnostic sweat-chloride finding).
- **Sweat rates and rehydration**: thermoregulatory sweating commonly 1–2 L/hr (up to **4 L/hr** when heat-acclimatized) — a major fraction of total-body water. Free-water drinking rehydrates poorly (it lowers ECF osmolality, suppressing thirst and AVP and ↑ renal free-water loss); **NaCl + water** rehydrates better (keeps plasma [Na⁺] up, sustains thirst, delays diuresis).
- **Acclimatization**: over weeks, aldosterone-mediated ↑ ductal NaCl reabsorption makes sweat more hypotonic — salt loss falls from >30 g/day to a few g/day — conserving NaCl and effective circulating volume while preserving evaporative cooling. Training also lowers the core-temperature threshold (and skin-temperature threshold) for sweating onset; the hypothalamus drives sweating from rising core T. Above ambient ~30°C, radiation/convection/conduction fail and **evaporative cooling dominates** (and is itself impaired by high humidity).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Apocrine sweat glands have no role in temperature regulation in humans, although they may act as a source of pheromones." (p. 1217)
- > "Because the water permeability of the epithelium lining the sweat duct is low, water reabsorption is limited, so that the final secretory fluid is always hypotonic to plasma." (p. 1218)
- > "In patients with cystic fibrosis … abnormal sweat gland function is attributable to a defect in the CFTR." (p. 1218)
- > "With ample, continuing hydration, a heat-acclimatized individual can sweat up to 4 L/hr during maximal sweating." (p. 1219)
- > "A more effective means of restoring body fluid volume is to ingest NaCl with water." (p. 1219)

## Section 6 — Endurance (aerobic) training (pp. 1219–1222)

### Subsection headings
- **Aerobic training requires regular periods of stress and recovery** (pp. 1219–1220)
- **Aerobic training increases maximal O₂ delivery by increasing plasma volume and maximal cardiac output** (pp. 1219–1221)
- **Aerobic training enhances O₂ diffusion into muscle** (p. 1221)
- **Aerobic training increases mitochondrial content** (pp. 1221–1222)

### Core claims
- **Four conditions for a training effect**: (1) intensity above a critical threshold, (2) sufficient duration per session, (3) regular repetition (several times/week), (4) adequate rest between sessions (adaptations occur during recovery). Progressive intensity recruits and adapts type I → IIa → IIx in order; strength training hypertrophies IIx. Detraining reverses adaptations (loss of aerobic capacity in I/IIa, loss of mass in IIx). Conditioning raises VO₂max and heat-elimination capacity.
- **Maximizing CaO₂**: theoretically via ↑ alveolar ventilation, ↑ pulmonary diffusing capacity, better V/Q matching, or ↑ [Hb] — but in practice the lung already saturates Hb to ~97% even at max power, so ventilation/diffusion is not the bottleneck, and training does **not** raise [Hb]. In fact [Hb] is slightly low in endurance athletes — **sports anemia** — from plasma-volume expansion outpacing red-cell expansion. **Blood doping** (pre-competition transfusion) raises O₂ capacity but also viscosity and vascular resistance (and is illegal and hazardous, especially with sweat-driven hemoconcentration).
- **Maximizing cardiac output (the dominant mechanism)**: training does not raise max heart rate and has little effect on extraction, so nearly all the VO₂max gain comes from ↑ **maximal cardiac stroke volume**. Plasma-volume expansion ↑ preload → ↑ filling → ↑ stroke volume (Starling's law), with cardiac hypertrophy. Max cardiac output can rise ~40% while max aerobic power rises ~50% — the extra ~10% from enhanced extraction (Cao₂ − Cvo₂)max via capillary and mitochondrial proliferation. A trained heart also delivers a given output at lower heart rate (more efficient: ↑ SV costs less myocardial work than ↑ HR). Plasma-volume rise reflects ↑ albumin (translocation + hepatic synthesis pulling fluid in) and also improves heat transport to skin.
- **O₂ diffusion into muscle** (a smaller fraction of the gain): a systemic analog of Fick's law for alveolar diffusion. Trained muscle has ~**60% more capillaries per cross-sectional area** than sedentary muscle → greater diffusion surface area (↑ D_O₂), shorter capillary-to-fiber diffusion distance, more capillary length/volume (longer RBC transit time → more complete extraction), and higher capillary PO₂ (from greater flow) sustaining the diffusion driving force.
- **Mitochondrial biogenesis**: mitochondrial O₂-consumption capacity already exceeds cardiovascular supply, so mitochondrial content does **not** limit VO₂max — but endurance training nearly **doubles** muscle mitochondrial content, raising the capacity to oxidize fat (glycogen sparing; the marathon/ultramarathon phenotype) and creating a larger O₂ sink that promotes extraction. The stimulus is repeated fiber activation raising time-averaged [Ca²⁺]ᵢ, acting (1) directly on nuclear-gene transcription and (2) via ↑[AMP] → **AMP kinase (AMPK)** → transcription. Newly made transcription factors (e.g., **TFAM**) enter mitochondria to drive transcription/translation of electron-transport-chain genes; nuclear-encoded proteins are imported by the mitochondrial import machinery into multisubunit complexes.

### Equations
- **VO₂max determinant expansion** (Eq. 60-9):
  $$\dot V_{aO_2,\text{max}} = \text{HR}_{opt} \cdot \text{SV}_{opt} \cdot (C_{aO_2} - C_{\bar v O_2})_{\text{max}}$$
  Maximal O₂ uptake = (optimal HR × optimal SV = optimal maximal cardiac output) × maximal a–v O₂-content difference. Training acts almost entirely through SV_opt (→ cardiac output), with a smaller extraction term.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "It is during the recovery period that the adaptations to exercise actually occur." (p. 1219)
- > "[hemoglobin] tends to be slightly lower in endurance athletes, a phenomenon called sports anemia, which reflects an expansion of the plasma compartment." (p. 1220)
- > "Because aerobic training does not increase maximal heart rate and has a relatively small effect on O₂ extraction, nearly all the increase in VO₂max that occurs with training must be the result of an increase in maximal cardiac output." (p. 1220)
- > "Well-conditioned individuals have a 60% greater number of capillaries per cross-sectional area of muscle than do sedentary people." (p. 1221)
- > "Endurance training can also increase the mitochondrial content of skeletal muscle fibers nearly 2-fold by stimulating the synthesis of mitochondrial enzymes and other proteins." (p. 1221)

## Glossary

- **Motor unit** — one motor neuron + all fibers it innervates; functional unit of contraction.
- **Innervation ratio** — fibers per motor neuron; small in fine-control muscles.
- **Size principle** — recruitment smallest (type I) → largest (type IIx); set by motor-neuron input resistance.
- **Rate coding** — grading force by firing frequency once recruitment is maximal.
- **Type I / IIa / IIx fibers** — slow-oxidative / fast-oxidative-glycolytic / fast-glycolytic.
- **Series elastic elements (SEE)** — tendon + connective tissue spring; carry passive tension (stretch–shorten cycle).
- **Concentric / isometric / eccentric** — shortening (positive work) / fixed-length / lengthening (negative work, brake).
- **Moment arm** — perpendicular distance from line of force to joint center; torque = force × moment arm.
- **Phosphagens** — ATP + PCr; immediate energy supply.
- **Creatine kinase / adenylate kinase (myokinase)** — ATP-buffering reactions.
- **Anaerobic (nonoxidative) glycolysis** — glycogen → lactate, 2 ATP/glucose, self-limiting via H⁺.
- **Lactate shuttle / Cori cycle / glucose–alanine cycle** — interorgan lactate and gluconeogenic loops.
- **Lactate (anaerobic) threshold** — ~65% VO₂max; production outstrips clearance → exponential blood [lactate].
- **Crossover concept** — shift from fat to carbohydrate as intensity rises.
- **VO₂ / VO₂max** — O₂ consumption / its plateau; objective index of aerobic capacity.
- **Oxygen deficit / EPOC (oxygen debt)** — early shortfall covered by phosphagens + glycolysis; post-exercise oxidative recovery of those stores and lactate (see oxidative-metabolism narrative, p. 1210).
- **Fick principle** — VO₂ = blood flow × (a–v) O₂ difference; whole-body: VO₂max = CO × (CaO₂ − CvO₂).
- **Convective O₂ delivery** — cardiac output × arterial O₂ content (Eq. 60-7).
- **Functional hyperemia / metabolic vasodilation / muscle pump** — local mechanisms raising active-muscle flow.
- **Central vs. peripheral fatigue** — CNS-level vs. fiber-level; high- vs. low-frequency fatigue.
- **Hitting the wall** — glycogen-depletion fatigue.
- **Effective circulating volume** — defended over skin flow during heat exercise; falls via fluid shift, sweat, cutaneous pooling.
- **Eccrine vs. apocrine sweat glands** — thermoregulatory vs. odor/pheromone.
- **ENaC / CFTR** — ductal Na⁺/Cl⁻ reabsorption; CF defect → salty sweat.
- **Sports anemia** — dilutional [Hb] fall from plasma-volume expansion in endurance athletes.
- **Mitochondrial biogenesis / AMPK / TFAM** — Ca²⁺- and AMP-driven training adaptation.
