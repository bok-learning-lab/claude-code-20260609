# Chapter 41 — Organization of the Gastrointestinal System

> Section VII · The Gastrointestinal System · pp. 852–862 · Author: Henry J. Binder

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Overview of digestive processes** (pp. 852–855) — the GI tract as a sequential-processing tube; wall structure (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, serosa); assimilation = digestion + absorption; the secreted enzymes; the three neurohumoral control modes (endocrine, neural, paracrine); excretion, fluid/electrolyte balance, and immunity (GALT).
2. **Regulation of GI function** (pp. 855–858) — the enteric nervous system ("minibrain") with its submucosal (Meissner) and myenteric (Auerbach) plexuses; ENS neurotransmitters (ACh, peptides, amines, NO); extrinsic autonomic modulation (vagal/pelvic parasympathetic, sympathetic); the bidirectional brain–gut axis and the immune system; GI peptide-hormone table.
3. **GI motility** (pp. 858–862) — slow waves and action potentials set smooth-muscle Vₘ; tonic vs. phasic contraction; the six sphincters; swallowing/peristalsis; small-intestinal churning vs. propulsion; the migrating motor complex (MMC) vs. the fed pattern; large-intestinal segmentation, mass peristalsis, and reservoir function.

## Section 1 — Overview of digestive processes (pp. 852–855)

### Subsection headings
- **The gastrointestinal tract is a tube that is specialized along its length for the sequential processing of food** (pp. 852–853)
- **Assimilation of dietary food substances requires digestion as well as absorption** (pp. 852–854)
- **Digestion requires enzymes secreted in the mouth, stomach, pancreas, and small intestine** (p. 854)
- **Ingestion of food initiates multiple endocrine, neural, and paracrine responses** (pp. 854–855)
- **In addition to its function in nutrition, the GI tract plays important roles in excretion, fluid and electrolyte balance, and immunity** (p. 855)

### Core claims
- **The GI tract is a tube from mouth to anus with accessory glands** (salivary glands, pancreas, liver/gallbladder) adding secretions (Fig. 41-1). Hollow organs are separated by sphincters and specialized along their length: mouth/oropharynx (chop, lubricate, begin carbohydrate + fat digestion); esophagus (conduit); stomach (storage, churning, proteases + acid); small intestine (completes digestion, primary absorption site); large intestine (reabsorbs fluid/electrolytes, stores feces).
- **Wall structure, generic cross-section (Fig. 41-2), inside → outside**:
  - **Mucosa** = epithelial layer + lamina propria (loose connective tissue with capillaries, enteric neurons, immune cells e.g. mast cells) + lamina muscularis mucosae (thin smooth-muscle layer). Surface area amplified by microvilli (apical), villi (evaginations) and crypts/glands (invaginations), and large folds.
  - **Submucosa** = loose connective tissue + larger blood vessels; may contain secretory glands.
  - **Muscularis externa** = inner **circular** + outer **longitudinal** smooth-muscle layers; the myenteric (enteric) neurons lie between them.
  - **Serosa** = enveloping connective-tissue layer covered with squamous epithelium.
- **Assimilation = digestion + absorption.** The sedentary body needs ~30 kcal/kg/day. Caloric uptake essentially occurs only via the GI tract (parenteral nutrition can substitute clinically). Nutrients are usually NOT ingested in absorbable form: triacylglycerols → fatty acids + monoacylglycerols; proteins/large peptides → amino acids + di/tripeptides; starch/disaccharides → monosaccharides. Solids >2 mm do not pass the pylorus.
- **Where the enzymes come from**: mouth (salivary/lingual amylase for carbohydrate, lingual lipase for fat); stomach (pepsins for protein; swallowed lingual lipase + some gastric lipase for fat; no gastric carbohydrate enzyme); small intestine completed by pancreatic enzymes (lipase, chymotrypsin, amylase) + brush-border enzymes (disaccharidases, dipeptidases) — the latter constitute **membrane digestion**.
- **Fluid load**: dietary fluid intake 1.5–2.5 L/day, but the load presented to the small intestine is **8–9 L/day** because of salivary, gastric, biliary, pancreatic, and small-intestinal secretions (~1 L/day secreted by the intestine itself). Almost all is reabsorbed; stool water ≈ 0.1 L/day. Diarrhea (>200 g/day) = increased secretion or decreased reabsorption (e.g., cholera).
- **Three neurohumoral control modes** (all have sensor + transmitter steps):
  - **Endocrine** — transmitter (peptide) released into blood. Example: protein in stomach → gastrin from antral **G cells** → blood → H⁺ secretion by parietal cells.
  - **Neural** — nerves/neurotransmitters acting via the ENS and/or CNS. Example: smell of food → vagal activation → ACh → parietal-cell H⁺ release.
  - **Paracrine** — transmitter released from a sensor cell acts on adjacent cells without entering blood or activating neurons. Example: histamine from **enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells** stimulates neighboring parietal cells.
- **Non-nutritive roles**: excretion (heavy metals Fe/Cu mainly via bile; organic anions/cations and drugs in bile); fluid/electrolyte balance; immunity via **GALT** (gut-associated lymphoid tissue) — organized aggregates (Peyer's patches) + diffuse immune cells. ~80% of immunoglobulin-producing cells are in the small intestine. Non-immunological defenses: gastric acid, mucin, peristalsis, the epithelial permeability barrier (impaired peristalsis → bacterial overgrowth, "blind loop"/stagnant bowel syndrome).

## Section 2 — Regulation of gastrointestinal function (pp. 855–858)

### Subsection headings
- **The ENS is a "minibrain" with sensory neurons, interneurons, and motor neurons** (pp. 855–856)
- **ACh, peptides, and bioactive amines are the ENS neurotransmitters that regulate epithelial and motor function** (pp. 856–857)
- **The brain–gut axis is a bidirectional system that controls GI function via the ANS, GI hormones, and the immune system** (pp. 856–858)

### Core claims
- **The enteric nervous system (ENS)** is the primary neural controller of GI function and is one of the three divisions of the autonomic nervous system (with sympathetic and parasympathetic). It contains **~100 million neurons** — roughly the number in the spinal cord — hence the "minibrain"/"little brain" concept. It resides entirely within GI tissue but is modifiable by the brain.
- **Two ganglionated plexuses** (Fig. 41-3A):
  - **Submucosal (Meissner's) plexus** — in the submucosa; present in small and large intestine; controls secretion/absorption and local blood flow.
  - **Myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus** — between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers; runs the whole length (proximal esophagus to rectum); controls motility.
  - Three additional, non-ganglionated plexuses exist: mucosal, deep muscular, and tertiary.
- **The ENS is a complete reflex circuit** that can operate without the spinal cord or brain. It has **sensory (afferent) neurons** (sensing distention/smooth-muscle tension, luminal chemistry — pH, osmolality, nutrients — and mechanical stimuli) → **interneurons** → **secretomotor (efferent) neurons** that drive smooth muscle, secretory/absorptive epithelium, submucosal vessels, and enteric endocrine cells.
- **Preprogrammed responses**: because diverse stimuli (e.g., jejunal distention vs. a bacterial enterotoxin) can elicit identical output (profuse fluid/electrolyte secretion + propulsive contractions), the ENS is thought to hold multiple preprogrammed reflex programs.
- **ENS neurotransmitters**: **ACh** is the primary pre- and postganglionic transmitter for both secretion and smooth-muscle activity. Many others: **VIP** (inhibits intestinal smooth muscle, stimulates fluid/electrolyte secretion — also a brain neurotransmitter); enkephalins, somatostatin, substance P; amines (serotonin); and **nitric oxide (NO)**. More than one transmitter can occupy a single neuron.
- **Excitatory vs. inhibitory motor neurons**: **ACh** is the predominant **excitatory** motor neurotransmitter; **VIP and NO** are the **inhibitory** motor neurotransmitters (load-bearing for sphincter relaxation, e.g. LES; their loss underlies achalasia).
- **Extrinsic autonomic modulation** (Fig. 41-3B):
  - **Parasympathetic** — vagus nerve from pharynx to distal colon; **pelvic nerves** (sacral cord) for the distal third of the colon. Preganglionic fibers use ACh and synapse on ENS neurons, which thereby serve as the postganglionic parasympathetic fibers (the ENS is "in a sense the parasympathetic ganglion"). Parasympathetic stimulation **increases secretion and motility**. The vagus also carries afferents to medullary autonomic centers → the loop is a **vagovagal reflex**.
  - **Sympathetic** — preganglionic fibers synapse (ACh) on postganglionic neurons in prevertebral ganglia; postganglionic sympathetic fibers either synapse in the ENS or directly innervate effectors. Net sympathetic effect is generally inhibitory to motility/secretion and vasoconstrictive (fight-or-flight reduces GI blood flow).
- **The brain–gut axis is bidirectional** with three parallel limbs — the **ENS**, **GI hormones**, and the **immune system** — giving redundancy/"fail-safe" control. Examples of CNS → gut: sight/smell of food increases gastric acid; fight-or-flight reduces GI flow. Examples of gut → CNS: **CCK** mediates satiety; **gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP)** injected into brain ventricles inhibits gastric acid. Neuroimmune signaling is mediated largely by **mast cells** in the lamina propria (release histamine acting on smooth muscle and epithelium).

- > "The submucosal (or Meissner's) plexus is found in the submucosa only in the small and large intestine. The myenteric (or Auerbach's) plexus is located between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers throughout the GI tract from the proximal end of the esophagus to the rectum." (p. 855)

## Section 3 — Gastrointestinal motility (pp. 858–862)

### Subsection headings
- **Tonic and rhythmic contractions of smooth muscle are responsible for churning, peristalsis, and reservoir action** (pp. 858–859)
- **Segments of the GI tract have both longitudinal and circular arrays of muscles and are separated by sphincters that consist of specialized circular muscles** (pp. 858–859)
- **Location of a sphincter determines its function** (pp. 859–860)
- **Motility of the small intestine achieves both churning and propulsive movement, and its temporal pattern differs in the fed and fasted states** (pp. 860–862)
- **Motility of the large intestine achieves both propulsive movement and a reservoir function** (p. 862)

### Core claims
- **Three motor functions**: (1) **segmental (nonpropulsive) contractions** → mixing/churning that enhances digestion and absorption; (2) **peristalsis** = a progressive wave of relaxation followed by contraction → caudad **propulsion**; (3) **reservoir function** (stomach, large intestine) held by sphincters.
- **Electrical basis** — the smooth-muscle membrane voltage (Vₘ) either:
  - **oscillates subthreshold** at a few cycles/minute = **slow-wave activity** (the basal electrical rhythm), or
  - **reaches threshold** → a true action potential.
  The integrated effect of slow waves + action potentials sets contractile activity. Slow waves arise as voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels depolarize the cell and raise [Ca²⁺]ᵢ, followed by Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ channels repolarizing it. *(The chapter does not name the interstitial cells of Cajal here, but these slow-wave pacemaker cells are the structural basis of the basal rhythm; the chapter localizes slow-wave/pacemaker behavior to the smooth-muscle syncytium.)*
- **Contraction is graded by [Ca²⁺]ᵢ** (smooth-muscle E-C coupling): agonists raise [Ca²⁺]ᵢ either by (1) G-protein–linked receptors → IP₃ → Ca²⁺ release from stores, or (2) opening/closing plasma-membrane Ca²⁺ channels. Excitatory (ACh) and inhibitory (VIP, NO) transmitters tune the frequency with which Vₘ crosses threshold, hence contractility. **Tonic** (sustained) contractions characterize sphincters; **phasic/rhythmic** (alternating contraction–relaxation) contractions characterize the propulsive organs.
- **Muscle layers are almost entirely smooth muscle.** Striated-muscle exceptions: the **UES**, the **upper third of the esophagus**, and the **external anal sphincter**. Inner circular + outer longitudinal arrangement throughout; myenteric ganglia between them.
- **Six sphincters** (Fig. 41-1), each with its own resting pressure and stimulus response; a seventh, the **sphincter of Oddi**, gates the common bile duct into the duodenum. General rule: **stimuli proximal to a sphincter cause relaxation; stimuli distal cause contraction.**
  - **UES** — striated muscle, highest resting pressure of all GI sphincters; swallowing center in the medulla via CN V, IX, X, XII; closed during inspiration (diverts air to the glottis), relaxes during swallowing.
  - **LES** — specialized smooth muscle; permits coordinated entry of food into the stomach and prevents reflux (low/poorly relaxing tone → GERD / achalasia). Relaxation mediated by the vagus and by intrinsic **VIP** and **NO**. Relaxes only after the UES has returned to resting pressure (~3 s after deglutition starts).
  - **Pyloric sphincter** — short, a relatively poor barrier; gastric emptying is governed by the integrated **antroduodenal cluster unit** (antrum + pylorus + duodenum) rather than pyloric pressure alone.
  - **Ileocecal sphincter** — positive resting pressure; ileal distention relaxes it, proximal-colon distention contracts it; under vagal, sympathetic, and ENS control.
  - **Internal anal sphincter** — smooth muscle, involuntary; supplies most of the resting anal tone; relaxes on rectal distention (**rectosphincteric reflex**).
  - **External anal sphincter** — striated muscle, under voluntary + involuntary control; contracts reflexly (sacral cord) when defecation is not desired.
- **Small-intestinal motility**: two classes — **churning** (segmental, nonpropulsive; mixes content with secretions, thins the unstirred water layer to aid absorption) and **propulsion** (propagated peristalsis: circular-muscle contraction + longitudinal relaxation upstream, the reverse downstream). The pattern differs by feeding state:
  - **Fasting** → the **migrating motor complex (MMC)**, a cyclic interdigestive pattern recurring every **90–120 min** in four phases (quiescence → rising activity → peak → declining). It is the "housekeeper" that sweeps residue, bacteria, and particles **>2 mm** out of the stomach/small intestine. A major determinant is the hormone **motilin** (a 22-amino-acid peptide made in duodenal mucosa, released just before phase 3). MMCs usually start in the stomach and travel to the ileum (~25% start in the duodenum/proximal jejunum).
  - **Feeding** terminates the MMC and initiates the **fed pattern** (segmental churning + peristalsis); induced by vagal activity (sham feeding triggers it) and caloric content/type (fat > protein). Motilin has no role in the fed pattern.
- **Large-intestinal motility**: the colon acts as two organs — proximal (ascending/transverse: fluid/electrolyte absorption + bacterial fermentation of short-chain fatty acids) and distal (descending/rectosigmoid: final desiccation, storage, reservoir). No distinct fasting/fed patterns. Proximal colon shows **nonpropulsive segmentation** (slow-wave–driven, churns content orad, produces **haustra**) and **mass peristalsis** (1–3×/day, propels content ≥20 cm; haustra disappear then reappear; may be triggered by eating). Distal colon mainly segments for desiccation/storage; rectal filling by mass peristalsis triggers the anal-sphincter reflexes leading to defecation.

### Equations

The chapter is organizational and states relations qualitatively. The load-bearing relation is the Vₘ-to-contraction logic of GI smooth muscle:

$$\text{slow waves (subthreshold } V_m \text{ oscillation, few cycles/min)} \;+\; \text{action potentials (when } V_m \ge V_{\text{threshold}}) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{contractile activity}$$

with contraction graded by intracellular calcium:

$$\text{tension} \propto [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i, \qquad [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i \uparrow \;\text{via}\; \begin{cases} \text{GPCR} \to \mathrm{IP_3} \to \text{Ca}^{2+}\text{ release from stores} \\ \text{plasma-membrane Ca}^{2+}\text{ channel gating} \end{cases}$$

(Quantitative smooth-muscle E-C coupling is developed on pp. 243–249; gas/transport conversion constants belong to other chapters.)

#### Figure 41-5 — Pressure changes initiated by rectal distention *(viewed)*

Three-panel manometry figure defining the defecation reflexes.
- **Panel A — rectum.** When passive (e.g., balloon) distention of the rectum is sufficiently large, it triggers an **active contraction** of the rectal smooth muscle (rectal pressure rises above the passive curve).
- **Panel B — internal anal sphincter.** The same passive rectal distention triggers **relaxation** of the smooth-muscle internal anal sphincter (pressure falls below baseline) — the **rectosphincteric reflex**.

### The four GI-signaling families (synthesis from text)

| Family | Definition (route) | Representative agents named in chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Endocrine** | Released into the blood by enteroendocrine cells | gastrin (G cells), CCK (I cells), secretin (S cells), GIP (K cells), motilin, neurotensin, PYY |
| **Paracrine** | Released locally onto adjacent cells; no blood, no neuron | somatostatin (D cells), histamine (ECL cells / mast cells) |

> The **incretin concept**: GIP from duodenal/jejunal K cells potentiates insulin release in response to an oral (vs. intravenous) glucose load — oral glucose evokes a larger insulin response, the "incretin effect."

## Glossary

- **Mucosa / submucosa / muscularis externa / serosa** — the four wall layers (Fig. 41-2).
- **Lamina propria; muscularis mucosae** — connective-tissue + thin smooth-muscle sublayers of the mucosa.
- **Villi / crypts (of Lieberkühn) / microvilli** — surface-area amplifiers; brush border carries membrane-digestion enzymes.
- **Membrane digestion** — final digestion by brush-border disaccharidases/dipeptidases.
- **Enteric nervous system (ENS)** — ~100 million neurons; the GI "minibrain."
- **Submucosal (Meissner's) plexus** — in submucosa; secretion/absorption; small + large intestine.
- **Myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus** — between circular and longitudinal muscle; motility; whole length.
- **Vagovagal reflex** — vagal afferent → medulla → vagal efferent loop.
- **Brain–gut axis** — bidirectional ENS + hormones + immune system.
- **GALT** — gut-associated lymphoid tissue (Peyer's patches, diffuse immune cells).
- **Slow wave** — subthreshold Vₘ oscillation; basal electrical rhythm (pacemaker by interstitial cells of Cajal).
- **Tonic vs. phasic contraction** — sustained (sphincters) vs. rhythmic (propulsive organs).
- **Tonic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ control** — IP₃-mediated store release + plasma-membrane Ca²⁺ channels.
- **Six sphincters** — UES, LES, pyloric, ileocecal, internal anal, external anal (+ sphincter of Oddi).
- **Antroduodenal cluster unit** — integrated antrum-pylorus-duodenum controlling gastric emptying.
- **Rectosphincteric reflex** — rectal distention relaxes the internal anal sphincter.
- **Migrating motor complex (MMC)** — fasting "housekeeper" pattern; ~90–120 min cycle; motilin-driven.
- **Fed pattern** — segmentation + peristalsis after a meal; vagally induced.
- **Mass peristalsis** — colonic propulsion ≥20 cm, 1–3×/day; haustra disappear.
- **Endocrine / paracrine / neurocrine / incretin** — the four GI-signaling families.
- **Gastrin, CCK, secretin, GIP, motilin** — major GI endocrine hormones.
- **Somatostatin, histamine** — major GI paracrine agents.
- **VIP, ACh, GRP, NPY, NO** — major GI neurocrine transmitters.
