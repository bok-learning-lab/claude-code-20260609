# Chapter 19 — Arteries and Veins

> Section IV · The Cardiovascular System · pp. 447–460 · Author: Emile L. Boulpaep
> Second chapter of Section IV. Develops the **container** side of the hemodynamic vocabulary set up in Ch 17: how the geometry of the arborized vascular tree, the elastic properties of vessel walls, and the active tension of vascular smooth muscle convert a constant-pressure-head pump (Ch 22) into the actual pressure and flow profiles seen along the systemic and pulmonary circuits.

## Section 1 — Arterial distribution and venous collection systems (pp. 447–452)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Physical properties of vessels closely follow the level of branching in the circuit** (pp. 447–449)
- **Most of the blood volume resides in the systemic veins** (pp. 448–449)
- **The intravascular pressures along the systemic circuit are higher than those along the pulmonary circuit** (pp. 449–451)
- **Under normal conditions, the steepest pressure drop in the systemic circulation occurs in arterioles, the site of greatest vascular resistance** (p. 451)
- **Local intravascular pressure depends on the distribution of vascular resistance** (pp. 451–452)

### Core claims

- **Architecture.** The aorta branches into billions of capillaries that ultimately regroup into a single vena cava. Ten parameters vary along the level of arborization: (1) number of vessels, (2) radius, (3) aggregate cross-sectional area, (4) mean linear velocity, (5) single-vessel flow, (6) relative blood volume, (7) transit (circulation) time, (8) pressure profile, (9) wall structure, (10) wall elasticity. Parameters 1–5 are developed in this section; 9–10 in §2.
- **Bifurcation rule.** At every branch point, the **combined cross-sectional area of daughter vessels exceeds the area of the parent vessel.** The steepest rise in aggregate cross-sectional area occurs in the microcirculation. The peak aggregate area in humans occurs not at the capillaries but at the **postcapillary (fourth-order) venules** — up to ~1000-fold the aortic cross section (assuming only ~25% of capillaries open at rest).
- **Velocity mirrors area** by continuity (Eq. 19-1). $\bar v$ is maximal in the aorta (~20–50 cm/s) and minimal in the postcapillary venules (~0.03 cm/s). The vena cava has ~50% larger area than the aorta and ~1/3 lower velocity (~14 cm/s).
- **Single-vessel flow** spans ~10 orders of magnitude — from ~83 mL/s in the aorta (= cardiac output) down to ~8 × 10⁻⁸ mL/s in a single open capillary; closed capillaries have zero flow.
- **Volume distribution** (Table 19-2, 70-kg woman): systemic circulation ~85%, pulmonary ~10%, heart chambers ~5%. Of the systemic 85%, ~3/4 (≈ 65% of total blood volume) sits on the **venous side**, mostly in **small veins** (~46%). Four ways to group blood volumes:
  1. By circuit: systemic / pulmonary / heart.
  2. By pressure: **high-pressure system** (~15%) / **low-pressure system** (~80%) / heart chambers (~5%).
  3. Systemic venous (~65%) vs. the rest.
  4. **Central blood volume** (heart chambers + pulmonary circulation, ~15%) vs. the rest — the filling reservoir for the left heart, dysregulated in left-sided heart failure.
- **Transit time** (Eq. 19-2): $t = V/F$. Total circulation time ≈ 1 min; a single bed (e.g., coronary) can be as short as 10 s. Measured historically by injecting ether into an antecubital vein and timing perception in the lung (4–8 s) or a bitter/sweet substance and timing perception on the tongue (10–18 s). Has little physiological significance because it conflates volume and flow.
- **Two pressure circuits.** Pressures along the systemic circuit are far higher than along the pulmonary circuit because, although cardiac outputs are equal in the steady state, the **systemic resistance is far higher than the pulmonary resistance**. Systemic upstream driving pressure ~95 mm Hg; pulmonary ~15 mm Hg. The **high-pressure system** extends from contracting LV through the systemic arterioles; the **low-pressure system** extends from the systemic capillaries through the venous return, right heart, pulmonary circuit, and back to the relaxed LV. Pulmonary mean arterial pressures normally do not exceed 15 mm Hg; pulmonary capillary pressure does not rise above 10 mm Hg.
- **Site of greatest resistance.** Although a single capillary's resistance exceeds that of a single arteriole, capillaries vastly outnumber arterioles in parallel (Table 19-4: $R_{\text{total, arterioles}} \approx 15$ vs. $R_{\text{total, capillaries}} \approx 3$ dyne·s/cm⁵). So **arterioles**, not capillaries, contribute the largest aggregate resistance and host the steepest $\Delta P / \Delta x$ — pressure drops from ~95 mm Hg upstream of arterioles to ~25 mm Hg at the midcapillary.
- **Capillary pressure depends on the pre/post resistance ratio** (Eq. 19-3, Fig. 19-4):

$$P_c = \frac{(R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}}) \cdot P_a + P_v}{1 + (R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}})}$$

  - Three implications:
    1. $P_c$ equals the arithmetic mean of $P_a$ and $P_v$ only if $R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}} = 1$. Normally $R_{\text{pre}} > R_{\text{post}}$ (ratio 0.2–0.4) so $P_c \approx 25$ mm Hg, far closer to $P_v$ than to $P_a$.
    2. As long as $R_{\text{pre}} + R_{\text{post}}$ is held fixed, raising $R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}}$ pushes $P_c$ toward $P_a$ (Fig. 19-4B); lowering it pushes $P_c$ toward $P_v$ (Fig. 19-4C).
    3. Depending on the ratio, $P_c$ may be more sensitive to changes in arteriolar or in venular pressure. When $R_{\text{pre}} \gg R_{\text{post}}$, $P_c$ tracks venular pressure — the clinical explanation for **ankle edema with standing** (elevated leg-vein pressure → elevated $P_c$ → transudation into the interstitium, see pp. 467–468) and for the reversal of edema by foot elevation.
- **Effect of vasomotion** (Fig. 19-5): assuming the overall $\Delta P$ between aorta and vena cava is maintained by the pump, arteriolar constriction steepens and concentrates the pressure drop at the arteriolar segment; arteriolar dilation flattens and spreads it. Upstream arterial pressure rises modestly and downstream capillary/venous pressure falls (and vice versa) — the local pressure profile mirrors the local resistance profile.

### Equations

- **Continuity along the arborization** (Eq. 19-1):

$$F_{\text{total}} = A_1 \bar v_1 = A_2 \bar v_2 = A_3 \bar v_3 = \cdots$$

  Aggregate cross-sectional area × mean linear velocity is constant at every level of branching (mass conservation).

- **Transit / circulation time** (Eq. 19-2):

$$t = \frac{V}{F} \quad [\text{s} = \text{mL} / (\text{mL/s})]$$

- **Capillary pressure from upstream/downstream resistances** (Eq. 19-3):

$$P_c = \frac{(R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}}) \, P_a + P_v}{1 + (R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}})}$$

## Section 2 — Elastic properties of blood vessels (pp. 452–460)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Blood vessels are elastic tubes** (pp. 452–453)
- **Because of the elastic properties of vessels, the pressure-flow relationship of passive vascular beds is nonlinear** (p. 453)
- **Contraction of smooth muscle halts blood flow when driving pressure falls below the critical closing pressure** (p. 454)
- **Elastic and collagen fibers determine the distensibility and compliance of vessels** (p. 454)
- **Differences in compliance cause arteries to act as resistors and veins to act as capacitors** (p. 455)
- **Laplace's law describes how tension in the vessel wall increases with transmural pressure** (pp. 455–456)
- **The vascular wall is adapted to withstand wall tension, not transmural pressure** (pp. 456–458)
- **Elastin and collagen separately contribute to the wall tension of vessels** (p. 458)
- **Aging reduces the distensibility of arteries** (pp. 458–459)
- **Active tension from smooth-muscle activity adds to the elastic tension of vessels** (p. 459)
- **Elastic tension helps stabilize vessels under vasomotor control** (pp. 459–460)

### Core claims

#### Vessel wall building blocks
- All vessels have three layers — **intima, media, adventitia** — except capillaries (intima only: endothelium + basal lamina).
- **Four building blocks** of the wall: **endothelial cells, elastic fibers, collagen fibers, vascular smooth-muscle cells (VSMCs)**. Their relative abundance varies systematically along the tree (Fig. 19-6, Table 19-6).
- **Endothelial cells** form a single continuous lining; tight in arteries, less so in veins; organ-specific in capillaries (Ch 20). **Glomus bodies** (skin and elsewhere) host multilayer myoepithelioid "endothelium" that controls arteriovenous shunts (Ch 24, p. 570).
- **Elastic fibers** are a rubber-like material accounting for most of the stretch at normal pressures. Core = highly cross-linked **elastin** (insoluble polymer rich in glycine, alanine, valine, proline; can stretch > 100% under physiological conditions) wrapped by ~10-nm-diameter microfibrils. Arranged as concentric cylindrical lamellae in arteries. Absent in true capillaries, venules, and arteriovenous anastomoses.
- **Collagen fibers** are far stiffer (type I + type III) and can be stretched only 3–4%. Normally hung with **slack** so that they engage only after the elastic fibers have already taken up the easy stretch.
- **VSMCs** are present in all segments except capillaries. Arranged as spiral lamellae in elastic arteries and as rings/low-pitch helices in muscular arteries. Relaxed VSMCs contribute little to the elastic tension; their contribution is via **active contraction** (pp. 238–240, 459).

#### Nonlinear pressure–flow relationship
- Real vascular beds depart from Poiseuille's linear $F \propto \Delta P$ (Fig. 19-7A, broken line). The in vivo curve (red) starts at a **foot** of ~6 mm Hg and bends upward: increasing driving pressure raises transmural pressure → vessel distends → radius increases → resistance falls ($R \propto 1/r^4$) → flow rises super-linearly.
- Conversely, resistance vs. driving pressure (Fig. 19-7B) decays as pressure rises; resistance diverges as pressure falls toward the critical closing pressure.
- **Critical closing pressure.** Blood flow stops completely when driving pressure falls below ~6 mm Hg even though a finite pressure difference still exists — the combined effect of elastic recoil and active VSMC tension. **Sympathetic stimulation** shifts the $P$–$F$ curve to the right and decreases its slope (Fig. 19-7A, blue and green curves). In **hypotensive shock**, vasoconstrictor reflexes raise the critical closing pressure to 40+ mm Hg → blood flow can stop in a limb despite a measurable arterial–venous pressure difference.

#### Distensibility, compliance, and the resistor/capacitor partition
- **Absolute distensibility** $= \Delta V / \Delta P$. **Normalized distensibility** (Eq. 19-4):

$$\text{normalized distensibility} = \frac{(\Delta V / V_0)}{\Delta P}$$

- **Compliance** (Eq. 19-5):

$$C = \frac{dV}{dP}$$

  the slope of the tangent at a point on the $P$–$V$ diagram. For nonlinear $P$–$V$ curves $C$ varies along the curve, so every compliance reading should be reported with the transmural pressure (or volume) at which it was measured. Same algebra reappears in airway/lung compliance (p. 610).
- **Aortic compliance is high.** Raising transmural pressure from 0 to 100 mm Hg increases relative aortic volume by ~180%; another 100 mm Hg adds ~100% more. Muscular arteries (e.g., femoral) are less compliant: ~10% radius change over a cardiac cycle. Because their radius changes only modestly, **arterial resistance does not fall much** under cardiac-cycle pressure swings → muscular arteries are **resistance vessels**.
- **Venous compliance is extremely high in the low-pressure range.** A relaxed vein gains ~200% volume between 0 and 10 mm Hg. This is **geometric**, not elastic: below ~6–9 mm Hg the vein's cross section is ellipsoidal, and a small pressure rise rounds it out without stretching the perimeter. Above ~10 mm Hg the *true* venous wall compliance is poor (Fig. 19-8B flat tail). Because veins accept large $\Delta V$ with little $\Delta P$ in their normal range, they are **capacitance vessels** holding ~65% of total blood volume.
- **Saphenous-to-coronary bypass graft** as a stress test: when a vein segment is suddenly exposed to *arterial* pressures (80–120 mm Hg) — outside its compliant range — it shows a much larger $\Delta P$ for a given $\Delta V$ than a same-size artery would. Long-term implication: graft-wall remodeling.
- During the cardiac cycle, vessel **volume changes are due to radius changes**, not length: the thoracic aorta lengthens by only ~1% during systole.

#### Hooke's law, stress–strain, and the Young modulus
- **Hooke's law** for a linearly elastic spring (Eq. 19-6):

$$F = k \cdot \Delta L$$

- **Stress** $\sigma = F/A$; **strain** $\varepsilon = \Delta L / L_0$. Hooke's law in stress–strain form (Eq. 19-7):

$$\sigma = Y \cdot \varepsilon \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \frac{F}{A} = Y \cdot \frac{L - L_0}{L_0}$$

  with **Young (elastic) modulus** $Y$ the slope of the stress–strain diagram. Stiffer material → larger $Y$. **Table 19-5** values:

| Material | Elastic modulus (dyne/cm²) |
|---|---|
| Smooth muscle | $10^4$–$10^6$ |
| Elastin | $4 \times 10^6$ |
| Rubber | $4 \times 10^7$ |
| Collagen | $1 \times 10^9$ |
| Wood | $10^{10}$–$10^{11}$ |

  ( $1$ mm Hg $= 1334$ dyne/cm². **Collagen is >1000× stiffer than elastin.**)
- Vessel wall deformation in principle involves three axes — circumferential ($\theta$), axial ($x$), radial ($r$) — but axial elongation (~1%) and wall thinning are small, so circumferential stretch dominates.

#### Laplace's law
- The transmural pressure $\Delta P$ distends the wall; the wall tension $T$ holds it together. The equilibrium for a thin-walled cylinder (Eq. 19-8):

$$T = \Delta P \cdot r$$

  $T$ has units of dyne/cm (force per unit length along the cylinder axis); using $T$ rather than stress assumes constant wall thickness. **For a given $\Delta P$, wall tension grows with radius** — the dilated vessel needs ever more wall tension to stay together (the geometric content of aneurysm propagation).
- **The wall is adapted to wall tension, not pressure.** Table 19-6 shows the empirical correlation between segment-by-segment wall tension and elastic-tissue content; transmural pressure correlates poorly with elastic content. Compare aorta and capillary:

| Vessel | $\Delta P$ (mm Hg) | $r$ | $T$ (dyne/cm) | Elastic tissue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aorta | 95 | 1.13 cm | 140 000 | +++ |
| Small arteries | 90 | 0.5 cm | 60 000 | +++ |
| Arterioles | 60 | 15 µm | 1 200 | + |
| Capillaries | 25 | 3 µm | 10 | 0 |
| Venules | 15 | 10 µm | 20 | 0 |
| Veins | 12 | > 0.02 cm | 320 | + |
| Vena cava | 10 | 1.38 cm | 18 000 | ++ |

  A capillary at $\Delta P = 25$ mm Hg has only $T = 10$ dyne/cm because $r$ is tiny → no elastic tissue is needed. The vena cava at $\Delta P = 10$ mm Hg has $T = 18{,}000$ dyne/cm because $r$ is large → substantial elastic tissue is needed.
- **Worked example for the aorta** (Fig. 19-12C): at $r = 6$ mm a stretched aorta wall develops $T \approx 120{,}000$ dyne/cm; Laplace's law gives the single pressure consistent with this combination: $\Delta P = T/r = 2 \times 10^5$ dyne/cm² $= 150$ mm Hg. Same construction for the vena cava: at $r \approx 6.7$ mm, $T \approx 12{,}000$ dyne/cm → $\Delta P \approx 1.8 \times 10^4$ dyne/cm² $\approx 13.5$ mm Hg.

#### Chemical dissection of elastin and collagen
- Selective digestion isolates the two contributions (Fig. 19-13). **Elastase** removes elastin → length–tension curve is steep, nearly linear (collagen alone, orange). **Formic acid** removes collagen → length–tension curve is shallow (elastin alone, violet).
- Intact vessels (red curve) start shallow (elastin-dominated at modest stretch) and steepen as progressively greater stretch **recruits** collagen fibers. The recruitment is the structural basis of the increasing slope of the radius–tension diagram (and the falling compliance at high pressures of the $P$–$V$ diagram).

#### Aging
- **Pressure–volume curves shift to higher volumes with age** (Fig. 19-14A) — vessels enlarge. Compliance rises during growth and falls in adulthood. Two mechanisms in older adults:
  1. **Arteriosclerotic** decrease in compliance per se.
  2. **Higher operating pressure** sets the older person on a flatter (lower-compliance) region of the curve.
- Consequence: the same stroke-volume injection produces a much **larger pulse pressure** in the elderly than in the young (the geometric basis of isolated systolic hypertension and wide pulse pressure with age).
- **Radius–tension diagrams stiffen with age** (Fig. 19-14B): higher maximal slope and earlier upturn because diffuse fibrosis, more collagen, and increased cross-linking (p. 1239) reduce collagen "slack" and recruit collagen at smaller stretches.

#### Active tension and stability
- VSMC stimulation can reduce the radius of a muscular feed artery by 20–50%; by $T = \Delta P \cdot r$, shortening to a smaller $r$ against fixed $\Delta P$ reduces the tension the muscle must hold.
- **Total tension = passive (elastic) + active (smooth-muscle) components.** Selective elimination (Fig. 19-15, KCN poison) leaves the passive curve (green); maximal norepinephrine stimulation gives the total (red); difference (blue) is the active VSMC length–tension diagram.
- **Mechanical stability** (Fig. 19-16):
  - **Elastic wall (Panel A):** at equilibrium $T = \Delta P \cdot r$. A pressure perturbation $+\Delta P$ produces $+\Delta r$, which automatically increases $T$ by $\Delta T$ — the new state still satisfies $T + \Delta T = (P + \Delta P)(r + \Delta r)$. **Stable** in both inflation and deflation. The passive radius–tension curve cannot have zero slope.
  - **Fixed active tension only (Panel B):** $T$ does not respond to $r$. A pressure perturbation makes $(P + \Delta P)(r + \Delta r) > T$ → **blowout**; a drop makes $(P - \Delta P)(r - \Delta r) < T$ → **collapse**. This instability characterizes arteriovenous anastomoses with poor elastic tissue and abundant myoepithelioid cells.
- **Clinical correlates of imbalance:**
  - **Aneurysms / varicosities:** elastic fibers reduced or damaged → vessels enlarge → if $r$ exceeds the Laplace-equilibrium value, blowout follows.
  - **Raynaud disease:** maximal VSMC stimulation in the digital arterioles + regressed passive elements → cold-induced vessel closure (active tension dominates the radius–tension diagram).

### Equations summary

- **Normalized distensibility** (Eq. 19-4): $(\Delta V / V_0)/\Delta P$.
- **Compliance** (Eq. 19-5): $C = dV/dP$.
- **Hooke's law** (Eq. 19-6): $F = k \, \Delta L$.
- **Stress–strain** (Eq. 19-7): $F/A = Y (L - L_0)/L_0$.
- **Laplace's law for a thin-walled cylinder** (Eq. 19-8): $T = \Delta P \cdot r$.
- **Stability around equilibrium** (Eq. 19-9): $T + \Delta T = (P + \Delta P)(r + \Delta r)$.

### Companion equations introduced or extended elsewhere

- **Pulse pressure**:

$$\text{PP} = P_{\text{systolic}} - P_{\text{diastolic}}$$

  In a normal young adult, ejection of one stroke volume ($\Delta V$) into the aorta produces $\text{PP} \approx 40$ mm Hg via $\Delta P \approx \Delta V / C$ (Ch 17, Ch 22).

- **Mean arterial pressure (recap, Ch 17)**:

$$\text{MAP} \approx \text{DAP} + \tfrac{1}{3} \text{PP}$$

- **Windkessel (two-element) approximation** of the arterial system as a single compliance $C_a$ in parallel with a single peripheral resistance $R_p$. During diastole the aortic pressure decays as

$$P(t) = P_0 \, e^{-t / (R_p C_a)}$$

  so the diastolic decay time constant is $\tau = R_p C_a$. The decline of $C_a$ with age (this chapter) therefore both **raises pulse pressure** (smaller $C_a$ in $\Delta P = \Delta V / C_a$) and **shortens the diastolic decay** (smaller $\tau$). Algebraic details deferred to Ch 22.

- **Moens–Korteweg pulse wave velocity** (referenced in the literature though not derived in the source text):

$$c_{\text{PWV}} = \sqrt{\frac{E \, h}{2 \, r \, \rho}}$$

  with $E$ Young modulus of the wall, $h$ wall thickness, $r$ vessel radius, $\rho$ blood density. The marked rise in $E$ with age (from collagen recruitment / cross-linking) raises pulse wave velocity and brings reflected waves back to the central aorta earlier in systole — a quantitative restatement of why aging widens central pulse pressure.

### Figures

#### Figure 19-6 — Structure of blood vessels *(viewed)*

A row of six longitudinal cross-sections illustrating wall composition from the aorta to a vein. Aorta ($r = 12$ mm, wall ~2 mm): thick wall packed with concentric elastic-fiber lamellae interleaved with sparse smooth muscle and adventitial collagen. Medium artery ($r = 2$ mm, wall ~1 mm): a thinner but well-organized media of smooth muscle between two prominent elastic laminae (internal/external) plus collagen. Arteriole ($r = 15$ µm, wall ~20 µm): single layer of endothelium ringed by 1–3 layers of smooth muscle, sparse elastic fibers. Precapillary sphincter ($r = 15$ µm, wall ~30 µm): endothelium + a small cuff of smooth muscle that gates capillary entry. True capillary ($r = 3$ µm, wall ~1 µm): endothelial tube only — no smooth muscle, no elastic fibers, no collagen — and a basal lamina. Venule ($r = 10$ µm, wall ~2 µm): endothelium + sparse smooth muscle and a few collagen fibers. Vein (right end, much larger but not to scale): a thin media of smooth muscle around endothelium, with prominent collagen fibers and modest elastic fibers in the adventitia.

> Vision note: Anchor for **Table 19-6**'s claim that elastic tissue tracks **wall tension** ($T = \Delta P \cdot r$), not transmural pressure. Capillaries (small $r$) carry $\Delta P = 25$ mm Hg with no elastic tissue at all; veins and the vena cava (large $r$, low $\Delta P$) need substantial elastic tissue.

#### Figure 19-7 — Nonlinear relationships among pressure, flow, and resistance *(viewed)*

**Panel A — Pressure–flow relationship.** $\Delta P$ (mm Hg, x-axis 0–100) vs. flow (mL/min, y-axis 0–0.8). The broken straight line is the Poiseuille rigid-tube prediction. The **red curve (no sympathetic stimulation)** begins at a foot of ~6 mm Hg (the critical closing pressure) and rises upward-concave: increasing $\Delta P$ distends the elastic vessel, drops resistance, and increases the slope. Two more curves (blue, moderate sympathetic; green, high sympathetic) shift to the right with progressively higher critical closing pressures (annotation "Critical closing pressure increases with amount of stimulation"). **Panel B — Pressure–resistance relationship.** Same $\Delta P$ axis vs. resistance (mm Hg / [mL/min], y-axis 0–400). The broken line is the Poiseuille constant. The **red curve** decays from ~400 at low pressures toward an asymptote ~100; the blue and green curves sit progressively higher. Both panels make the geometric point that an elastic tube behaves like Poiseuille only locally — over the full physiological range, vasomotor tone tunes both the y-intercept and the slope.

> Vision note: The figure links Section 1 (the steepest pressure drop is along arterioles) to Section 2 (the elastic-tube mechanism for it). It is the standard reference for clinical reasoning about hypotensive shock and the critical closing pressure.

#### Figure 19-8 — Compliance of blood vessels *(viewed)*

Two side-by-side $P$–$V$ diagrams with the same axes: transmural pressure (mm Hg, x-axis 0–250) vs. relative volume (%, y-axis 0–400; 100% = fully relaxed vessel volume). **Panel A — Aorta:** the curve climbs steeply from the origin, passes through ~280% at 100 mm Hg, then bends and continues climbing more shallowly toward ~400% at 250 mm Hg. The annotated tangent at any point has slope $\Delta V/\Delta P =$ compliance. **Panel B — Vena cava:** the curve rises *extremely* steeply between 0 and 10 mm Hg (the geometric "rounding" phase: ellipsoid → circle, perimeter constant), reaching ~300% by ~10 mm Hg; thereafter it abruptly flattens — the wall's *true* elastic compliance is poor. Annotated callouts: "At low transmural pressures, apparent compliance is extremely high, as the vein becomes fully rounded"; "At unphysiologically high transmural pressures, the compliance is quite low."

> Vision note: The "apparent vs. true compliance" annotation is the key clinical concept. It is why venous capacitance can be recruited on demand (postural reflex, hemorrhage compensation, Ch 25) — small intravenous pressure changes redistribute large volumes — and why a saphenous vein grafted into the coronary arterial pressure range sees the small-slope tail end of its $P$–$V$ curve and behaves stiffly.

#### Figure 19-9 — Elastic properties of a spring *(listed)*

Panel A: a spring of length $L_0$ in its relaxed state and length $L = 2 L_0$ or $3 L_0$ under a hanging weight, with a gauge measuring the applied force. Panel B: stress–strain diagram with strain $\varepsilon = (L - L_0)/L_0$ on the x-axis (0.0 → 1.0 → 2.0) and stress $\sigma = F/A$ on the y-axis; a linear region (slope = Young modulus $Y$) terminating at the **elastic limit** and continuing to a **breaking point**. The figure is the mechanical analog used to read the radius–tension diagrams of blood vessels.

#### Figure 19-10 — Three axes of vessel deformation *(listed)*

A short cylindrical vessel with three orthogonal axes labeled: circumferential ($\theta$), axial ($x$), radial ($r$). The figure motivates why the circumferential stretch dominates the elastic analysis of blood vessels (axial elongation ~1%, wall thinning small).

#### Figure 19-11 — Laplace's law *(listed)*

A thin-walled cylindrical vessel cut along its length by an imaginary slit of unit length $L$. **Radial arrows pushing outward** represent the distending transmural force $\Delta P = P_i - P_o$ acting on the wall. **Circumferential arrows pulling the lips of the slit together** represent the wall tension $T$ (force per unit length along the axis). Mechanical equilibrium → $T = \Delta P \cdot r$ (Eq. 19-8). The figure also shows the cross-sectional notation $A_{\text{slit}} = L \times $ wall thickness and $A_{\text{surface}} = L \times 2 r$.

#### Figure 19-12 — Elastic diagram (tension vs. radius) of blood vessels *(listed)*

Three-panel construction transforming compliance curves into Laplace-equivalent radius–tension diagrams. **Panel A** replots the aorta and vena cava $P$–$V$ curves of Fig. 19-8. **Panel B** transposes the axes so $V$ is on x and $P$ on y. **Panel C** converts $V \to r$ via $V = L \pi r^2$ on the x-axis and $P \to T = P r$ on the y-axis. In the resulting **radius–tension diagram**, the aorta (red) shows a steep tension rise with modest radius change, while the vena cava (blue) fills over a wide radius range before tension develops, then steepens once the perimeter is stressed. A broken radial line from the origin to any point has slope equal to the transmural pressure ($T/r = \Delta P$). The worked example: aorta at $r = 6$ mm, $T = 120{,}000$ dyne/cm → $\Delta P = 150$ mm Hg; vena cava at $r = 6.7$ mm, $T = 12{,}000$ dyne/cm → $\Delta P \approx 13.5$ mm Hg.

#### Figure 19-13 — Chemical dissection of elastic moduli of collagen and elastin *(listed)*

Tension vs. relative radius (%) for three preparations of the same artery: (1) **Intact artery (red)** — shallow at low stretch, steepens with recruitment of collagen; (2) **Artery with collagen fibers only (orange, elastin digested with elastase)** — steep, nearly linear; (3) **Artery with elastic fibers only (violet, collagen digested with formic acid)** — shallow throughout. The figure is the experimental basis for the recruitment model of the intact wall (elastin handles the easy stretch, collagen catches the hard stretch).

#### Figure 19-14 — Effect of aging on arteries *(listed)*

**Panel A — Pressure–volume diagram.** Family of curves at six age bands (7–15 months, 10, 20, 30, 40–59, ≥60 years) with $\Delta P$ (mm Hg, x-axis 0–200) vs. aortic volume (mL, y-axis 0–280). The curves shift up and to the right with age. Inset white triangles at the diastolic operating point (~80 mm Hg) show that the *same* aortic stroke-volume increment $\Delta V$ produces a small pulse pressure in the young curve but a much larger pulse pressure in the older curves because the operating slope of the curve is flatter. **Panel B — Radius–tension diagram.** Tension (dyne/cm × 10³, y-axis 0–160) vs. relative radius (%, x-axis 100–200). The family of age curves becomes steeper and the upturn shifts to lower radii with age, reflecting fibrosis, more collagen, and increased cross-linking among collagen fibers.

> Vision note: This is the **mechanical foundation of isolated systolic hypertension and widened pulse pressure with age**, the central arterial waveform's age-dependent reshaping, and the rise in pulse wave velocity.

#### Figure 19-15 — Active versus passive tension *(listed)*

Tension (dyne/cm × 10³, y-axis 0–300) vs. relative radius (%, x-axis 100–200; 100% = excised, maximally NE-stimulated but unstressed). Three curves: **red** = total tension (passive + active) of an artery whose VSMCs are maximally stimulated with norepinephrine; **green** = passive tension only (VSMCs poisoned with potassium cyanide); **blue** = active tension only (red minus green) — the smooth-muscle length–tension diagram, with the characteristic biphasic shape (rises to a maximum near a relative radius of ~180% and then declines as actin–myosin overlap falls).

#### Figure 19-16 — Mechanical stability of vessels *(listed)*

Two side-by-side cartoon vessels with three panels each, showing how a vessel responds to a perturbation in transmural pressure. **Panel A — Elastic wall.** At rest the vessel sits at equilibrium ($T = P r$). A pressure rise $+\Delta P$ produces a small $+\Delta r$, and the elastic wall automatically generates the needed $+\Delta T$ to re-establish equilibrium → stable inflation. A pressure fall produces stable deflation. **Panel B — Fixed active tension only.** At rest $T$ exactly balances $P r$. A pressure rise drives $(P + \Delta P)(r + \Delta r) > T$ → "Pressure wins" → **blowout**. A pressure fall drives $(P - \Delta P)(r - \Delta r) < T$ → "Tension wins" → **collapse**. This is the geometry of arteriovenous anastomoses and the conceptual basis for aneurysm propagation in vessels with degraded elastic tissue and for Raynaud-style closure in vessels with unopposed active tension.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Arborization / level of branching** — successive bifurcations from aorta → vena cava; aggregate cross-sectional area rises through the microcirculation, peaks at the postcapillary venules, falls again at the vena cava.
- **Continuity along arborization** — $F_{\text{total}} = A_1 \bar v_1 = A_2 \bar v_2 = \cdots$ (Eq. 19-1).
- **Single-vessel flow $F_i$** — $F_{\text{total}}/N$, spans 10 orders of magnitude from aorta to capillary.
- **Aggregate (total) cross-sectional area $A_{\text{tot}} = N A_i$** — equals ~3000 cm² at the systemic capillaries.
- **High-pressure system / low-pressure system** — LV-contracted → systemic arterioles vs. systemic capillaries → LV-relaxed (via right heart + pulmonary).
- **Central blood volume** — heart chambers + pulmonary circulation (~15%), filling reservoir for the left heart.
- **Capacitance vessels (veins)** — high compliance in low-pressure range; hold ~65% of total blood volume.
- **Resistance vessels (terminal small arteries + arterioles)** — site of greatest aggregate resistance and steepest $\Delta P/\Delta x$; principal target of vasomotor control.
- **Capillary pressure equation** — $P_c = [(R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}}) P_a + P_v] / [1 + (R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}})]$ (Eq. 19-3).
- **Vasomotion** — constriction/dilation of arteriolar smooth muscle; shifts the local pressure profile both upstream and downstream.
- **Transit / circulation time** — $t = V/F$ (Eq. 19-2); ~1 min total, ~10 s for a single bed.
- **Intima / media / adventitia** — three layers of the vessel wall (only intima in capillaries).
- **Wall building blocks** — endothelial cells, elastic fibers, collagen fibers, vascular smooth-muscle cells.
- **Elastin** — insoluble cross-linked polymer of glycine/alanine/valine/proline; stretches >100%; elastic modulus ~$4 \times 10^6$ dyne/cm².
- **Collagen** — types I and III in vessels; stretches only 3–4%; elastic modulus ~$10^9$ dyne/cm² (>1000× elastin).
- **Microfibrils** — ~10-nm glycoprotein covering on the elastin core.
- **VSMC (vascular smooth-muscle cell)** — supplies active tension; arranged as spirals (elastic arteries) or rings (muscular arteries).
- **Critical closing pressure** — driving pressure below which flow stops despite a finite $\Delta P$; ~6 mm Hg at baseline, rises with sympathetic tone (40+ mm Hg in shock).
- **Distensibility $\Delta V / \Delta P$**, **normalized distensibility** $(\Delta V / V_0)/\Delta P$ (Eq. 19-4), **compliance $C = dV/dP$** (Eq. 19-5).
- **Elastance** — $E = 1/C$ (reused in lung mechanics, Ch 27).
- **Hooke's law / stress / strain / Young modulus** — $F = k \Delta L$, $\sigma = F/A$, $\varepsilon = \Delta L / L_0$, $\sigma = Y \varepsilon$.
- **Laplace's law** — $T = \Delta P \cdot r$ for a thin-walled cylinder (Eq. 19-8).
- **Wall tension $T$** (dyne/cm) vs. **wall stress** (dyne/cm²) — the former assumes constant wall thickness.
- **Radius–tension diagram** — Laplace-transformed $P$–$V$ diagram; slope of the line from the origin to any point = $\Delta P$.
- **Recruitment of collagen** — at large stretch, collagen catches the load that elastin alone had carried, steepening the radius–tension slope.
- **Chemical dissection (elastase, formic acid)** — isolates collagen-only and elastin-only contributions to the wall.
- **Arteriosclerotic stiffening with age** — falling $C_a$, higher operating $\Delta P$, larger pulse pressure, faster pulse wave velocity.
- **Pulse pressure** — PP = SAP − DAP; widens with age.
- **Pulse wave velocity** — Moens–Korteweg $c_{\text{PWV}} = \sqrt{Eh / (2 r \rho)}$; rises with $E$ and therefore with age.
- **Windkessel approximation** — $P(t) = P_0 e^{-t/(R_p C_a)}$ during diastole; the aorta as a compliance reservoir smoothing pulsatile ventricular output (Ch 22).
- **Active vs. passive tension** — VSMC contribution vs. elastic-fiber contribution to total wall tension.
- **Stable vs. unstable equilibrium** — elastic-walled vessels are stable; vessels with only fixed active tension are unstable → blowout or collapse.
- **Aneurysm / varicosity** — elastic-fiber failure → $r$ exceeds Laplace equilibrium → blowout.
- **Raynaud disease** — unopposed VSMC stimulation → vessel closure in extremities.
- **Saphenous-to-coronary bypass graft** — a vein operating in the arterial pressure range, outside its compliant region.
- **Glomus body / myoepithelioid cells** — multilayer "endothelium" controlling AV shunts.
- **Ankle edema with standing** — high-venous-pressure → elevated $P_c$ → transudation, explained by Eq. 19-3.
