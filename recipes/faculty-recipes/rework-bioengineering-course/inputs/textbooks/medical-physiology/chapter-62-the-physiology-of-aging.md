## Section 1 — Concepts in aging (pp. 1235–1237)

### Subsection headings
- **During the 20th century, the age structure of populations in developed nations shifted toward older individuals** (pp. 1235–1236)
- **The definition, occurrence, and measurement of aging are fundamental but controversial issues** (pp. 1235–1236)
- **Aging is an evolved trait** (pp. 1236–1237)
- **Human aging studies can be cross-sectional or longitudinal** (pp. 1237)
- **Whether age-associated diseases are an integral part of aging remains controversial** (p. 1237)

### Core claims

- **Life expectancy vs. maximum lifespan.** *Life expectancy* is the projected mean length of life of a cohort born in a given year (or of people of a given age), computed from a population's mortality characteristics. In the U.S. it rose from ~47.9 y (men, 1900) to 76.4 y (2012), and ~50.7 → 81.2 y (women). *Maximum lifespan* — the age reached by the longest-lived of the old — has changed little over recorded history; what has changed is the **fraction** of the population reaching advanced ages.
- **Demography of aging.** The fraction of the U.S. population ≥65 y rose from 4% (1900) to 12.4% (2000). The shift depended only modestly on increased life expectancy at birth; the larger driver was a **falling birth rate**, which enlarges the elderly fraction. The post-WWII "baby boom" is visible as a bulge in the population pyramid (Fig. 62-1).
- **Aging = senescence.** Aging in this chapter is the synonym for senescence: "the progressive deteriorative changes during the adult period of life that underlie an increasing vulnerability to challenges and thereby decrease the ability of the organism to survive." Biological age is distinguished from chronological age, but no agreed panel of **biomarkers of aging** yet exists, so individual aging rate cannot be quantitated.
- **Gompertz mortality law (1825).** After early adulthood, the **age-specific death rate** (fraction entering an age interval who die during it) rises **exponentially** with adult age. The slope of the log-mortality-vs-age line has traditionally been taken as the rate of population aging — the gompertzian "gold standard," now challenged by some biogerontologists.
- **Aging is an evolved trait — by default.** Aging is *not* a programmed adaptation analogous to development. It evolved because the **force of natural selection declines with age**: in a hostile environment most individuals die before senescence is expressed, so deleterious genes acting only at advanced ages escape selection. Three non-exclusive evolutionary mechanisms (each with experimental support):
  - **Mutation accumulation** (Peter Medawar, 1952) — deleterious mutations whose effects appear only at advanced ages are not removed by selection.
  - **Antagonistic pleiotropy** (George Williams, 1957) — alleles that *increase* fitness in early adulthood but are deleterious in late life are positively selected; aging is a byproduct.
  - **Disposable soma** (Tom Kirkwood, 1977) — finite energy is partitioned between reproduction and somatic maintenance to maximize lifetime progeny; less energy goes to maintenance than is needed for indefinite survival, and a hostile environment shifts more energy toward reproduction.
- **Study designs.** *Cross-sectional* designs (different age cohorts studied over a short period) are cheap but suffer two confounders: the **cohort effect** (different cohorts had different environments, e.g., education) and **selective mortality** (high-risk individuals are underrepresented at older ages). *Longitudinal* designs (repeated study of the same subjects) avoid these but are costly, require an organizational structure outliving the investigator, and face dropout, lifestyle change, and measurement-repetition effects.
- **Primary vs. secondary aging.** *Primary aging* = intrinsic changes with age, unrelated to disease or environment. *Secondary aging* = changes from the interaction of primary aging with environment/disease. Whether age-associated diseases (coronary disease, stroke, many cancers, type 2 diabetes, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, cataracts, Alzheimer, Parkinson) are an *integral part* of aging remains contested — Holliday argues the distinction between pathological and non-pathological age changes "is not at all fundamental."

### Equations

- **Gompertz law of mortality** — the age-specific death rate (hazard / force of mortality) rises exponentially with adult age:

  $$\mu(t) = \mu_0\, e^{\gamma t}$$

  where $\mu(t)$ is the age-specific death rate at age $t$, $\mu_0$ the (extrapolated) baseline rate at the start of the adult interval, and $\gamma$ the **Gompertz slope** — the exponential rate constant traditionally interpreted as the *rate of population aging*. On a semi-log plot ($\ln \mu$ vs. $t$) this is a straight line of slope $\gamma$, which is exactly the form of Figure 62-2.

  Equivalently, taking logarithms:

  $$\ln \mu(t) = \ln \mu_0 + \gamma t$$

  The doubling time of mortality is $t_2 = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\gamma}$ (for humans, roughly 8 years).

- **Survival curve / rectangularization (qualitative).** Let $S(t)$ be the fraction of a cohort surviving to age $t$, with $S(t) = \exp\!\big(-\!\int_0^t \mu(\tau)\,d\tau\big)$. As premature (environmental/infectious) deaths are prevented, deaths cluster near the maximum lifespan and $S(t)$ becomes increasingly **rectangular** — flat near $S \approx 1$ for most of life, then a steep terminal drop. Improved life expectancy moves the *median* of $S(t)$ right and squares the curve without necessarily moving the *maximum* (the right-hand foot). An intervention that slows aging proper (e.g., caloric restriction, Fig. 62-7) instead shifts the entire curve — including its maximum — to the right.

## Section 2 — Cellular and molecular mechanisms of aging (pp. 1238–1242)

### Subsection headings
- **Oxidative stress and related processes that damage macromolecules may have a causal role in aging** (pp. 1238–1240)
- **Inadequacy of repair processes may contribute to the aging phenotype** (pp. 1240–1241)
- **Dysfunction of the homeostasis of cell number may be a major factor in aging** (pp. 1241–1242)

### Core claims — three classes of mechanism

The chapter frames aging as a long-term **imbalance between damage and repair**: development creates and repairs; a brief adult balance follows; then damage chronically outpaces repair. No single mechanism is "the" cause.

**(1) Damage**

- **Free-radical / oxidative-stress theory.** Reactive oxygen species (ROS) — H₂O₂, the hydroxyl radical (·OH, the most reactive), and the superoxide anion radical (O₂·⁻) — have an unpaired outer-orbital electron and react with target molecules, propagating radical chain reactions. The historical antecedent is Pearl's 1928 "rate of living" theory (since discarded). The **quantitatively dominant ROS source is the mitochondrial electron transport chain** (complexes I and III), generating O₂·⁻ as a byproduct. <1% of O₂ used in aerobic metabolism becomes ROS, but even that would be lethal without defenses. ROS also have physiological roles (thyroid-hormone synthesis, NADPH-oxidase/myeloperoxidase bacterial killing; NO is itself a free-radical signaling molecule). **Caveat:** genetically engineered mice over/under-expressing antioxidant enzymes do **not** support oxidative stress as *the* cause of aging.
- **Glycation and glycoxidation → AGEs.** Glycation is the **nonenzymatic** reaction of reducing-sugar carbonyl groups (e.g., glucose) with amino groups of proteins/DNA: glucose + lysine → Schiff base → Amadori compound/product → (with oxidation, *glycoxidation*) advanced glycation end products (AGEs) such as carboxymethyllysine or the cross-link **pentosidine**. AGEs accumulate on **long-lived proteins**; glycemia drives the rate (enhanced in diabetes; cf. HbA1c, Box 29-1). Cerami's **glycation hypothesis of aging** rests on the resemblance of the aging and diabetic phenotypes. AGE consequences: lens opacification (cataract), AGE-mediated **collagen cross-linking** stiffening blood vessels and connective tissue, DNA damage.
- **Mitochondrial damage.** Because mitochondria are the major ROS source, they are also a major **target**. mtDNA — unprotected by histones — accumulates damage faster than genomic DNA. The **mitochondrial theory of aging**: mtDNA damage → reduced ATP generation → loss of cell function → aging.
- **Somatic mutation.** Radiation, toxic chemicals, and (recognized recently) oxidative stress damage genomic and mtDNA; damage sits at a steady state between insult and repair. The **DNA-damage theory of aging**: accumulated damage interferes with replication/transcription, impairing cell function. Whether somatic mutation alone suffices to cause the organismic aging phenotype is unclear.

**(2) Inadequate repair**

- **DNA repair theory:** repair capacity declines with age → rising steady-state damaged-DNA level → compromised genome integrity; unrepaired/mis-repaired DNA in **stem cells** may be especially important.
- **Protein homeostasis (proteostasis):** deamidation, racemization, isomerization, mis/unfolding, aggregation, and fragmentation degrade proteins. Defenses: **chaperones** (folding/refolding) and the **ubiquitin/proteasome system** (degradation of non-refolded proteins). Chaperone levels and proteasome catalytic activity fall with age; small **oligomers** (not mature amyloid fibers) are the most toxic species in diseases such as Alzheimer. Total-body protein turnover declines, so mean protein lifetime lengthens; extracellular-matrix collagen/elastin undergo oxidation, glycation, and cross-linking.
- **Autophagy** — lysosomal degradation, three types: **macroautophagy** (autophagosome double membrane fuses with lysosome), **microautophagy** (lysosome membrane engulfs single molecules), **chaperone-mediated autophagy** (chaperone–cargo complex binds a lysosomal-membrane receptor; cargo is unfolded and translocated). With age, lysosomes expand, enzyme activities change, and undegraded **lipofuscin** accumulates; all three autophagy types are impaired.

**(3) Dysregulation of cell number**

- **Hayflick limit & telomeres.** Human fibroblasts in culture divide a limited number of times (Hayflick & Moorhead, 1961). Telomeres (repeated DNA + proteins capping linear chromosomes) **shorten with each division** (Harley, late 1980s); at a critical length the cell can no longer divide — a probable molecular basis of the Hayflick limit. **Telomerase** (extends telomeres) is high in germline and cancer cells, absent/low in most somatic cells. Telomere shortening cannot affect truly **postmitotic** cells. Blackburn, Greider & Szostak shared the 2009 Nobel for telomere/telomerase work. Immune decline (immunosenescence) may partly reflect shortened **T-lymphocyte** telomeres limiting proliferative bursts.
- **Cell removal — necrosis, apoptosis, necroptosis.** *Necrosis*: uncontrolled response to severe trauma — cell swelling, membrane-integrity loss, lysis, inflammation. *Apoptosis* (programmed cell death): ATP-requiring, gene-driven, organelle-preserving, membrane-intact, non-inflammatory, with cell shrinkage and fragmentation into apoptotic bodies cleared by phagocytosis. *Necroptosis*: a regulated, orchestrated alternative to apoptosis, triggered by many of the same stresses, engaged when caspases are suppressed (e.g., by pathogens); effectors include **RIP1/RIP3 kinases and MLKL** (a porin-like plasma-membrane protein); implicated in ischemia-reperfusion injury of brain, kidney, liver. **Dysregulation of apoptosis promotes aging**: too little → damaged cells persist / cancer risk; too much → unnecessary cell loss.

Composite diagram of **three interacting routes to apoptosis**, converging on executioner caspases.
- **(1) Extrinsic / death-receptor pathway (top left).** Trimeric **FasL** binds three **Fas** receptors (TNFR-family death receptors with cytosolic **death domains**); clustering recruits the adapter **FADD**, which aggregates **procaspase-8** → autoproteolytic activation of **caspase-8** (initiator). ## Section 3 — Aging of the human physiological systems (pp. 1242–1246)

### Subsection headings
- **Aging people lose height and lean body mass but gain and redistribute fat** (pp. 1243)
- **Aging thins the skin and causes the musculoskeletal system to become weak, brittle, and stiff** (pp. 1243–1244) — skin, skeletal muscle (sarcopenia), bone (osteoporosis), synovial joints.
- **The healthy elderly experience deficits in sensory transduction and speed of central processing** (pp. 1244–1245) — sensory, motor, cognitive.
- **Aging causes decreased arterial compliance and increased ventilation-perfusion mismatching** (pp. 1244–1245) — cardiovascular, pulmonary, exercise.
- **Glomerular filtration rate falls with age in many but not all people** (p. 1245)
- **Aging has only minor effects on gastrointestinal function** (pp. 1245–1246)
- **Aging causes modest declines in most endocrine functions** (pp. 1245–1246)

### Core claims — the homeostatic-reserve principle

Aging erodes the **reserve/redundancy** of physiological systems, so a deficit is often invisible until an **unusual challenge** or until function falls below a critical level. Individual variation is large: some show "successful" aging with minimal decline; the **distinction between primary aging and disease** (e.g., atherosclerosis, COPD) is essential — in the *absence* of such diseases, age changes in many systems are modest.

- **Body composition.** Height peaks ~16–17 y (women) / 18–19 y (men), then declines (vertebral-disk compression + bone loss), falling 2.5–5% by age 70. **Fat-free mass and lean body mass decline progressively** across adult life in both sexes (true even in lifelong athletes). **Adipose fat increases and redistributes** — accumulating around abdominal viscera and subcutaneous abdomen, lost from extremities and face. **Visceral fat secretes proinflammatory adipokines** that may promote senescence.
- **Skin.** Intrinsic aging thins the epidermis (slower keratinocyte turnover; fewer melanocytes → less photoprotection; fewer Langerhans cells → less immune surveillance) and the dermis (↓elastin/collagen, altered glycosaminoglycans → dermis ~20% thinner, stiffer). **Photoaging** (UV) adds coarse wrinkles. Fewer/weaker sweat and sebaceous glands; graying (fewer follicular melanocytes); slower nail growth.
- **Sarcopenia.** Progressive loss of skeletal-muscle mass beyond ~50 y — chiefly loss of **number** (and some size) of fibers, from inactivity **plus** loss of motor neurons innervating **type II** motor units; denervated fibers atrophy/die or are reinnervated by sprouting → larger motor units → less fine control. Strength training can still enlarge fibers and add mass in the elderly.
- **Bone / osteoporosis.** Remodeling balances osteoclast resorption and osteoblast formation; from the late third decade **resorption exceeds formation** (driven by ROS, ↓IGF-1, ↑PTH) → slow bone loss. **Women lose bone rapidly for 5–10 y after menopause** (↓estrogen, ↑FSH), then revert to the male rate. **Osteoporosis** = bone mineral density ≥2.5 SD below the young-adult mean (WHO); raises fracture risk (Box 52-3).
- **Synovial joints.** Articular cartilage thins, loses water and proteoglycans, gains collagen cross-links; ↓tensile stiffness/fatigue resistance/strength; impaired chondrocytes → reduced flexibility and a major role in **osteoarthritis**.
- **Nervous system (less severe than commonly believed, absent neurodegeneration).** *Sensory:* ↓touch, vibration, two-point discrimination, proprioception/vestibular; impaired thermoregulation; high-frequency **hearing loss** (organ-of-Corti hair-cell loss); **presbyopia**, fewer cones, sluggish pupil, poorer rod dark-adaptation; ↓taste and especially **olfaction** ("pastel" flavors). *Motor:* the hallmark is **slowed central processing → longer reaction time** (worse with response complexity); poorer balance/posture → frequent falls; slower, shorter, more frequent steps. *Cognitive:* in the absence of dementia, decline is **modest** — slowed processing, reduced novel-problem solving and some memory types, but preserved use of stored knowledge and conceptual retrieval; older people still learn, but more slowly.
- **Cardiovascular.** ↓Arterial distensibility → ↑systolic pressure, slight ↓diastolic, **widened pulse pressure**; ↑afterload → **LV wall thickening (myocyte hypertrophy, not hyperplasia)**. Resting preload unchanged; early diastolic filling falls but ↑left-atrial contraction compensates. **Postural hypotension** is common (blunted baroreceptor reflex). The aging heart is **less responsive to adrenergic stimulation**, so exercise stroke-volume rises via the **Frank-Starling** mechanism (↑heart rate is blunted, ↑stroke volume; ↑LV end-diastolic/end-systolic volumes; ↓max ejection fraction).
- **Pulmonary.** ↓Respiratory-muscle strength/endurance (type IIa atrophy); ↓lung volumes and forced expiratory volumes (FEV₁); small airways collapse more readily (atelectasis) from collagen/elastin degeneration → **V/Q mismatch**, ↓resting arterial PO₂. Healthy elderly do not *fail* gas exchange, but homeostatic challenge can unmask compromise.
- **Exercise.** **Maximal O₂ uptake (V̇O₂max) declines** with age (more in untrained), from ↓muscle mass, ↓CV and pulmonary function, ↓pulmonary diffusing capacity (D_L; ↓alveolar capillary volume) and ↑V/Q mismatch. Muscle and the CV system remain responsive to training into the 10th decade.
- **Renal.** ↓Renal blood flow; ↓basal renin/angiotensin II. Cross-sectional data show **GFR declining from age 30** linearly — but the **Baltimore Longitudinal Study** found one-third with the predicted decline, one-third steeper, **one-third with no decline at all**: GFR decline is *not inevitable*. Tubular function declines: poorer handling of Na⁺ load, urine dilution/concentration, and excretion of K⁺/phosphate/acid. **Bladder** capacity/compliance fall, detrusor over- and under-activity rise → urgency, nocturia, frequency, incomplete emptying.
- **Gastrointestinal (minor).** GI function in healthy elderly is largely preserved. Loss of gastric-acid secretion is now attributed to **H. pylori**, *not* aging itself. Skeletal-muscle loss at both ends → minor chewing/swallowing/continence changes. **Liver mass, hepatic blood flow, drug clearance, and regenerative capacity all fall significantly.**
- **Endocrine (modest declines).** Total energy expenditure ↓ (chiefly ↓activity); resting metabolic rate ↓ tracks ↓fat-free mass (RMR per kg fat-free mass unchanged).
  - *Insulin:* impaired glucose tolerance mainly from ↑insulin resistance (↑adiposity, ↓activity), with a small intrinsic-aging component plus an age-related ↓insulin secretion.
  - *GH/IGF-1 (somatopause):* ↓pulsatile GH → markedly ↓plasma IGF-1; functional consequences unclear.
  - *Adrenal:* cortisol and aldosterone well preserved; **DHEA falls markedly**.
  - *Thyroid:* function preserved into the 9th decade; in centenarians ↓TSH and ↓free T₃ (impaired 5′/3′-deiodinase).
  - *PTH:* rises with age (↑secretion).
  - *Gonadal:* **menopause** ~50 y (abrupt cessation of female fertility, ↓estrogen, ↑FSH); men have no abrupt change but a progressive decline — **andropause** (Box 54-1).

### Clinical box

**Box 62-1 — Frailty.** A geriatric syndrome (≈7% of those >65 y, ≈20% of those >80 y). **Fried frailty criteria** — frailty = ≥3 of: unintentional weight loss (≥4 kg/y), self-reported exhaustion, weakness (↓grip strength), slow walking speed, low physical activity. Frail individuals face increased falls, disability, hospitalization, and death. Frailty is **distinct from age-associated disease and from disability** (≈27% of a frail cohort had neither comorbidity nor disability), though comorbidity/disability are both risk factors and outcomes. Involuntary terminal weight loss is **not** simply reduced intake (raising food intake usually fails; aged rats lose weight terminally even while eating more). Frailty likelihood rises steeply as the number of abnormal physiological processes grows.

## Section 4 — Aging slowly (pp. 1246–1247)

### Subsection headings
- **Caloric restriction slows aging and extends life in several species, including some mammals** (pp. 1246–1247)
- **Genetic alterations can extend life in several species** (pp. 1247)
- **Proposed interventions to slow aging and extend human life are controversial** (p. 1247)

### Core claims

- **Caloric restriction (CR).** Restricting food intake (~60% of ad libitum) from soon after weaning increases **both mean and maximum lifespan** of rats (McCay, 1935; Fig. 62-7) and also of mice, hamsters, dogs, fish, several invertebrates, and yeast; rhesus-monkey studies suggest CR extends life in nonhuman primates too. The effect is from **caloric** restriction, not a specific nutrient, and works even when begun in young-adult/middle age. Most biogerontologists conclude CR **retards aging** because CR animals keep youthful physiology and CR **delays most age-associated diseases** (neoplastic, degenerative, immune).
- **CR mechanisms (none firmly established).** Early hypotheses — retarded growth (McCay) and reduced fat mass — are refuted (effects dissociable from fat mass; works when started in adulthood). Leading candidates:
  - **↓Oxidative stress** (most popular) — CR lowers accumulated oxidative damage.
  - **↓Glycation/glycoxidation** via sustained lower plasma glucose.
  - **↓Insulin and ↓IGF-1** — and genetic data strongly tie **reduced insulin-like signaling** to longevity.
  - **mTOR inhibition** — **rapamycin** extends life even in 600-day-old mice; mTOR senses nutrient status and regulates growth/division.
  - **Hormesis** — beneficial cellular responses to mild repeated stress (CR is a mild daily stress); CR rodents better tolerate acute insults (surgery, toxins, heat).
- **Genetic longevity determinants.** Large interspecies lifespan differences (Drosophila <100 d, mice <5 y, humans >100 y) are primarily genetic; selective breeding alters longevity. Single-gene findings — many converging on **insulin-like signaling**:
  - **age-1** (C. elegans, Friedman & Johnson 1988) — partial loss of function of a **PI3-kinase** in the insulin-like pathway markedly extends life. (Invertebrates have one combined insulin/IGF-1 pathway.)
  - **Ames dwarf mice** (Prop-1 mutation) — no pituitary GH/TSH/prolactin → low thyroid hormone, IGF-1, insulin → **extended lifespan**.
  - **GHR/BP knockout** mice (no GH receptor) — high GH, low IGF-1, **life extension**; reducing the **insulin receptor** 85–90% in adipose also extends life.
  - **KLOTHO overexpression** — Klotho protein suppresses insulin/IGF-1 signaling and extends mouse life.
  - **Sirtuins** — yeast **sir2** overexpression raises Sir2 (an rDNA-stabilizing deacetylase) and extends replicative life; Sir2 orthologs extend C. elegans, Drosophila, and human cell lines, but their role in CR's action is not yet clear.
  - These pathways likely interact as a **longevity-signaling network**.
- **Antiaging interventions are controversial.** Exercise and diet genuinely reduce coronary disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes. But claimed "magic bullets" — antioxidants (vitamins E, C), amino acids (methionine), drugs (deprenyl), hormones (melatonin, DHEA, GH, estrogen, testosterone) — have **no credible evidence** of slowing/reversing human aging, and may carry harm (estrogen + progestin HRT's cardiovascular harm is the cautionary case; recombinant GH is "of concern" given that insulin-like signaling *promotes* aging).

### Figures

#### Figure 62-7 — Survival curves: ad libitum vs. restricted diet *(viewed)*

**y-axis: percent of rats alive (0–100); x-axis: age in days (to ~1500).** Two sigmoid survival curves for 115 male F344 rats each. The **ad libitum** curve falls first (median and maximum at lower ages); the **restricted-diet** curve (60% of ad libitum intake from 6 weeks) is shifted bodily to the right — **both the median and the maximum length of life are markedly greater**. This is the figure that visually distinguishes "preventing premature death" (which squares/rectangularizes a survival curve without moving its right foot) from **slowing aging itself**, which shifts the *entire* curve including its maximum. Data from Yu, Masoro, Murata et al., *J Gerontol* 1982.

## Glossary

- **Life expectancy** — projected mean lifespan of a cohort, from population mortality data.
- **Maximum lifespan** — age of the longest-lived; little changed historically.
- **Senescence / aging** — progressive deteriorative adult-life changes → ↑vulnerability.
- **Gompertz law** — age-specific death rate rises exponentially with adult age, $\mu(t)=\mu_0 e^{\gamma t}$.
- **Rectangularization of the survival curve** — deaths cluster near maximum lifespan as premature death is prevented.
- **Biomarker of aging** — sought-after quantitative index of biological (vs. chronological) age; none agreed.
- **Mutation accumulation / antagonistic pleiotropy / disposable soma** — three evolutionary theories.
- **Primary vs. secondary aging** — intrinsic vs. environment/disease-interacting changes.
- **ROS** — O₂·⁻, H₂O₂, ·OH; chiefly mitochondrial (ETC complexes I/III).
- **SOD / catalase / glutathione peroxidase** — enzymatic antioxidant defenses; **Fenton reaction** generates ·OH.
- **Glycation / glycoxidation / AGEs** — nonenzymatic sugar–protein adducts (carboxymethyllysine, pentosidine).
- **Mitochondrial theory** — mtDNA damage → ↓ATP → aging.
- **Hayflick limit** — finite division number of cultured somatic cells.
- **Telomere / telomerase** — chromosome caps shortening per division; telomerase extends them (germline, cancer).
- **Apoptosis** — programmed cell death; extrinsic (Fas/caspase-8), intrinsic (MPTP/cytochrome c/apoptosome/caspase-9), p53/DNA-damage pathways.
- **Necroptosis** — regulated caspase-independent death (RIP1/RIP3, MLKL).
- **Lipofuscin** — undegraded lysosomal pigment accumulating with age.
- **Sarcopenia** — age-related skeletal-muscle loss.
- **Osteoporosis** — BMD ≥2.5 SD below young-adult mean (WHO).
- **Frailty** — geriatric syndrome (Fried criteria; ≥3 of 5).
- **Homeostatic reserve** — system redundancy masking decline until challenged.
- **Menopause / andropause / somatopause** — gonadal (abrupt female / gradual male) and GH-axis declines.
- **Immunosenescence** — age-related immune decline.
- **Caloric restriction (CR)** — reduced intake extending mean and maximum lifespan.
- **Hormesis** — benefit from mild repeated stress.
- **Insulin-like signaling / mTOR / sirtuins** — nutrient-sensing longevity pathways.
