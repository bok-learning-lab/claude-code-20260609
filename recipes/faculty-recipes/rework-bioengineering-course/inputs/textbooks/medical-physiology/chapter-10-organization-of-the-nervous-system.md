## Section 1 — Cells of the nervous system (pp. 254–258)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The neuron is the functional unit of the nervous system** (p. 254)
- **The neuron has four functional regions: dendrites, soma, axon, and presynaptic terminals** (pp. 254–256)
- **Neurons can be classified by morphology and by neurotransmitter** (pp. 256–257)
- **Glia outnumber neurons and provide critical metabolic, structural, and signaling support** (pp. 257–258)

### Core claims — neurons
- The human CNS contains roughly $10^{11}$ neurons. Each makes ~$10^3$–$10^4$ synaptic connections; total ~$10^{14}$–$10^{15}$ synapses.
- **Four functional regions** (Fig. 10-1):
  - **Dendrites** — receptive surface; receive synaptic inputs; covered in dendritic spines on many cell types.
  - **Soma (cell body / perikaryon)** — nucleus, biosynthetic machinery; usually a few μm to ~100 μm across.
  - **Axon** — single output process emerging from the **axon hillock**, supporting all-or-none AP conduction over distances from μm to >1 m.
  - **Presynaptic terminals** — at axon termini and en passant boutons; release neurotransmitter.
- **Axoplasmic transport**:
  - **Anterograde fast** (~200–400 mm/day) — kinesin-mediated; vesicles, mitochondria, membrane proteins.
  - **Retrograde fast** (~100–200 mm/day) — dynein-mediated; signaling endosomes, viruses (rabies, herpes, polio), tetanus toxin.
  - **Slow transport** (0.2–8 mm/day) — cytoskeletal proteins (tubulin, neurofilament subunits) and some cytosolic enzymes.
- **Morphological types**: unipolar (invertebrate sensory; some autonomic), pseudounipolar (mammalian DRG sensory), bipolar (retinal, olfactory), multipolar (CNS — the dominant class).
- **Neurotransmitter classification**: cholinergic, glutamatergic, GABAergic, glycinergic, monoaminergic (dopaminergic, noradrenergic, serotonergic, histaminergic), peptidergic. The same neuron typically releases one classical transmitter plus one or more peptide co-transmitters.

### Core claims — glia
- Three macroglial classes plus microglia + ependyma:
  - **Astrocytes** — most numerous; star-shaped processes contact synapses (perisynaptic processes) and capillary endothelium (perivascular endfeet). Roles: K⁺ buffering (spatial buffering), glutamate uptake (EAAT1/2 — GLAST and GLT-1), neurotransmitter cycling (glutamate–glutamine cycle), formation of the blood–brain barrier (induce endothelial tight junctions; see Ch 11), reactive gliosis after injury, GFAP marker.
  - **Oligodendrocytes (CNS) / Schwann cells (PNS)** — myelinate axons. One oligodendrocyte myelinates many axon segments; one Schwann cell myelinates one internode of one axon. Markers: MBP, PLP, MAG, CNPase.
  - **Microglia** — CNS-resident macrophages of myeloid origin; perform phagocytosis, surveillance, and immune signaling. Markers: Iba1, CD68.
  - **Ependymal cells** — ciliated cuboidal epithelium lining ventricles and central canal; specialized at the choroid plexus to produce CSF (see Ch 11).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The neuron has four functional regions: dendrites, soma, axon, and presynaptic terminals." (p. 254)
- > "Axons can be exceptionally long; the typical axon contains much more cytoplasm than does the cell body, up to 1000 times as much." (p. 256)
- > "Glia outnumber neurons in the brain." (p. 257)
- > "Astrocytes have specialized end-feet that contact both the cell bodies of neurons and the surface of blood vessels, providing the cellular link between neurons and their blood supply." (p. 258)
- > "Oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the PNS form the myelin sheath." (p. 258)

## Section 2 — CNS and PNS gross anatomy (pp. 258–264)

### Subsection headings
- **The CNS comprises the brain and spinal cord** (pp. 258–261)
- **The brain has three principal divisions: forebrain, brainstem, cerebellum** (pp. 259–261)
- **The PNS has somatic, autonomic, and enteric components** (pp. 261–263)
- **Dermatomes and myotomes reflect the segmental origin of the spinal cord** (pp. 263–264)

### Core claims
- **Forebrain** = telencephalon (cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, amygdala) + diencephalon (thalamus, hypothalamus, epithalamus, subthalamus). The telencephalon is divided into four cortical lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital.
- **Brainstem** = midbrain (mesencephalon) + pons (metencephalon) + medulla (myelencephalon). Houses the cranial-nerve nuclei (CN III–XII) and the ascending/descending tract junctions, plus the reticular formation.
- **Cerebellum**: separate hindbrain structure responsible for motor coordination, motor learning, balance, and increasingly recognized cognitive contributions.
- **Spinal cord**: 31 segments (8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal). Each segment contributes a pair of dorsal and ventral roots that combine into a mixed spinal nerve.
- **PNS**:
  - **Somatic**: CNs (12 pairs) + spinal nerves. Afferent (sensory) + efferent (somatomotor) fibers.
  - **Autonomic** (Ch 14): sympathetic (T1–L2, "thoracolumbar"), parasympathetic ("craniosacral" — CNs III, VII, IX, X plus S2–S4), enteric.
  - **Enteric**: ~$10^8$ neurons in the GI wall organized into the myenteric (Auerbach) and submucosal (Meissner) plexuses; functions semi-autonomously (Ch 41).
- **Dermatomes**: each spinal segment innervates a strip of skin (e.g., T4 ≈ nipple line, T10 ≈ umbilicus, L1 ≈ inguinal); useful for localizing spinal-cord and root lesions.
- **Myotomes**: corresponding muscle innervation patterns; key roots include C5–T1 (upper extremity), L2–S2 (lower extremity).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The brain is divided into three principal regions: the forebrain, the brainstem, and the cerebellum." (p. 258)
- > "The 12 pairs of cranial nerves and 31 pairs of spinal nerves constitute the peripheral nervous system." (p. 261)
- > "The enteric nervous system is sometimes considered a third division of the autonomic nervous system." (p. 263)
- > "Dermatomes reflect the segmental origin of the spinal cord." (p. 263)

## Section 3 — Functional organization: gray, white, nuclei, tracts (pp. 264–268)

### Subsection headings
- **Gray matter contains cell bodies; white matter contains myelinated axons** (pp. 264–265)
- **Nuclei (CNS) and ganglia (PNS) are clusters of neuronal cell bodies** (p. 265)
- **The cerebral cortex has six layers with stereotyped input/output connectivity** (pp. 266–267)
- **Major ascending and descending tracts in the spinal cord** (pp. 267–268)

### Core claims
- **Gray matter** — cell bodies, dendrites, synapses, glia (predominantly astrocytes), capillary beds; appears darker due to high cellular density.
- **White matter** — myelinated axon tracts; appears white due to lipid-rich myelin.
- **Cerebral cortex**: six layers (I most superficial, VI deepest):
  - **I (molecular)** — sparse cells, abundant horizontal connections.
  - **II (external granular)** — small pyramidal and granule cells.
  - **III (external pyramidal)** — pyramidal cells projecting cortico-cortically (commissural via corpus callosum).
  - **IV (internal granular)** — thalamic input layer; thick in primary sensory cortex.
  - **V (internal pyramidal)** — large pyramidal cells; major output to subcortical structures (corticospinal, corticobulbar).
  - **VI (multiform)** — projection to thalamus.
- **Major spinal tracts** (Ch 12, 16 detail):
  - **Ascending**:
    - **Dorsal columns** (gracile and cuneate fasciculi) — fine touch, vibration, proprioception (uncrossed in cord; cross in medulla).
    - **Spinothalamic** — pain, temperature, crude touch (cross at the segmental level in cord).
    - **Spinocerebellar** — unconscious proprioception to cerebellum.
  - **Descending**:
    - **Lateral corticospinal** — voluntary movement; crosses at the pyramidal decussation in medulla.
    - **Anterior corticospinal** — small contingent; bilateral axial control.
    - **Rubrospinal, vestibulospinal, reticulospinal, tectospinal** — postural and reflex control.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Gray matter consists of cell bodies, dendrites, and the proximal portions of axons; white matter consists of myelinated axons." (p. 264)
- > "Clusters of neuronal cell bodies in the CNS are called nuclei; in the PNS they are called ganglia." (p. 265)
- > "The cerebral cortex has six layers, each with characteristic connections." (p. 266)

## Section 4 — Sensory and motor systems — overview (pp. 268–272)

### Subsection headings
- **Sensory information travels from the periphery to the cortex via labeled-line pathways** (pp. 268–270)
- **Voluntary motor commands descend from cortex through the corticospinal tract; the cerebellum and basal ganglia modulate them** (pp. 270–272)

### Core claims
- **Sensory pathway template**: 1st-order neuron (peripheral receptor → DRG/cranial sensory ganglion) → synapse in CNS → 2nd-order neuron decussates → reaches thalamus → 3rd-order neuron in thalamus (e.g., VPL for body, VPM for face) → primary sensory cortex.
- **Major somatosensory pathways**:
  - **Dorsal column–medial lemniscus** — touch/vibration/proprioception; 1st-order axon ascends ipsilaterally to medulla, 2nd-order crosses at the medial lemniscus → VPL → S1.
  - **Anterolateral (spinothalamic)** — pain/temperature; 1st-order synapse in dorsal horn, 2nd-order crosses at the same spinal segment, ascends contralaterally → VPL → S1.
- **Motor system**:
  - **Primary motor cortex (M1, layer V pyramidal cells)** → corticospinal tract → pyramidal decussation in medulla → lateral corticospinal tract → ventral horn α-motor neurons → muscle.
  - **Cerebellum** — comparator: receives copies of motor commands (corollary discharge) and sensory feedback; outputs corrections to motor cortex via thalamus. Lesions → ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor.
  - **Basal ganglia** — gate motor program selection: cortex → striatum → globus pallidus → thalamus → cortex (direct pathway facilitates; indirect pathway inhibits). Dopamine from substantia nigra pars compacta biases the system. Lesions → Parkinson's (hypokinetic), Huntington (hyperkinetic).
- **Lower motor neuron lesion vs. upper motor neuron lesion**:
  - **LMN lesion**: flaccid paralysis, hypotonia, areflexia, fasciculations, atrophy.
  - **UMN lesion**: spastic paralysis, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign, no fasciculations, less atrophy. Localization is one of the most clinically useful distinctions in neurology.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Sensory information is conveyed from the periphery to the cortex along labeled-line pathways." (p. 269)
- > "Voluntary movement is controlled by the primary motor cortex through the corticospinal tract." (p. 271)
- > "The cerebellum and basal ganglia modulate motor output." (p. 271)

## Section 5 — Development, injury, and plasticity (pp. 272–274)

### Subsection headings
- **Wallerian degeneration follows axotomy** (pp. 272–273)
- **PNS axons regenerate; CNS axons generally do not** (p. 273)
- **Synaptic and structural plasticity persist into adulthood** (pp. 273–274)
- **Adult neurogenesis is restricted to a few zones (SVZ, dentate gyrus)** (p. 274)

### Core claims
- **Wallerian degeneration**: distal stump of a severed axon swells, fragments within 24–72 h; Schwann cells (PNS) or microglia (CNS) clear debris.
- **PNS regeneration**: Schwann cells dedifferentiate, secrete neurotrophins (NGF, BDNF, GDNF), and form bands of Büngner that guide regenerating axon sprouts toward distal targets. Functional recovery is possible but imperfect (misrouting, slow regrowth ~1 mm/day).
- **CNS regeneration is blocked** by inhibitory myelin proteins (Nogo-A, MAG, OMgp), glial scar formation (reactive astrocytes producing CSPGs), and limited intrinsic growth program of mature CNS neurons.
- **Adult neurogenesis** in mammals is restricted to:
  - **Subventricular zone (SVZ)** → migration along the rostral migratory stream → olfactory bulb (largely in rodents; more debated in humans).
  - **Subgranular zone of dentate gyrus** → integration into hippocampal granule-cell layer; implicated in pattern separation and certain memory tasks.
- **Synaptic plasticity** (developed in Ch 13): LTP (long-term potentiation, NMDA-receptor dependent) and LTD (long-term depression) underlie learning and memory.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The distal portion of a severed axon undergoes Wallerian degeneration." (p. 272)
- > "Schwann cells facilitate axon regeneration in the PNS by forming bands of Büngner." (p. 273)
- > "Regeneration in the adult CNS is poor because of inhibitory factors and limited intrinsic growth capacity." (p. 273)

## Glossary

- **Neuron** — basic signaling cell; four regions (dendrites, soma, axon, terminals).
- **Axon hillock** — site of AP initiation.
- **Dendritic spine** — small protrusion that hosts most excitatory synapses on pyramidal cells.
- **Axoplasmic transport** — fast (kinesin/dynein) vs. slow (cytoskeletal).
- **Glia** — astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, microglia, ependyma.
- **GFAP, MBP, Iba1** — glial cell-type markers.
- **CNS / PNS / ANS / ENS** — divisions of the nervous system.
- **Forebrain / brainstem / cerebellum** — three brain regions.
- **Telencephalon / diencephalon / mesencephalon / metencephalon / myelencephalon** — embryologic divisions.
- **Cranial nerves CN I–XII**.
- **Dermatome / myotome**.
- **Gray matter / white matter / nucleus / ganglion / tract**.
- **Cortical layers I–VI**.
- **Dorsal column–medial lemniscus / spinothalamic / spinocerebellar** — ascending pathways.
- **Lateral / anterior corticospinal / rubrospinal / vestibulospinal / reticulospinal / tectospinal** — descending pathways.
- **Pyramidal decussation** — at the cervicomedullary junction.
- **VPL / VPM** — thalamic somatosensory relay nuclei.
- **M1, S1, V1, A1** — primary motor, somatosensory, visual, auditory cortices.
- **UMN vs LMN lesion** — clinical distinction.
- **Wallerian degeneration / bands of Büngner**.
- **Nogo-A, MAG, OMgp, CSPGs** — CNS regeneration inhibitors.
- **SVZ / dentate gyrus** — adult neurogenic niches.
