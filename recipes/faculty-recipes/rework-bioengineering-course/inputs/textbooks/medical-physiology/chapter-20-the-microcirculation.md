# Chapter 20 — The Microcirculation

> Section IV · The Cardiovascular System · pp. 461–482 · Author: Emile L. Boulpaep
> This is the "business end" chapter of Section IV: the capillary as the exchange surface. It uses the transport vocabulary built in Ch 5 (Fick's law, reflection coefficient, $L_p$, AQPs) and the hemodynamic vocabulary built in Ch 17 (driving pressure, resistance, compliance) and applies them to the smallest vessels. Section IV chapters that follow (Chs 23, 24, 25) inherit the local-control and vasomotion concepts developed here.

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Organization of the microcirculation** (pp. 461–463) — arterioles → metarterioles → precapillary sphincters → true capillaries → venules; three capillary types (continuous, fenestrated, sinusoidal/discontinuous); endothelial junctions and the BBB exception.
2. **Capillary exchange of solutes** (pp. 463–467) — gas exchange and Krogh's tissue cylinder; the O₂ extraction ratio; Fick's law for diffusion across the capillary wall; the $P_X \cdot S$ product; small-pore effect for hydrophilic solutes; large-pore effect (transcytosis) for macromolecules.
3. **Capillary exchange of water — the Starling principle** (pp. 467–474) — the four Starling forces; the classical Starling equation; capillary, interstitial, and subglycocalyx pressures; revised glycocalyx model; ultrafiltration vs. absorption; **edema mechanisms** (Box 20-1).
4. **Lymphatics** (pp. 474–477) — initial vs. collecting lymphatics; primary and secondary lymph valves; the three convective loops (cardiovascular, transvascular, lymphatic); lymph as the protein-return pathway.
5. **Regulation of the microcirculation** (pp. 477–482) — VSMC contractile machinery (MLCK, MLC phosphorylation); myogenic response (Bayliss); local metabolic control (PO₂, PCO₂, pH, K⁺, lactate, ATP, ADP, adenosine); endothelium-derived factors (NO, EDHF, PGI₂, ET, TXA₂, EDCF); autoregulation; angiogenesis (VEGF, FGF, angiopoietins; angiostatin, endostatin).

## Section 1 — Organization of the microcirculation (pp. 461–463)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The microcirculation serves both nutritional and non-nutritional roles** (p. 461)
- **The microcirculation extends from the arterioles to the venules** (pp. 461–463)

### Core claims
- The **microcirculation** = the segment from the first-order arteriole to the first-order venule. The capillary is the principal site for exchange of gases, water, nutrients, and waste; in most tissues, capillary flow is exclusively nutritional, but in some (glomerulus, skin AV anastomoses) it is largely non-nutritional (filtration, thermoregulation, signaling, host defense).
- **Architecture (Fig. 20-1):** a single arteriole and venule bracket a network of true capillaries, sometimes shortcut by a **metarteriole** (larger than a capillary, with discontinuous, mostly non-innervated VSMCs). **Precapillary sphincters** at the arteriole–capillary or metarteriole–capillary junction control access of blood to specific capillary segments. Sphincter open/close can change tissue blood flow by an order of magnitude or more.
- **Vessel-by-vessel architecture (radii and wall structure):**
  - **Terminal arteries / feed arteries:** inner radius ≥ 25 μm; endothelium + internal elastic lamina + ≥ 2 continuous layers of innervated VSMCs.
  - **Arterioles:** 5–25 μm; single continuous layer of innervated VSMCs.
  - **Metarterioles:** shorter; discontinuous, mostly not innervated VSMCs.
  - **Precapillary sphincter:** small cuff of smooth muscle, usually not innervated, very responsive to local conditions.
  - **True capillaries:** 2–5 μm; single endothelial layer + basement membrane + reticular collagen ± pericytes; endothelium 200–300 nm thick except at the nucleus.
  - **Venules:** 5–25 μm; discontinuous layer of VSMCs (some local flow control + some solute exchange).
- **Caveolae and transcytosis.** Some endothelial cells carry **caveolae** (see Ch 2) on luminal and basal surfaces for ligand binding; receptor- and fluid-phase endocytosis generates ~70-nm caveolin-coated vesicles. Pinocytotic vesicles can string together and even form **transendothelial channels**.
- **Interendothelial junctions (Fig. 20-2).** Cleft width ~10 nm, narrowing to ~4-nm adhering junctions and to **tight junctions** sealed by claudins 1, 3, and 5 (CLDN1/3/5) plus occludin. **CLDN5 is endothelium-specific.** Most brain capillaries lack paracellular gaps in this strand — the structural basis for the blood-brain barrier (BBB).
- **Three capillary types (Fig. 20-3):**
  1. **Continuous** — most common; clefts 10–15 nm wide (e.g., skeletal muscle). In the **BBB**, clefts are replaced by narrow tight junctions.
  2. **Fenestrated** — 50- to 80-nm cylindrical membrane-lined holes (**fenestrations**) often capped by a thin diaphragm. Found where large fluid/solute fluxes are required: intestine, choroid plexus, exocrine glands, renal glomerulus.
  3. **Sinusoidal (discontinuous)** — large fenestrations plus **gaps 100–1000 nm** between adjacent cells; found in liver, bone marrow, spleen.

## Section 2 — Capillary exchange of solutes (pp. 463–467)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The exchange of O₂ and CO₂ across capillaries depends on the diffusional properties of the surrounding tissue** (pp. 463–464)
- **The O₂ extraction ratio of a whole organ depends primarily on blood flow and metabolic demand** (p. 464)
- **According to Fick's law, the diffusion of small water-soluble solutes across a capillary wall depends on both the permeability and the concentration gradient** (pp. 464–465)
- **The whole-organ extraction ratio for small hydrophilic solutes provides an estimate of the solute permeability of capillaries** (pp. 465–466)
- **Small polar molecules have a relatively low permeability because they can traverse the capillary wall only by diffusing through water-filled pores (small-pore effect)** (pp. 466–467)
- **The exchange of macromolecules across capillaries can occur by transcytosis (large-pore effect)** (p. 467)

### Core claims — gas exchange and Krogh's tissue cylinder
- **Gases (O₂, CO₂, NO)** cross the capillary by a **transcellular** route through the two endothelial membranes and cytosol, with permeability essentially identical to that of surrounding tissue.
- **Krogh's tissue cylinder model (Fig. 20-4A):** each capillary "owns" a coaxial cylinder of tissue of radius $r_t$ ≈ half the mean intercapillary distance, and supplies it with O₂ by radial diffusion as blood flows axially. Eight factors set the $P_{O_2}(x, r)$ field: (1) arterial $[O_2]_{\text{dissolved}}$, (2) total $O_2$ content (mostly Hb-bound, ~20 mL O₂/dL), (3) capillary blood flow $F$, (4) radial diffusion coefficient $D_r$, (5) capillary radius $r_c$, (6) tissue radius $r_t$, (7) tissue O₂ consumption $\dot Q_{O_2}$, (8) axial position $x$.
- **Capillary density** correlates with O₂ demand: very high in myocardium and lungs, low in joint cartilage. In **skeletal muscle**, ~5 × 10⁵ capillaries/g exist but only ~20% are perfused at rest (Fig. 20-4B). Exercise opens precapillary sphincters → density of perfused capillaries rises → effective $r_t$ shrinks → tissue $P_{O_2}$ rises (Fig. 20-4C).

### Core claims — extraction ratio (whole-organ Fick)
- For a substance leaving the capillary (e.g., O₂), the **a–v difference** = $[X]_a - [X]_v$. Normalized to arterial content, this is the **extraction ratio**:

$$E_X = \frac{[X]_a - [X]_v}{[X]_a}$$

For O₂ with $[O_2]_a = 20$ mL/dL and $[O_2]_v = 15$ mL/dL, $E_{O_2} = 5/20 = 0.25$ (25%).
- The whole-organ Fick principle (Eq. 20-3) rearranges to:

$$[O_2]_a - [O_2]_v = \frac{\dot Q_{O_2}}{F} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E_{O_2} = \frac{\dot Q_{O_2}}{F \cdot [O_2]_a}$$

so extraction ratio **rises with metabolic demand** and **falls with flow**.

### Core claims — small water-soluble solutes (Fick's law for diffusion across the capillary wall)
- For hydrophilic solutes smaller than albumin, the pathway is **paracellular**: clefts, fenestrae, gaps (where present). Fick's law (Eq. 20-4):

$$J_X = P_X \cdot ([X]_c - [X]_{\text{if}})$$

with $J_X$ in mol·cm⁻²·s⁻¹, $P_X = D_X / a$ (permeability coefficient, cm·s⁻¹; $D_X$ is the in-wall diffusion coefficient, $a$ is wall thickness). Because capillary surface area is rarely known, the operational quantity is **mass flow**:

$$Q_X = S \cdot J_X = P_X \cdot S \cdot ([X]_c - [X]_{\text{if}}) \quad \text{(Eq. 20-5)}$$

- **Whole-organ permeability–surface-area product.** Combining the Fick principle with Fick's law gives the **Renkin-Crone-style relation** for extraction of a hydrophilic solute (Eq. 20-7):

$$E_X = 1 - e^{-(P_X \cdot S)/F}$$

So a measured $E_X$ and an independent $F$ yield $P_X \cdot S$ for the whole organ. Table 20-1 (inulin) shows that **$P_X \cdot S$ varies ~40-fold from resting skeletal muscle to heart** — mostly because $S$ (number of open capillaries) differs ~10-fold, with $P_X$ itself only ~4-fold higher in heart (denser fluid-filled clefts).

| Tissue (rabbit) | $P_X \cdot S$ for inulin (×10⁵ cm³/s/g) | $S$ (cm²/g) | $P_X$ (×10⁵ cm/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart | 4.08 | 800 | 5.1 |
| Lung | 3.80 | 950 | 4.0 |
| Small intestine | 1.79 | 460 | 3.9 |
| Diaphragm | 0.76 | 400 | 1.9 |
| Ear | 0.34 | 58 | 5.9 |
| Skeletal muscle (rest) | 0.09 | 79 | 1.2 |

- **BBB exception.** Cerebral capillary tight junctions (CLDN5 + occludin) permit essentially no paracellular flux of sucrose or inulin, so $P_X$ for hydrophilics is extremely low — but water permeability is comparable to other organs because water crosses the endothelial cells via AQP1.
- **$P_X \cdot S$ is dynamic.** Arteriolar/sphincter tone changes $S$; cytokines (e.g., histamine from mast cells, mediators of inflammation) widen interendothelial clefts and raise $P_X$.

### Core claims — small-pore effect and the glycocalyx fiber matrix
- Permeability falls sharply with solute Stokes-Einstein radius (Table 20-2, cat skeletal muscle, $S \approx 70$ cm²/g):

| Substance | Equivalent radius (nm) | Permeability (×10⁵ cm/s) |
|---|---|---|
| NaCl | 0.14 | ~3110 |
| Urea | 0.16 | ~2320 |
| Glucose | 0.36 | ~370 |
| Sucrose | 0.44 | ~250 |
| Raffinose | 0.56 | ~190 |
| Inulin | 1.62 | ~25 |

- Classical interpretation (Pappenheimer, two-pore model): a numerous population of **small pores** with equivalent radius ~3 nm + a smaller population of **large pores** ≥ 10 nm. Modern interpretation: small pores correspond to **discontinuities in tight-junction strands** in series with a **fiber matrix** of glycoproteins / **glycocalyx** on the luminal side and clefts on the abluminal side (Fig. 20-6). The endothelium-specific adhesion molecules **VE-cadherin (CDH5)** and **PECAM1 (CD31)** are glycoprotein components of the paracellular fiber matrix.
- **Charge selectivity.** Negative fixed charges in the glycocalyx make apparent permeability of negatively charged proteins much lower than that of equivalent neutral macromolecules, and that of positively charged macromolecules higher. (The same charge-selective sieve, in a more extreme form, dominates the glomerular filtration barrier — Ch 34.)
- $P_X$ also **rises along the capillary** from arteriolar to venular end, because clefts widen and fenestrae become more common — so for the same $([X]_c - [X]_{\text{if}})$, solute flux is higher at the venous end.

### Core claims — large-pore effect (transcytosis)
- Macromolecules with radius > 1 nm cross by (i) low-rate diffusion through wide clefts/fenestrae/gaps where present and (ii) **caveolar transcytosis** ("large-pore" pathway). Transcytosis is a five-step ferry: luminal equilibration → vesicle pinch-off → cytoplasmic shuttling/transient fusion → fusion with the abluminal membrane → abluminal equilibration.
- **Sieving.** Apparent permeability falls steeply with molecular radius (Table 20-3): myoglobin (1.9 nm) > albumin (3.5 nm) ≫ ferritin (6.1 nm, ~0). Probable mechanisms: steric hindrance at the necks of nascent vesicles and exclusion by the glycocalyx mesh. Ferritin is internalized but only a tiny fraction is delivered abluminally; the rest is degraded.
- The BBB has both continuous tight junctions **and** low transcytosis — its very low macromolecular permeability follows.
- **Solvent drag** (convective solute movement) is minor for small solutes with high $P_X$ (e.g., glucose) but is significant for macromolecules across the large-pore pathway.

- > "The Fick principle confirms our intuition that the extraction ratio should increase with increasing metabolic demand but decrease with increasing flow." (p. 464)

## Section 3 — Capillary exchange of water: the Starling principle (pp. 467–474)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Fluid transfer across capillaries is convective and depends on net hydrostatic and osmotic forces (i.e., Starling forces)** (pp. 467–468)
- **Capillary blood pressure ($P_c$) falls from ~35 mm Hg at the arteriolar end to ~15 mm Hg at the venular end** (p. 469)
- **Interstitial fluid pressure ($P_{\text{if}}$) is slightly negative, except in encapsulated organs** (pp. 469–470)
- **Capillary colloid osmotic pressure ($\pi_c$), which reflects the presence of plasma proteins, is ~25 mm Hg** (p. 470)
- **Interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure ($\pi_{\text{if}}$) varies between 0 and 10 mm Hg among different organs** (pp. 470–472)
- **The Starling principle predicts ultrafiltration at the arteriolar end and absorption at the venular end of most capillary beds** (pp. 472–473)
- **For continuous capillaries, the endothelial barrier for fluid exchange is more complex than considered by Starling** (pp. 472–474)

### Equations

- **Classical Starling equation** (Eq. 20-8) for volume flux $J_v$ (cm³·cm⁻²·s⁻¹) and total flow $F = J_v \cdot S_f$:

$$J_v = L_p \big[ (P_c - P_{\text{if}}) - \sigma (\pi_c - \pi_{\text{if}}) \big]$$

  $L_p$ = hydraulic conductivity (cm·s⁻¹·(mm Hg)⁻¹); $\sigma$ = average colloid osmotic reflection coefficient (0–1); $S_f$ = functional surface area. Positive $J_v$ = filtration out of capillary; negative $J_v$ = absorption.

- **van 't Hoff theoretical colloid osmotic pressure** (Eq. 20-9):

$$\Delta\pi_{\text{theory}} = R T \Delta [X]$$

  Plasma proteins, being imperfectly reflected, give $\Delta\pi_{\text{obs}} = \sigma \cdot \Delta\pi_{\text{theory}}$. For plasma proteins $\sigma$ is close to 1 in tight continuous capillaries (highest in brain), intermediate in muscle, and lowest in discontinuous beds (liver).

- **Net filtration pressure** (the bracketed term in Eq. 20-8):

$$\text{NFP} = (P_c - P_{\text{if}}) - \sigma (\pi_c - \pi_{\text{if}})$$

  NFP > 0 → filtration (ultrafiltration when $\sigma$ for proteins is 1). NFP < 0 → absorption.

- **Glycocalyx-modified ("revised") Starling** (Eq. 20-11): the relevant reference compartment is the **subglycocalyx fluid** (subscript $sg$), not the bulk interstitium:

$$J_v = L_p \big[ (P_c - P_{sg}) - \sigma (\pi_c - \pi_{sg}) \big]$$

  At low or zero net flow, $P_{sg} \to P_{\text{if}}$ and $\pi_{sg} \to \pi_{\text{if}}$ and the equation reduces to the classical form. During ultrafiltration, $P_{sg} > P_{\text{if}}$ and $\pi_{sg} < \pi_{\text{if}}$ (proteins get washed out of the cleft) — both effects oppose further filtration. During attempted absorption, water leaves the subglycocalyx space first, raising $\pi_{sg}$, which rapidly halts inward flow ("osmotic asymmetry" or **rectification**).

- **Small solutes ($\sigma \approx 0$) drop out of the Starling equation across the capillary wall.** They have full osmotic effect across the plasma membrane (where $\sigma_{\text{NaCl}} \approx 1$) — see Ch 5.

### Core claims — the four pressures and their typical values
**Table 20-4** (terms in the Starling equation, paraphrased): $J_v$ (cm³·cm⁻²·s⁻¹), $L_p$, $P_c$, $P_{\text{if}}$, $\pi_c$, $\pi_{\text{if}}$, $\sigma$, $F$, $S_f$.

- **Capillary hydrostatic pressure $P_c$.** In human skin, $P_c$ ≈ **35 mm Hg arteriolar end → ~15 mm Hg venular end**; with arteriolar $P_a$ = 60 and venular $P_v$ = 15, midcapillary $P_c$ ≈ **25** mm Hg, not the 37.5 arithmetic mean, because precapillary resistance exceeds postcapillary resistance ($R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}} \approx 0.3$). Hence:

| Manipulation (Table 20-5) | $P_a$ | $P_v$ | $P_c$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control | 60 | 15 | 25 |
| +10 mm Hg arteriolar | 70 | 15 | 27 (+2) |
| +10 mm Hg venular | 60 | 25 | 33 (+8) |

  **A given pressure rise at the venous end raises $P_c$ four times as much as the same rise at the arterial end** — the hemodynamic basis for venous-congestion edema (right-heart failure → dependent edema; left-heart failure → pulmonary edema).

  Tissue-specific $P_c$: **glomerular ≈ 50 mm Hg** (required for filtration; Ch 34); **retinal** capillaries are also high to keep patent against ~20 mm Hg vitreous pressure; **pulmonary** capillaries unusually low (**5–15 mm Hg**) to prevent alveolar edema. $P_c$ also varies with **gravity** (a capillary bed below the heart has higher $P_c$) and with **vasomotion** (precapillary sphincter oscillation).

- **Interstitial fluid pressure $P_{\text{if}}$.** Slightly **negative** in loose tissues (~−2 mm Hg in skin, subcutaneous tissue, lung). Verified by Guyton's implanted-capsule technique (Fig. 20-7), which gives values of **−2 to −10 mm Hg** after the wound heals; the wick-in-needle technique gives similar subatmospheric values. **Positive** in encapsulated organs: +1 to +3 mm Hg in kidney parenchyma, +10 mm Hg in Bowman's space (the relevant outside compartment for glomerular capillaries), variable in alveoli during the respiratory cycle. The interstitium has **biphasic compliance**: stiff at low volume (a small added volume drives $P_{\text{if}}$ up sharply) and very compliant once gels are disrupted (large volumes can accumulate at only small further pressure rises) — the structural reason loose subcutaneous tissue accommodates so much edema fluid.

- **Capillary colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_c$.** Plasma protein concentration ≈ 7.0 g/dL ≈ 1.5 mM total. van 't Hoff (with $\sigma$ ≈ 1 for albumin and globulins) predicts ~28 mm Hg; observed $\pi_c \approx$ **25 mm Hg** (Fig. 20-8). Because **albumin** is small (lower MW than the globulins) and abundant, it dominates $\pi_c$; replacing 1 g of γ-globulin with 1 g of albumin raises $\pi_c$ (because the number of particles per gram is higher). $\pi_c$ rises **steeper than linearly** with protein concentration (non-ideal solution + Donnan asymmetry that retains extra cations and raises lumen osmolality). $\pi_c$ changes little along the capillary because <1% of plasma volume is filtered. **The Donnan effect** (Ch 5, Fig. 5-15) adds to $\pi_c$ because plasma proteins carry net negative charge, retaining extra cations in plasma.

- **Interstitial fluid colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_{\text{if}}$.** Estimated from lymph protein content: 1–3 g/dL in leg, 3–4 g/dL in intestine, 4–6 g/dL in liver, giving $\pi_{\text{if}}$ ≈ 3–15 mm Hg. Average $\pi_{\text{if}}$ ≈ 3 mm Hg. $\pi_{\text{if}}$ **rises along the capillary axis** (Table 20-6) because protein-free filtrate enters interstitium upstream while protein-free absorbate leaves it downstream.

**Table 20-6 — Starling forces along a continuous capillary in loose tissue:**

| Location | $P_c$ | $P_{\text{if}}$ | $\sigma \pi_c$ | $\sigma \pi_{\text{if}}$ | NFP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arteriolar end | +35 | −2 | +25 | +0.1 | **+12** mm Hg (filtration) |
| Venular end | +15 | −2 | +25 | +3 | **−5** mm Hg (absorption) |

  Worked classical Starling at the two ends:

$$J_v^{\text{art}} = L_p \big[(35 - (-2)) - (25 - 0.1)\big] = L_p \cdot (+12 \text{ mm Hg}) \quad \text{(Eq. 20-10)}$$

$$J_v^{\text{ven}} = L_p \big[(15 - (-2)) - (25 - 3)\big] = L_p \cdot (-5 \text{ mm Hg})$$

  Filtration upstream, absorption downstream, with $L_p$ itself rising toward the venular end (clefts widen). The Landis–Pappenheimer book-keeping with these values gave ≈ 20 L/day filtered, 16–18 L/day absorbed, **net ≈ 2–4 L/day to lymphatics**. These numerical estimates are now considered overestimates because (i) $P_c$ values were not corrected for gravity or vasomotion, (ii) the classical model ignored the glycocalyx, and (iii) $P_{\text{if}}$ and $\pi_{\text{if}}$ are not invariant.

### Core claims — the revised glycocalyx model (Fig. 20-10)
- **The semipermeable barrier that reflects proteins is the luminal glycocalyx**, not the full endothelium. The relevant abluminal reference is the **subglycocalyx fluid** at the top of the paracellular cleft, a *third* compartment between the glycocalyx and the bulk interstitium.
- **Predictions:**
  - **During ultrafiltration:** $P_{sg} > P_{\text{if}}$ (cleft pressurized) and $\pi_{sg} < \pi_{\text{if}}$ (proteins washed out). Both oppose further filtration → measured filtration rates are **lower than the classical Starling estimate**.
  - **Near zero net flow:** revised model collapses to classical Starling.
  - **During absorption:** water reaching the lumen is replaced in the subglycocalyx space by interstitial water, but proteins concentrate in the cleft, raising $\pi_{sg}$ and quickly halting absorption. Hence the **rectification / osmotic asymmetry** between the two flow directions: a given $|$NFP$|$ produces a larger flux for filtration than for absorption.
- Consequence: most continuous capillaries are in a state of **net low-grade filtration along their whole length**, with the lymphatics — not "venular absorption" — being the principal sink. Sustained absorption is restricted to specialized beds (e.g., intestinal mucosa, where low $P_c$ + high $\pi_c$ → continuous absorption; the glomerulus is the opposite extreme — sustained filtration along the whole capillary).

### Clinical box — Interstitial Edema (Box 20-1, pp. 471)

Edema = excess of salt and water in the extracellular space, particularly the interstitium. Generalized (often first apparent as facial puffiness) or localized (often dependent: legs in heart failure). The Starling framework predicts four mechanism classes:

- **High $P_c$:**
  - **Prolonged standing** → gravity raises venous and capillary pressure in legs; muscle pump usually returns the fluid through veins/lymphatics, but failure to contract → orthostatic edema.
  - **Left-sided heart failure** → ↑ pulmonary venous pressure → ↑ pulmonary capillary $P_c$ → pulmonary edema (↓ lung compliance, ↓ gas exchange).
  - **Right-sided heart failure** → ↑ central venous pressure → ↑ systemic capillary $P_c$ → dependent edema, hepatic + intestinal transudate → **ascites**.
- **Low $\pi_c$ (hypoproteinemia):**
  - **Nephrotic syndrome** (urinary protein loss).
  - **Protein malnutrition / impaired hepatic synthesis** (cirrhosis).
  - **Pregnancy** (plasma volume expansion outstrips protein synthesis).
- **↑ Permeability / ↓ $\sigma$ (capillary wall failure):**
  - **Inflammation** — histamine, cytokines widen interendothelial clefts, ↑ $L_p$, ↓ $\sigma$ → "swelling" of cardinal-signs fame.
  - **Cerebral edema** after head injury — BBB breakdown in a rigid skull → microcirculatory occlusion.
  - **Ischemia / reperfusion** — endothelial deterioration → ↑ $L_p$, ↓ $\sigma$ → protein extravasation collapses the colloid gradient and aggravates edema.
- **Lymphatic obstruction:**
  - Post-mastectomy lymphadenectomy, malignant nodal infiltration, filariasis → local upstream edema.

## Section 4 — Lymphatics and the convective loops (pp. 474–477)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Lymphatics return excess interstitial fluid to the blood** (pp. 474–477)
- **The circulation of extracellular fluids involves three convective loops: blood, interstitial fluid, and lymph** (p. 477)

### Core claims
- **Initial vs. collecting lymphatics (Fig. 20-11).** Initial (terminal) lymphatics are thin-walled endothelial channels with few tight junctions; overlapping endothelial flaps act as **primary lymph valves** (one-way microvalves). **Anchoring filaments** tether the initial lymphatics to surrounding connective tissue so that interstitial swelling pulls the lumen open.
  - **Expansion phase (Fig. 20-11A):** $P_{\text{if}} > P_{\text{lymph}}$ → microvalves open → fluid enters; secondary lymph valves stay closed.
  - **Compression phase (Fig. 20-11B):** external pressure (skeletal-muscle contraction, respiration, peristalsis) raises $P_{\text{lymph}}$ → primary microvalves close, **secondary lymph valves** (downstream, like venous valves) open → fluid is propelled toward the veins.
- **Collecting lymphatics** are similar to small veins (endothelium + sparse smooth muscle); they carry pressures from +1 to +10 mm Hg, increasing stepwise at each valve. Smooth-muscle **vasomotion** in collecting lymphatics is driven by intrinsic myogenic responses: downstream occlusion → ↑ $P_{\text{lymph}}$ → ↑ contraction frequency; upstream occlusion → the opposite. Lymph nodes punctuate the path. Large lymphatics drain into the **left and right subclavian veins**.
- **Distribution.** Most organs have both initial and collecting lymphatics. Skeletal muscle and intestine contain only initial lymphatics. **Brain has no lymphatics**. Densest networks in skin and genitourinary, respiratory, and GI tracts.
- **Flow regulation (Fig. 20-12).** Lymph flow rises steeply with $P_{\text{if}}$ in the low-compliance range of interstitium ($-$2 to ~0 mm Hg) — efficient buffer. In the high-compliance range (already-edematous tissue, $P_{\text{if}}$ ≈ +2 to +4 mm Hg), the lymphatic flow–pressure curve plateaus, so lymphatic return **cannot scale up** to compensate further → "edema begets more edema." Mice lacking initial lymphatics (congenital lymphedema model) display elevated $P_{\text{if}}$ and $\pi_{\text{if}}$ + interstitial volume expansion.
- **Protein and cell return.** Lymphatic transport is the only route by which interstitial proteins return to blood (filtered proteins cannot diffuse against the inward $\pi_c$ gradient). Lymph carries leukocytes (no RBCs or platelets at rest). 100–200 g of protein/day — nearly the whole plasma protein content — moves into and back from interstitium each day.
- **The three convective loops (Fig. 20-13).**

| Loop | Daily flow | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cardiovascular | ~7200 L/day (CO 5 L/min) | The blood loop. |
| 2. Transvascular (capillary filtration–absorption) | ~20 L/day filtered at arteriolar end, ~16–18 L/day absorbed at venular end | Classical Landis–Pappenheimer numbers; modern data suggest both are overestimates. |
| 3. Lymphatic | 2–4 L/day | Difference between transvascular filtration and absorption. |
| (Diffusive exchange of H₂O) | ~80,000 L/day | An order of magnitude larger than CO; pure exchange, no net transfer. |
| (Diffusive exchange of glucose) | ~20,000 g/day diffuses; ~400 g/day metabolized; ~20 g/day enters by convection | Diffusion supplies the vast majority of metabolized glucose. |
| Protein flux into interstitium | 100–200 g/day via the large-pore (transcytotic) route + minor solvent drag | 95–195 g/day returns via lymph; ~5 g/day via venular solvent drag. |

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Lymphatics arise in the interstitium as small, thin-walled channels of endothelial cells that then join together to form increasingly larger vessels." (p. 474)
- > "Fluid does not normally accumulate in the interstitium because this excess fluid and protein move into the lymphatics. Thus, each day, the lymphatics return to the circulation 2 to 4 L of interstitial fluid, maintaining a steady state." (p. 475)
- > "Smooth-muscle contraction drives lymph toward the veins." (p. 475)
- > "Extracellular fluid moves in three convective loops … the cardiovascular loop … the transvascular loop … the lymphatic loop." (pp. 476–477)
- > "The diffusional exchange of water occurs at a much higher rate than does convective movement … ~80,000 L/day across all of the body's systemic capillaries." (p. 476)
- > "Nearly all of the filtered protein (95 to 195 g/day) depends on the convective lymphatic loop for its ultimate recovery." (p. 477)

## Section 5 — Regulation of the microcirculation (pp. 477–482)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The active contraction of vascular smooth muscle regulates precapillary resistance, which controls capillary blood flow** (pp. 477–478)
- **Tissue metabolites regulate local blood flow in specific vascular beds, independently of the systemic regulation** (pp. 478–479)
- **The endothelium of capillary beds is the source of several vasoactive compounds, including nitric oxide, endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factor, and endothelin** (pp. 480–481)
- **Autoregulation stabilizes blood flow despite large fluctuations in systemic arterial pressure** (p. 481)
- **Blood vessels proliferate in response to growth factors by a process known as angiogenesis** (pp. 481–482)

### Core claims — resistance decomposition and the VSMC contractile machinery

- The microcirculatory resistance has three series components (Eq. 20-12):

$$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{pre}} + R_{\text{cap}} + R_{\text{post}}$$

  Capillary flow:

$$F_{\text{cap}} = \frac{P_a - P_v}{R_{\text{pre}} + R_{\text{cap}} + R_{\text{post}}}$$

  Because $R_{\text{cap}}$ is small and $R_{\text{post}}/R_{\text{pre}} \approx 0.3$, $R_{\text{pre}}$ (arteriolar + sphincter tone) dominates total resistance — and **capillary flow is roughly inversely proportional to $R_{\text{pre}}$**.

- **VSMC contractile machinery** (extends Ch 9 smooth-muscle physiology):
  - **Trigger:** rise in [Ca²⁺]ᵢ → Ca²⁺·calmodulin → activation of **MLCK** → phosphorylation of regulatory **myosin light chain (MLC)** → myosin–actin interaction → contraction.
  - **MLCK regulation:** PKA (cAMP-driven) and PKG (cGMP-driven) **phosphorylate MLCK** → **inactivate** it → ↓ MLC-P → relaxation. Therefore intracellular **Ca²⁺, cAMP, and cGMP** are the three principal second-messenger axes for VSMC tone.
  - **Contraction** is favored by ↑ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ, ↓ [cAMP]ᵢ, or ↓ [cGMP]ᵢ.
  - **Relaxation** is favored by ↓ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ, ↑ [cAMP]ᵢ, or ↑ [cGMP]ᵢ.
- VSMCs are **multiunit** in most vessels (each cell separately innervated, contrast with unitary syncytial smooth muscle); they receive both neural (sympathetic / parasympathetic) and humoral inputs. **Vasomotion** = spontaneous rhythmic [Ca²⁺]ᵢ-driven oscillations of tone, generated by pacemaker currents or slow-wave depolarizations. Pharmacomechanical coupling allows humoral agents to raise [Ca²⁺]ᵢ without altering Vm.

### Core claims — neural and humoral mechanisms (Tables 20-7, 20-8)

- **Sympathetic vasoconstriction** (most beds): NE → α-AR → Gq → PLC → ↑ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ → contraction.
- **Sympathetic vasoconstriction (variant):** NE → α-AR → Gi → ↓ AC → ↓ cAMP → ↓ PKA → ↓ MLCK-P → ↑ MLCK activity → contraction.
- **Cotransmission** of NE + ATP (P2X) + NPY (Y1R) in some beds.
- **β₂ vasodilation** (skeletal muscle): epinephrine → β₂-AR → Gs → ↑ AC → ↑ cAMP → ↑ PKA → ↑ MLCK-P (inactivation) → relaxation.
- **Parasympathetic vasodilation** in erectile tissue: ACh on **endothelial** M₃ → ↑ [Ca²⁺] → eNOS → NO → VSMC → sGC → ↑ cGMP → PKG → relaxation (+ co-release of VIP → VIPR1/2 → Gs → cAMP path; + presynaptic M₂ inhibition of NE release).
- **Parasympathetic vasodilation in salivary glands** and **sympathetic cholinergic vasodilation in sweat glands**: ACh → M₃ on gland cell → ↑ kallikrein → ↑ bradykinin → B₂ on endothelial cell → NO.
- **Major humoral vasoconstrictors** (Table 20-8): angiotensin II (AT₁); arginine vasopressin (V1a); serotonin (5-HT₂); NPY (Y1R); endothelin-1 (ET_A); thromboxane A₂ (TP); ATP (P2X, ligand-gated Ca²⁺ channel); adenosine (A₁, via Gi → ↓ cAMP, in some beds also A₃).
- **Major humoral vasodilators** (Table 20-8): histamine (H₂ → ↑ cAMP); VIP (VIPR1/2 → ↑ cAMP, also opens K⁺ channels → hyperpolarization); ANP (NPR1 / particulate guanylyl cyclase → ↑ cGMP → PKG); NO (via soluble GC, see below); PGI₂ and PGE₂ (IP and EP₂/EP₄ → ↑ cAMP); bradykinin (B₂R on endothelium → eNOS + PLA₂ → PGI₂); ATP via metabotropic P2Y → eNOS; adenosine via A₂A/A₂B → ↑ cAMP, K_ATP-channel opening → hyperpolarization.

### Core claims — local metabolic control (Table 20-9)

In **systemic** vessels, the following changes vasodilate (in the **pulmonary** circulation the opposite, see Ch 31):
- **↓ PO₂** (active hyperemia): ↓ [ATP]ᵢ → adenosine release → A₂ → vasodilation; also ↑ PGI₂ and ↑ NO release.
- **↑ PCO₂ / ↓ pH** (interstitial acidosis): vasodilation.
- **↑ [K⁺]ₒ:** transient hyperpolarization closes voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → vasodilation.
- **↑ [Lactate]ₒ:** acidifies interstitium → vasodilation.
- **↓ [ATP]ᵢ:** opens K_ATP channels → hyperpolarization → vasodilation.
- **↑ [ATP]ₒ, [ADP]ₒ:** activate P2Y purinergic receptors.
- **↑ [Adenosine]ₒ:** A₂ receptor → ↑ cAMP → vasodilation.

These mechanisms produce two stereotyped local responses:
- **Active hyperemia.** Metabolic activity (e.g., exercising muscle) → local accumulation of vasodilator metabolites → increased local blood flow matched to demand.
- **Reactive hyperemia.** Following a period of arterial occlusion, accumulated metabolic vasodilators (+ myogenic relaxation from low transmural pressure) drive a transient over-perfusion when flow is restored. (Discussed quantitatively in Ch 24.)

### Core claims — endothelium-derived vasoactive factors (Table 20-10)

**Vasodilators**

- **Nitric oxide (NO; originally EDRF).** Bradykinin, ACh, and ↑ shear stress activate the constitutively expressed **endothelial NOS (eNOS / NOS III)**, which converts L-arginine → NO (Ca²⁺/CaM-dependent). NO diffuses to the VSMC, activates **soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC)** → ↑ cGMP → PKG, with three converging effects: (i) phosphorylates and inactivates MLCK → ↓ MLC-P; (ii) phosphorylates SERCA → ↓ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ; (iii) phosphorylates BK_Ca channels → hyperpolarization. Pharmacological exploitation: organic nitrates (nitroglycerin) release NO to relieve angina; **sildenafil (Viagra)** inhibits PDE5 → ↑ cGMP → smooth-muscle relaxation in corpus cavernosum (and pulmonary vasculature). NO also inhibits platelet aggregation and adhesion.
- **Endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factor (EDHF).** A separate factor (or family) released in response to ACh; hyperpolarizes VSMC → ↓ Ca²⁺ entry → relaxation. Particularly important in small resistance vessels.
- **Prostacyclin (PGI₂).** Cyclooxygenase + prostacyclin synthase → PGI₂ → IP receptor on VSMC → Gs → ↑ cAMP → ↓ MLCK activity. Critical for the perinatal pulmonary vasodilation that establishes pulmonary circulation at birth (Ch 57). PGE₂ acts on EP₂/EP₄ in a similar Gs/cAMP fashion.

**Vasoconstrictors**

- **Endothelin (ET).** A family of 21-residue peptides (ET-1, -2, -3) cleaved from preproendothelin → proendothelin → mature ET. **ET_A** on VSMC → Gq → PLC → IP₃ → ↑ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ → extremely potent and **long-lasting** vasoconstriction. **ET_B** mediates vasodilation in some beds (and constriction in others). Released by hypoxia and many pathological stimuli; central to the pathophysiology of pulmonary arterial hypertension (the basis of bosentan / ambrisentan therapy).
- **Thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂).** From endothelial cells and platelets via COX. TP receptor → opens L-type Ca²⁺ channels → ↑ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ → contraction; also ↑ superoxide (O₂⁻) → reacts with NO → ↓ NO bioavailability.
- **EDCF₁, EDCF₂.** Endothelium-derived constricting factors mobilized by anoxia (EDCF₁) and rapid stretch (EDCF₂); likely include superoxide-anion species (superoxide dismutase blocks the response).

### Core claims — myogenic response and autoregulation (Fig. 20-14)

- **Myogenic response (Bayliss effect).** Stretch of the VSMC membrane opens stretch-activated nonselective cation channels → depolarization → opens L-type Ca²⁺ channels → ↑ [Ca²⁺]ᵢ → **contraction**. Hence ↑ perfusion pressure → ↑ stretch → ↑ tone → ↑ resistance → constant flow.
- **Autoregulation.** In the autoregulatory range, a pressure step transiently increases flow (rigid-tube response) but VSMC tone then ratchets up over seconds to minutes via combined myogenic + metabolic adjustments (↑ tissue $P_{O_2}$ as more O₂ arrives also constricts), returning flow toward baseline. Outside the autoregulatory range, the vessel behaves more like an elastic (or even passively distensible) tube.
- **Where autoregulation is most prominent:** **kidney, brain, heart** (organs intolerant of ischemia and/or whose function depends on filtration). It is entirely local; it overrides — and persists despite the absence of — neural and endocrine inputs.

### Core claims — inflammation and leukocyte adhesion

(Embedded in the inflammation discussion of Box 20-1 and the endothelial-permeability section above.)
- Inflammatory mediators (histamine from mast cells; bradykinin; cytokines IL-1, TNF-α) → endothelial cell shape change → **widened interendothelial clefts** (↑ $L_p$, ↓ $\sigma$ for proteins) → protein-rich exudate.
- Endothelial activation upregulates **P-selectin** (rapid, from Weibel-Palade bodies) and **E-selectin** (slow, transcriptional) → leukocytes **roll**, then firmly adhere via integrins binding **ICAM-1 / VCAM-1**, then **transmigrate** through the cleft (diapedesis) — Ch 18 and immunology textbooks elaborate.
- The functional consequence at the Starling level: ↑ permeability + ↑ surface area (Sf) → ↑ filtration → tissue swelling (one of the cardinal signs of inflammation).

### Core claims — angiogenesis (Table 20-11)

- **Adult microcirculation is largely static**, with notable exceptions: wound healing, inflammation, tumor growth, cyclic endometrial remodeling, exercise training, altitude acclimatization.
- **Sequence of new-vessel growth:** dissolution of the venular basement membrane → activation and proliferation of quiescent endothelial cells → migration toward growth-factor sources → tube formation → connection of nascent tubes → smooth-muscle differentiation around the new tube.
- **Promoters:**
  - **VEGF** (PDGF family, endothelial RTK ligand) — produced by fibroblasts and many tumors; FVIIa promotes VEGF. **Avastin (bevacizumab)** is an anti-VEGF monoclonal used in non-small-cell lung cancer.
  - **FGF family** (22 members in humans; FGF2/bFGF especially angiogenic). Both VEGF and FGF2 ↑ NOS expression → NO → endothelial proliferation/migration.
  - **Angiopoietin 1 (ANGPT1) / Tie2 RTK** — required for embryonic vascular development.
  - **Angiogenin** — a plasma RNase; cancer raises plasma levels.
- **Inhibitors:**
  - **Angiostatin** — kringle fragment of plasminogen (proteolyzed by MMPs, elastase); promotes endothelial apoptosis and blocks tube formation. Trials in advanced lung cancer.
  - **Endostatin** — fragment of collagen XVIII produced by tumor ECM.
  - **ANGPT2** — natural antagonist of ANGPT1 at Tie2; required for postnatal angiogenic remodeling (despite the "inhibitor" label, its role is permissive remodeling rather than simple blockade).
- **Clinical relevance:**
  - **Coronary artery disease** — chronic ischemia promotes collateral vessel growth.
  - **Cancer** — tumor angiogenesis enables growth and metastasis (Folkman's hypothesis).
  - **Diabetic retinopathy** — pathological retinal neovascularization → blindness; treated with anti-VEGF.

- > "Intracellular Ca²⁺, cAMP, and cGMP are the principal second messengers responsible for modulating vascular tone." (p. 478)
- > "Myogenic regulation refers to an intrinsic mode of control of activity in which stretch of the VSMC membrane activates stretch-sensitive nonselective cation channels." (p. 478)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Microcirculation** — first-order arteriole through first-order venule.
- **Arteriole / metarteriole / precapillary sphincter / true capillary / venule** — vessel-class taxonomy with characteristic radii and wall structure.
- **Pericyte** — contractile/regulatory cell wrapping the capillary, especially prominent in CNS and retina.
- **Continuous / fenestrated / sinusoidal (discontinuous) capillary** — three structural classes (Fig. 20-3).
- **Fenestration / diaphragm / interendothelial cleft / gap** — paracellular pathway features.
- **Caveola / transcytosis / transendothelial channel** — vesicular pathway.
- **Tight junction / claudin (CLDN1, CLDN3, CLDN5) / occludin / VE-cadherin (CDH5) / PECAM1 (CD31)** — molecular components of the endothelial junction.
- **Blood-brain barrier (BBB)** — continuous tight junctions, near-zero paracellular permeability to hydrophilics, low transcytosis.
- **Krogh's tissue cylinder** — radial-diffusion model of capillary–tissue gas exchange.
- **a–v difference** — $[X]_a - [X]_v$.
- **Extraction ratio $E_X$** — $([X]_a - [X]_v)/[X]_a$; for hydrophilics: $E_X = 1 - e^{-P_X S / F}$.
- **Permeability coefficient $P_X$** — $D_X / a$ (cm·s⁻¹); $P_X \cdot S$ is the operational whole-organ exchange parameter.
- **Small-pore effect / large-pore effect** — paracellular cleft-fiber-matrix sieving vs. caveolar transcytosis.
- **Solvent drag** — convective solute transport; minor for small solutes, important for macromolecules.
- **Starling equation** — $J_v = L_p A [(P_c - P_{\text{if}}) - \sigma(\pi_c - \pi_{\text{if}})]$.
- **Hydraulic conductivity $L_p$** — water permeability of the capillary wall.
- **Reflection coefficient $\sigma$** — 0 (freely permeant) to 1 (perfectly reflected); ~1 for plasma proteins in most beds, 0 for NaCl across the endothelium.
- **Capillary hydrostatic pressure $P_c$** — ~35 mm Hg arteriolar, ~15 mm Hg venular in skin; ~50 in glomerulus; ~5–15 in pulmonary capillaries.
- **Interstitial fluid pressure $P_{\text{if}}$** — slightly subatmospheric in loose tissue, positive in encapsulated organs.
- **Colloid (oncotic) osmotic pressure $\pi_c, \pi_{\text{if}}$** — generated by plasma proteins (~25 mm Hg, dominated by albumin) vs. interstitial proteins (varies, ~3 mm Hg average).
- **Net filtration pressure (NFP)** — bracketed term in the Starling equation.
- **Ultrafiltration vs. absorption** — direction of $J_v$.
- **Subglycocalyx fluid / revised Starling / rectification** — modern refinement.
- **Edema** — interstitial fluid accumulation; classified by which Starling parameter is deranged (↑ $P_c$, ↓ $\pi_c$, ↑ $L_p$/↓ $\sigma$, lymphatic obstruction).
- **Initial vs. collecting lymphatics; primary vs. secondary lymph valves; anchoring filaments** — lymphatic architecture.
- **Three convective loops** — cardiovascular (7200 L/day), transvascular (filtration ~20 / absorption ~16–18 L/day), lymphatic (2–4 L/day).
- **Precapillary resistance $R_{\text{pre}}$** — dominant resistance in the microcirculation; sets capillary flow.
- **MLCK / MLC phosphorylation / Ca²⁺-calmodulin / PKA / PKG** — VSMC contractile control axis.
- **Vasomotion** — spontaneous rhythmic VSMC contractions.
- **Myogenic (Bayliss) response** — stretch-activated VSMC contraction.
- **Active hyperemia / reactive hyperemia** — metabolic and post-occlusion vasodilation.
- **Autoregulation** — pressure-independent flow over a defined range; prominent in kidney, brain, heart.
- **eNOS / NO / sGC / cGMP / PKG / sildenafil (PDE5 inhibitor)** — endothelial vasodilator axis.
- **EDHF, PGI₂, PGE₂** — additional endothelial vasodilators.
- **Endothelin (ET-1, ET_A, ET_B) / TXA₂ / EDCF** — endothelial vasoconstrictors.
- **Selectins (P, E) / ICAM-1 / VCAM-1 / leukocyte rolling, adhesion, diapedesis** — inflammatory recruitment cascade.
- **Angiogenesis / VEGF / FGF2 / ANGPT1–2 / Tie2 / angiostatin / endostatin** — vessel-growth biology.
