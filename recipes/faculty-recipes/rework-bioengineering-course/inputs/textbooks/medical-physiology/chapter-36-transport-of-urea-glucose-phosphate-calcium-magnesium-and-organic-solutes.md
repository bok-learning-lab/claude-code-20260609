# Chapter 36 — Transport of Urea, Glucose, Phosphate, Calcium, Magnesium, and Organic Solutes

> Section VI · The Urinary System · pp. 770–791 · Authors: Gerhard Giebisch, Erich E. Windhager, Peter S. Aronson

## Chapter map (top-level)

This is a long, multi-solute chapter. It is organized below as one section per solute class.

1. **Urea** (pp. 770–772) — filtered, reabsorbed (PT + IMCD), and secreted (thin limbs); UT-A/UT-B transporters; medullary recycling; flow dependence of urea excretion; the BUN/creatinine ratio.
2. **Glucose** (pp. 772–774) — apical electrogenic Na/glucose cotransport (SGLT2 early, SGLT1 late) + basolateral GLUT facilitated diffusion; the titration curve (threshold, saturation/Tm, splay, clearance → GFR).
3. **Other organic solutes** (pp. 774–785) — amino acids (transporter table, hyperaminoacidurias), oligopeptides (PepT1/PepT2), proteins (megalin/cubilin endocytosis), mono-/di-/tricarboxylates, PAH secretion (Tm-limited, RPF measurement), urate (reabsorption + secretion), organic cations (OCT2/MATE), nonionic diffusion (pH-dependent excretion of weak acids/bases).
4. **Phosphate** (pp. 785–787) — apical Na/phosphate cotransport (NaPi-IIa/IIc, PiT-2); titration with Tm at the top of normal; PTH, FGF23/phosphatonins, vitamin D regulation.
5. **Calcium** (pp. 787–789) — paracellular reabsorption (PT, TAL) vs. transcellular (DCT: TRPV5, calbindin, PMCA1b/NCX1); PTH, vitamin D, CaSR, diuretic effects.
6. **Magnesium** (pp. 789–791) — paramount paracellular reabsorption in the TAL via claudin-16/19; transcellular DCT via TRPM6; regulation.

## Section 1 — Urea (pp. 770–772)

### Subsection headings
- **The kidney filters, reabsorbs, and secretes urea** (pp. 770–771)
- **Urea excretion rises with increasing urinary flow** (p. 772)

### Core claims
- **Normal plasma urea** is 2.5–6 mM; clinical labs report it as **blood urea nitrogen (BUN)** in (mg elemental N)/dL, normal **7–18 mg/dL**. Urea is the primary nitrogenous end-product of amino-acid catabolism (liver, from NH₃); urinary urea excretion is ~450 mmol/day on a Western diet.
- Urea is **freely filtered, then both reabsorbed and secreted**. Because reabsorption exceeds secretion, net excretion is less than filtered. At average urine flow the kidneys excrete **~40%** of the filtered urea.
- **Reabsorption sites**: proximal tubule and medullary collecting duct. **Secretion sites**: thin limbs of the loop of Henle.
- **Proximal tubule**: luminal [urea] starts equal to plasma; water reabsorption raises luminal [urea], generating a gradient that drives **passive reabsorption** (transcellular or paracellular diffusion) plus **solvent drag** across tight junctions. More fluid reabsorption → more urea reabsorption.
- **Urea transporters (facilitated diffusion)**: the *SLC14A2* gene encodes **UT-A1, UT-A2, UT-A3** (splice variants); **UT-B1/UT-B2** are encoded by *SLC14A1*. UT-A2 is the prototypical ~55-kDa, 10-TM glycoprotein; UT-A1 is a 97-kDa, 20-TM protein (essentially UT-A3 linked to UT-A2 via an intracellular loop). UT-B1/UT-B2 sit in the descending vasa recta.
- **Thin descending limb (tDLH) / thin ascending limb (tALH)** — secretion: in juxtamedullary nephrons, near the loop tip the medullary interstitial [urea] exceeds luminal [urea], so the deep tDLH **secretes** urea via UT-A2; the tALH continues to secrete. In superficial nephrons the entire tDLH may carry UT-A2 and secrete. Net effect: urea delivery rises to **~110% of the filtered load** at the cortical collecting ducts.
- **Inner medullary collecting duct (IMCD)** — transcellular reabsorption: **UT-A1** moves urea across the apical membrane; **UT-A3** probably mediates basolateral exit. **AVP (ADH), acting via cAMP, stimulates UT-A1 and UT-A3** — the link to the urinary concentrating mechanism (developed pp. 811–814).
- **Flow dependence**: because urea transport is concentration-gradient driven, **excretion rises with urine flow**. At low flow (much water + urea reabsorbed) only ~15% of filtered urea is excreted; at high flow up to ~70%. In volume depletion, urine flow falls and urea excretion drops out of proportion to GFR, so **BUN/creatinine ratio rises above its normal ~10** (both in mg/dL) — laboratory confirmation of volume depletion. Creatinine clearance, like inulin, is flow-independent.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Because the tubules reabsorb more urea than they secrete, the amount of urea excreted in the urine is less than the quantity filtered." (p. 770)
- > "The primary sites for urea reabsorption are the proximal tubule and the medullary collecting ducts, whereas the primary sites for secretion are the thin limbs of the loop of Henle." (p. 770)
- > "Arginine vasopressin (AVP)—which is also known as antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and acts through cAMP … stimulates UT-A1 and UT-A3." (p. 770)
- > "Because urea transport depends primarily on urea concentration differences across the tubule epithelium, changes in urine flow unavoidably affect renal urea handling." (p. 772)
- > "In clinical conditions such as volume depletion … urea excretion decreases out of proportion to the fall in GFR. The resulting high BUN can thus serve as laboratory confirmation of volume depletion." (p. 772)
- > "The plasma [BUN]/[creatinine] ratio increases from its normal value of ~10 (both concentrations in mg/dL)." (p. 772)

## Section 2 — Glucose (pp. 772–774)

### Subsection headings
- **The proximal tubule reabsorbs glucose via apical, electrogenic Na/glucose cotransport and basolateral facilitated diffusion** (pp. 772–773)
- **Glucose excretion in the urine occurs only when the plasma concentration exceeds a threshold** (pp. 773–774)

### Core claims
- **Fasting plasma glucose** 4–5 mM (70–100 mg/dL). Glucose is freely filtered and almost completely reabsorbed (mostly in the first third of the PT), so only trace amounts appear in urine.
- **Transcellular, two-step transport**: apical **Na/glucose cotransport (SGLT)** concentrates glucose in the cell; basolateral **GLUT (SLC2 family)** facilitated diffusion exports it. The basolateral Na-K pump keeps [Na⁺]ᵢ low and the cell interior negative, so the Na⁺ electrochemical gradient drives uphill (secondary active) glucose uptake. **Phloridzin** inhibits the apical SGLTs; the GLUTs are relatively phloridzin-insensitive. Only D-glucose (not L-glucose) is carried.
- **Early PT (S1): SGLT2 (SLC5A2)** — high-capacity, low-affinity, **Na:glucose = 1:1**, responsible for **~90%** of glucose reabsorption; basolateral exit via **GLUT2**. *SGLT2 inhibitors are now used to treat diabetic hyperglycemia.*
- **Late PT (S3): SGLT1 (SLC5A1)** — high-affinity, low-capacity, **Na:glucose = 2:1**. The 2:1 stoichiometry delivers more electrochemical energy per glucose, generating a far larger transepithelial gradient and driving luminal [glucose] to near zero. Basolateral exit via **GLUT1**. (Panel notes: SGLT2 can accumulate glucose to [glucose]ᵢ/[glucose]ₗᵤₘₑₙ as high as ~70; SGLT1's 2:1 coupling can establish a ratio as high as ~4900.)
- Falling **paracellular glucose permeability** along the PT helps maintain the steep gradient and near-zero exit concentration.
- **Glucose titration curve — four key characteristics**: (1) **threshold** (~200 mg/dL, ~11 mM — glucose appears in urine only above this, well above the normal ~100 mg/dL); (2) **saturation / transport maximum (Tm)** ~400 mg/min (SGLTs fully saturated); (3) **splay** (Tm is approached gradually, not abruptly — reflects anatomical + kinetic heterogeneity among nephrons, e.g. mismatched filtered load vs. reabsorptive capacity, different SGLT distributions); (4) **clearance approaches GFR** at high plasma [glucose] (glucose then behaves like inulin). These four features also apply to amino acids, organic-anion metabolites (lactate, citrate, α-KG), PAH, and phosphate.

### Equations

For each solute handled by filtration + reabsorption, the three rates plotted in Fig. 36-4A are:

- **Filtered load** (orange): $$ \text{Filtered load} = \text{GFR} \times P_{X} $$ a straight line through the origin (constant GFR).
- **Excretion** (green): $$ E_X = \text{Filtered load} - \text{Reabsorption} = (\text{GFR}\times P_X) - R_X $$ zero below threshold; above threshold it rises and, once reabsorption saturates at $T_m$, runs parallel to (filtered load − $T_m$).
- **Reabsorption** (red): rises with filtered load until it plateaus at the **transport maximum** $T_m$ (for glucose ≈ 400 mg/min).
- **Clearance**: $$ C_X = \frac{E_X}{P_X} \xrightarrow[\;P_X \to \infty\;]{} C_{\text{inulin}} = \text{GFR} $$ as the reabsorbed amount becomes negligible relative to the filtered load.

## Section 3 — Other organic solutes (pp. 774–785)

### Subsection headings
- **The proximal tubule reabsorbs amino acids using a wide variety of apical and basolateral transporters** (pp. 774–777)
- **An H⁺-driven cotransporter takes up oligopeptides; endocytosis takes up proteins** (pp. 777–779)
- **Two separate apical Na⁺-driven cotransporters reabsorb monocarboxylates and di-/tricarboxylates** (pp. 779–780)
- **The proximal tubule secretes PAH and a variety of other organic anions** (pp. 780–781)
- **PAH secretion is a Tm-limited mechanism** (pp. 781–782)
- **The proximal tubule both reabsorbs and secretes urate** (pp. 782–783)
- **The late proximal tubule secretes several organic cations** (pp. 783–784)
- **Nonionic diffusion of neutral weak acids and bases explains pH-dependent excretion** (pp. 784–785)

### Core claims

**Amino acids.** Total plasma amino acids ~2.4 mM; freely filtered; PT reabsorbs **>98%** transcellularly using many transporters with overlapping specificity (Table 36-1). Apical entry is Na⁺-driven, H⁺-driven, or via exchangers; basolateral exit by exchangers (some Na⁺-dependent) and facilitated diffusion. Each amino acid must cross **both** an apical and a basolateral transporter. Examples: glutamate apical via **SLC1A1** (2 Na⁺ + H⁺ in, K⁺ out); Lys⁺/Arg⁺ apical via heterodimer **SLC7A9/SLC3A1** (exchange for neutral AA), basolateral via **SLC7A7/SLC3A2**; neutral AAs (not Pro) apical via **SLC6A19** (Na⁺). **Competitive inhibition** among structurally similar amino acids, and competition for the shared Na⁺ energy source, can blunt reabsorption (e.g. glucose uptake depolarizing the apical membrane slows Na⁺-driven AA uptake). **Tm values for amino acids are generally low**, so plasma elevations spill into urine. Hyperaminoacidurias (Table 36-2, Box 36-1): prerenal/overflow (hyperargininemia), competition (Lys/ornithine), renal (anionic aminoaciduria SLC1A1; **Hartnup disease** SLC6A19; **cystinuria** SLC7A9/SLC3A1 → cystine stones; **lysinuric protein intolerance** SLC7A7/SLC3A2), and generalized PT dysfunction (**Fanconi syndrome** — also spills glucose, HCO₃⁻, phosphate, low-MW proteins).

**Oligopeptides.** PT reabsorbs ~99%. Brush-border peptidases (γ-glutamyltransferase, aminopeptidases, endopeptidases, dipeptidases) hydrolyze luminal peptides (including angiotensin II); the cell takes up surviving 2–5-residue peptides (and resistant ones like carnosine) via apical **H⁺/oligopeptide cotransporters PepT1 (SLC15A1)** (early PT, low-affinity/high-capacity) and **PepT2 (SLC15A2)** (late PT, high-affinity/low-capacity) — analogous to SGLT2/SGLT1. Cytosolic peptidases then complete hydrolysis.

**Proteins.** Filtered albumin (4–20 mg/L, ~0.01–0.05% of plasma) amounts to 0.7–3.6 g/day; urinary albumin is normally only ~30 mg/day, so tubules reabsorb **96–99%** of filtered albumin. Reabsorption is by **receptor-mediated endocytosis** via the apical **megalin/cubilin/amnionless** receptor complex → clathrin-coated vesicles → endosomes → lysosomes (acid proteases) → basolateral release of amino acids. The kidney accounts for up to ~80% of metabolic clearance of small peptide hormones (insulin, PTH, ANP, glucagon); ESRD raises their levels.

**Mono-/di-/tricarboxylates.** Blood carboxylates 1–3 mM (lactate largest). Apical Na⁺-driven uptake: **monocarboxylates** (lactate, pyruvate, acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate) via **SLC5A8/SLC5A12**; **di-/tricarboxylates** (α-KG, malate, succinate, citrate) via **NaDC1 (SLC13A2)**. Basolateral exit: monocarboxylates via H⁺-coupled **MCT2 (SLC16A7)**; dicarboxylates via organic-anion exchangers **OAT1 (SLC22A6)/OAT3 (SLC22A8)**. Ketoacidosis (starvation/diabetes) can overwhelm the Tm and spill ketone bodies.

**PAH and organic-anion secretion.** PAH is ~20% protein-bound, so ~80% is filterable; at low plasma [PAH] the kidney excretes ~90% of arterial PAH in a single pass → basis for **renal plasma flow (RPF)** measurement. Secretion is mainly in the **late PT (S3)** by **tertiary active transport**: basolateral Na-K pump (primary) → NaDC3 (SLC13A3) accumulates α-KG (secondary, 3 Na⁺ : 1 dicarboxylate) → basolateral **OAT1/OAT3** exchange intracellular α-KG for PAH (tertiary). Apical exit by anion exchange / electrogenic facilitated diffusion (e.g. OATv1) or ABC transporter (MRP4). **Probenecid** competes and inhibits PAH secretion. The S3 also secretes a wide range of other organic anions (Table 36-3).

**PAH titration (Tm-limited).** Increasing plasma [PAH] makes excretion rise faster than filtration; net **secretion saturates** at a **Tm ~60–80 mg/min** (plateau near plasma [PAH] ~20 mg/dL). Above the Tm, further excretion rises only from increased filtered load, and PAH can no longer be used for RPF. **PAH clearance decreases** with rising plasma [PAH], **approaching inulin clearance** from above — opposite to glucose clearance, which approaches inulin clearance from below.

**Urate.** Plasma urate 3–7 mg/dL (0.2–0.4 mM), elevated in gout. Filtered, then both reabsorbed and secreted; reabsorption dominates so only **~10%** is excreted. Reabsorption: paracellular diffusion (enhanced in volume depletion → raises plasma urate) + transcellular active apical uptake via **URAT1 (SLC22A12)** (urate/monocarboxylate exchange), **OAT4 (SLC22A11)** and **OAT10 (SLC22A13)** (urate/dicarboxylate exchange), with basolateral exit by voltage-driven **URATv1 (SLC2A9)**. Secretion: basolateral OAT1/OAT3 uptake (exchange for α-KG), apical exit via **NPT1 (SLC17A1)**, **NPT4 (SLC17A3)**, and ABC transporters **MRP4 (ABCC4)**, **BCRP (ABCG2)**. **Uricosuric agents** (probenecid, salicylate, NSAIDs) inhibit URAT1 → increase urate excretion (probenecid treats gout).

**Organic cations.** Late PT secretes endogenous monoamine neurotransmitters (dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, histamine), creatinine, and exogenous cations (morphine, quinine, amiloride). Basolateral uptake by polyspecific **OCT2 (SLC22A2)** (facilitated diffusion driven by the inside-negative voltage); apical extrusion by organic-cation/H⁺ exchangers **MATE1 (SLC47A1)** and **MATE2-K (SLC47A2)** plus ABC transporter **MDR1 (ABCB1)**. Because the apical Na-H exchanger sets up the H⁺ gradient that powers the MATEs, cation extrusion is **tertiary active transport**.

**Nonionic diffusion (pH-dependent excretion).** Neutral species of weak acids/bases cross membranes far faster than their charged forms. **Acidifying the lumen** promotes reabsorption of a neutral weak acid (e.g. salicylic acid — A⁻ titrated to HA, which diffuses out) and secretion/retention of a neutral weak base. **Alkalinizing the lumen** promotes excretion of weak acids and reabsorption of weak bases (e.g. chloroquine). Clinical use: **alkalinize urine (HCO₃⁻) and/or increase flow** to treat salicylate/aspirin overdose. At urine pH ~7.5, C(total salicylate) ≈ GFR; lower pH drives FE(salicylate) → 0, higher pH raises it markedly.

## Section 4 — Phosphate (pp. 785–787)

### Subsection headings
- **The proximal tubule reabsorbs phosphate via apical Na/phosphate cotransporters** (pp. 785–786)
- **Phosphate excretion in the urine already occurs at physiological plasma concentrations** (p. 786)
- **PTH inhibits apical Na/phosphate uptake, promoting phosphate excretion** (pp. 786–787)
- **Fibroblast growth factor 23 and other phosphatonins also inhibit apical Na/phosphate uptake** (p. 787)

### Core claims
- Total plasma Pᵢ varies 0.8–1.5 mM (2.5–4.5 mg/dL elemental P): ~50% ionized, ~40% complexed to small solutes, 10–15% protein-bound (Table 36-4). Only free + complexed (~0.7–1.3 mM) is filterable. At pH 7.4, ~80% of ionized phosphate is **HPO₄²⁻**, the rest H₂PO₄⁻. Daily filtered load ~7000 mg — far exceeding the extracellular pool, so most must be reabsorbed.
- **PT reabsorbs 80–95%** of filtered phosphate (negligible distally); **5–20% excreted**. Transcellular: apical secondary active **Na/phosphate cotransport**, three transporters — **NaPi-IIa (SLC34A1)** (electrogenic, 3 Na⁺ : 1 HPO₄²⁻), **NaPi-IIc (SLC34A3)** (electroneutral, 2 Na⁺ : 1 HPO₄²⁻; prefers HPO₄²⁻ so **low luminal pH inhibits** reabsorption; most important in humans — its mutations cause phosphate wasting/hypophosphatemia), and **PiT-2 (SLC20A2)** (electrogenic, 2 Na⁺ : 1 H₂PO₄⁻). Basolateral exit mechanism is unknown.
- **Titration**: phosphate shares threshold, Tm saturation, and splay with glucose, but differs: (1) **some phosphate is excreted even at normal plasma levels** — the kidney sits near its Tm in the normal range, so small plasma increases sharply raise excretion (the kidney is a physiological phosphate regulator); (2) the **phosphate Tm is reached at the high end of normal**, whereas glucose Tm is far above physiological; (3) the **phosphate Tm is hormonally regulated** (PTH, vitamin D, acid-base), unlike the insulin-insensitive glucose Tm.
- **PTH** is the most important regulator: it **inhibits phosphate reabsorption** (promotes excretion), a rapid action not requiring protein synthesis. Via PTH1R → Gαs (cAMP/PKA) and Gαq (PLC/PKC) → endocytotic **removal of NaPi from the apical membrane** + lysosomal targeting. **Dopamine** (D1, cAMP/PKA) also inhibits. High dietary phosphate, phosphate loading, glucocorticoids, and acidosis decrease reabsorption; **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D** and phosphate depletion upregulate the cotransporter (Table 36-5).
- **Phosphatonins** cause renal phosphate wasting (oncogenic osteomalacia, autosomal dominant and X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets): **FGF23, sFRP4, MEPE**. **FGF23** (released by osteocytes in hyperphosphatemia; acts via **FGFR1** + coreceptor **Klotho**) reduces NaPi expression → augments phosphate excretion, and also lowers renal 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D production → reduced intestinal phosphate absorption.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The proximal tubule reabsorbs 80% to 95% of the filtered phosphate. More distal nephron segments reabsorb a negligible fraction." (p. 785)
- > "Because HPO₄²⁻ rather than H₂PO₄⁻ is the preferred substrate of NaPi-IIc, low luminal pH inhibits phosphate reabsorption." (p. 786)
- > "The most important hormonal regulator is PTH, which inhibits phosphate reabsorption and thus promotes phosphate excretion. PTH is a classic example of a rapid regulator that does not require protein synthesis." (p. 786)
- > "PKA and PKC promote endocytotic removal of NaPi from the apical membrane with targeting for degradation in lysosomes." (p. 786)
- > "In response to hyperphosphatemia, osteocytes release FGF23, which acts via the receptor tyrosine kinase FGFR1 and the coreceptor Klotho to reduce the expression of Na/phosphate cotransporters." (p. 787)

## Section 5 — Calcium (pp. 787–789)

### Subsection headings
- **Binding to plasma proteins and Ca²⁺-anion complexes influence Ca²⁺ filtration and reabsorption** (pp. 787–788)
- **The proximal tubule reabsorbs two thirds of filtered Ca²⁺; more distal segments reabsorb nearly all the remainder** (p. 788)
- **Transcellular Ca²⁺ movement is a two-step process: apical channel entry and basolateral extrusion by Na/Ca exchange and a Ca pump** (pp. 788–789)
- **PTH and vitamin D stimulate—whereas high plasma Ca²⁺ inhibits—Ca²⁺ reabsorption** (p. 789)

### Core claims
- Total plasma calcium 2.2–2.6 mM (8.8–10.6 mg/dL): ~40% protein-bound (nonfilterable, mainly albumin), ~15% complexed to small anions (carbonate, citrate, phosphate, sulfate), ~45% free **ionized Ca²⁺** (Table 36-6). Filterable fraction ~60%. The body regulates free [Ca²⁺] (normally **1.0–1.3 mM**). **Acidosis raises, alkalosis lowers** [Ca²⁺] (H⁺ competes for anionic binding sites) — acute alkalosis can mimic hypocalcemia. Low albumin lowers *total* measured calcium without lowering [Ca²⁺].
- **~99% of filtered Ca²⁺ is reabsorbed**, at PT, TAL, and DCT.
  - **PT ~65%** — **paracellular**, not hormonally controlled. Water reabsorption raises luminal [Ca²⁺] → favorable gradient for passive paracellular diffusion + solvent drag. A **high-NaCl diet** (volume expansion → less PT Na⁺/water reabsorption) **reduces Ca²⁺ reabsorption and raises urinary Ca²⁺** → calcium-stone risk.
  - **TAL ~25%** — mostly **paracellular**, driven by the lumen-positive voltage. **Loop diuretics** (furosemide) abolish that voltage → acutely inhibit Ca²⁺ reabsorption (calciuric).
  - **DCT ~8%** — **transcellular** and the major regulatory site (despite small mass).
  - Collecting ducts ~1%, role ill-defined.
- **Transcellular DCT Ca²⁺ transport (two steps)**: with [Ca²⁺]ᵢ ~100 nM (~10⁴-fold below ECF) and apical voltage ~ −70 mV, a steep gradient drives **passive apical entry via the epithelial Ca²⁺ channels TRPV5 and TRPV6 (ECaC1/ECaC2)**. **Calbindins** buffer cytosolic Ca²⁺ to keep [Ca²⁺]ᵢ low and the gradient favorable. Basolateral extrusion (against the gradient): primary active **plasma-membrane Ca²⁺-ATPase (PMCA1b)** dominates at low [Ca²⁺]ᵢ; **Na-Ca exchanger (NCX1)** contributes when [Ca²⁺]ᵢ rises (and can reverse to load Ca²⁺ if the inward Na⁺ gradient falls).
- **Regulation (Table 36-7)**:
  - **PTH** — most important; stimulates Ca²⁺ reabsorption in DCT/connecting tubule. On **TRPV5**: increases transcription and open probability, inhibits endocytosis; also upregulates calbindin and NCX1. Acts via PTH1R → PKA + PKC (both essential).
  - **Vitamin D** — increases distal Ca²⁺ reabsorption (gene transcription; upregulates TRPV5 and calbindins), complementing its major action of intestinal Ca²⁺ absorption.
  - **Plasma Ca²⁺ / CaSR** — high [Ca²⁺] activates the basolateral **Ca²⁺-sensing receptor (CaSR)** in the TAL → reduces lumen-positive voltage and paracellular Ca²⁺ permeability → less Ca²⁺ reabsorption (compensates hypercalcemia). Chronic hypercalcemia also impairs urinary concentration → a **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus**, which dilutes urine and helps avoid Ca²⁺ stones.
  - **Diuretics**: loop diuretics (furosemide) **increase** Ca²⁺ excretion (lose lumen-positive voltage) — useful in hypercalcemia. **Thiazides** (inhibit DCT Na/Cl cotransport → lower [Cl⁻]ᵢ → hyperpolarize → more apical Ca²⁺ entry) and **amiloride** (block apical Na⁺ channel → hyperpolarize) **increase** Ca²⁺ reabsorption — exploited to lower urinary Ca²⁺ in stone-formers.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The kidney reabsorbs ~99% of the filtered load of calcium, principally at the proximal tubule, the thick ascending limb (TAL), and the distal convoluted tubule (DCT)." (p. 788)
- > "Virtually all proximal-tubule Ca²⁺ reabsorption occurs via the paracellular route." (p. 788)
- > "In contrast to the proximal tubule and TAL, the DCT reabsorbs Ca²⁺ predominantly via an active, transcellular route." (p. 788)
- > "The most important regulator of renal Ca²⁺ reabsorption is PTH, which stimulates Ca²⁺ reabsorption in the DCT and the connecting tubule." (p. 789)
- > "Chronic hypercalcemia results in a dilute urine, a form of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus." (p. 789)
- > "Thiazide diuretics inhibit Na/Cl cotransport and stimulate Ca²⁺ reabsorption in the DCT." (p. 789)

## Section 6 — Magnesium (pp. 789–791)

### Subsection headings
- **Most Mg²⁺ reabsorption takes place along the TAL** (pp. 789–790)
- **Mg²⁺ reabsorption increases with depletion of Mg²⁺ or Ca²⁺, or with elevated PTH levels** (pp. 790–791)

### Core claims
- ~99% of body magnesium is in **bone (~54%)** or **intracellular (~45%, mostly muscle)**. Plasma total Mg 0.8–1.0 mM (1.8–2.2 mg/dL): ~30% protein-bound, <10% complexed (phosphate, citrate, oxalate), ~60% free **ionized Mg²⁺** (Table 36-8). Disturbances are usually losses (GI malabsorption, diarrhea, renal disease, diuretics); depletion → tetany, arrhythmias, increased peripheral vascular resistance; severe hypermagnesemia → nausea, hyporeflexia, respiratory insufficiency, cardiac arrest.
- **Reabsorption pattern is distinctively distal** (not proximal): normally **≤5%** of filtered Mg²⁺ is excreted.
  - **PT only ~15%** — paracellular; water reabsorption roughly doubles luminal [Mg²⁺] vs. Bowman's space, driving passive paracellular diffusion + solvent drag. PT Mg²⁺ permeability is **lower than for Ca²⁺**, so Mg²⁺ reabsorption is less volume-sensitive than Ca²⁺.
  - **TAL ~70%** — the dominant site; **paracellular**, driven by the lumen-positive voltage. **Claudin-16 and claudin-19** (tight-junction proteins) provide the high paracellular cation permeability; **CLDN16/CLDN19 mutations** cause autosomal recessive severe renal Mg²⁺ wasting with hypomagnesemia.
  - **DCT ~10%** — **transcellular**: apical entry via the **TRPM6** cation channel (driven by the inside-negative membrane potential; apical Kv1.1 K⁺ efflux neutralizes the entering positive charge); basolateral extrusion possibly by **Na-Mg exchange (SLC41A1)**.
- **Regulation (Table 36-9)**:
  - **Mg²⁺ depletion** → FE(Mg) falls to <2% (upregulated TAL + DCT reabsorption).
  - **Hypermagnesemia / hypercalcemia** — the kidney discriminates poorly; either cation **inhibits** both Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ reabsorption in the TAL (binding the **CaSR**), increasing excretion of both.
  - **Hormones** — **PTH** is the most important (increases distal Mg²⁺ reabsorption); AVP, glucagon, calcitonin stimulate TAL reabsorption (cAMP/PKA), largely by modulating paracellular movement.
  - **Diuretics** generally **decrease** Mg²⁺ reabsorption (loop diuretics and mannitol act in the loop; thiazides reduce DCT reabsorption partly by downregulating TRPM6).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In contrast to the predominantly 'proximal' reabsorption pattern of the major components of the glomerular filtrate, Mg²⁺ reabsorption occurs mainly along the TAL." (p. 790)
- > "The TAL absorbs ~70% of the filtered magnesium load." (p. 790)
- > "In the TAL, the tight-junction proteins claudin 16 and claudin 19 account for the high paracellular cation permeability that is necessary for paracellular Mg²⁺ reabsorption." (p. 790)
- > "Mutations of CLDN16 or CLDN19 genes cause an autosomal recessive disorder of severe renal Mg²⁺ wasting with hypomagnesemia." (p. 790)
- > "Mg²⁺ entry across the apical membrane may take place via the TRPM6 cation channel." (p. 790)
- > "The kidney seems to discriminate poorly between increases in plasma [Mg²⁺] and [Ca²⁺]." (p. 791)

## Glossary

- **BUN** — blood urea nitrogen; normal 7–18 mg/dL; BUN/creatinine ratio normally ~10, rises in volume depletion.
- **UT-A1/A2/A3, UT-B1/B2** — urea transporters (facilitated diffusion); AVP stimulates UT-A1/A3 in the IMCD.
- **Medullary urea recycling** — IMCD reabsorption → interstitium → thin-limb secretion → re-delivery; central to urine concentration.
- **SGLT1 / SGLT2** — apical Na/glucose cotransporters (2:1 late PT / 1:1 early PT).
- **GLUT1 / GLUT2** — basolateral facilitated-diffusion glucose carriers.
- **Phloridzin** — SGLT inhibitor.
- **Transport maximum (Tm)** — saturable reabsorption/secretion plateau.
- **Threshold / splay** — plasma level at which a solute first appears in urine / rounded approach to Tm from nephron heterogeneity.
- **PepT1 / PepT2** — apical H⁺/oligopeptide cotransporters.
- **Megalin / cubilin / amnionless** — apical endocytic receptor complex for filtered protein.
- **NaDC1 / NaDC3** — Na/dicarboxylate cotransporters (apical / basolateral).
- **OAT1 / OAT3 / OAT4 / OAT10** — organic anion transporters.
- **PAH** — para-aminohippurate; Tm-limited secretion; clearance ≈ RPF at low plasma [PAH].
- **URAT1 / URATv1** — urate transporters; URAT1 is the uricosuric-drug target.
- **OCT2 / MATE1 / MATE2-K / MDR1** — organic-cation transporters.
- **Nonionic diffusion** — pH-dependent reabsorption/excretion of weak acids/bases.
- **NaPi-IIa / NaPi-IIc / PiT-2** — apical Na/phosphate cotransporters.
- **Phosphatonins (FGF23, sFRP4, MEPE)** — phosphaturic factors; FGF23 acts via FGFR1 + Klotho.
- **TRPV5 / TRPV6 (ECaC1/ECaC2)** — apical Ca²⁺ channels (DCT).
- **Calbindin** — cytosolic Ca²⁺-buffering protein.
- **PMCA1b / NCX1** — basolateral Ca²⁺ extrusion (pump / Na-Ca exchanger).
- **CaSR** — Ca²⁺-sensing receptor (TAL); inhibits paracellular Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ reabsorption when plasma [Ca²⁺]/[Mg²⁺] is high.
- **Claudin-16 / claudin-19** — TAL tight-junction proteins enabling paracellular Mg²⁺ (and Ca²⁺) reabsorption; mutations → renal Mg²⁺ wasting.
- **TRPM6** — apical DCT Mg²⁺ channel; **SLC41A1** — basolateral Na-Mg exchanger.
