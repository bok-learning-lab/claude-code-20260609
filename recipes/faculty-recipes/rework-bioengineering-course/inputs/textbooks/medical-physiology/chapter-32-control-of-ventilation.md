## Section 1 — Ventilatory output and breathing patterns (pp. 700–704)

### Subsection headings
- **Respiratory motor pools innervate the diaphragm, intercostals, and accessory muscles** (pp. 700–702) — phrenic (C3–C5), intercostal (T1–T12), other.
- **Eupnea: normal quiet breathing at ~12 breaths/min and 6 L/min minute ventilation** (p. 702)
- **Many abnormal breathing patterns reflect specific neuropathology** (Box 32-2, pp. 703–704)

### Core claims
- Motor output to respiratory muscles originates from spinal motoneuron pools that are driven by descending brainstem premotor neurons (DRG and VRG).
- The **diaphragm** is innervated by the **phrenic nerve (C3–C5)** — spinal cord injury above C3 makes spontaneous breathing impossible.
- Quiet breathing (eupnea) uses the diaphragm and parasternal intercostals; expiration is passive.
- Forced breathing recruits accessory inspiratory muscles (scalenes, SCM) and active expiratory muscles (abdominal wall, internal intercostals).
- **Patterns of breathing** distinguished and clinically diagnostic (Box 32-2):
  - **Eupnea** — normal.
  - **Sigh** — periodic larger-than-tidal breath; recruits collapsed alveoli.
  - **Tachypnea** — rapid shallow breathing.
  - **Hyperpnea** — increased minute ventilation matched to demand (e.g., exercise; not a disease).
  - **Hyperventilation** — increased ventilation above metabolic demand → ↓ PaCO₂; alkalosis. Causes include hypoxia, anxiety, sepsis, salicylate, brainstem lesion.
  - **Kussmaul** — deep, fast respirations in metabolic acidosis (e.g., DKA).
  - **Central neurogenic hyperventilation** — brainstem lesion (rostral pons/midbrain).
  - **Cheyne–Stokes** — crescendo–decrescendo cycles separated by apneas; heart failure, stroke, high altitude. Periodic-breathing instability.
  - **Apneustic** — prolonged inspiratory hold; pontine lesion below pneumotaxic center.
  - **Gasping** — episodic deep inspirations with long expiratory pauses; severe brainstem damage; agonal pattern.
  - **Cluster, ataxic, Biot** — irregular grouped breaths with apneas; medullary lesions.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Respiratory output is set to match the metabolic demand of the body for O₂ and CO₂ exchange." (p. 700)
- > "The diaphragm is innervated by the phrenic nerves, which arise from cervical spinal segments C3 to C5." (p. 700)
- > "Eupnea: normal breathing." (p. 703)
- > "Cheyne–Stokes respiration: a benign respiratory pattern. Cycles of a gradual increase in tidal volume, followed by a gradual decrease in tidal volume, and then a period of apnea. Seen with bilateral cortical disease or congestive heart failure, or in healthy people during sleep at high altitude." (Box 32-2, p. 703)

## Section 2 — Brainstem rhythm generation (pp. 704–710)

### Subsection headings
- **Two anatomically distinct respiratory groups have been identified in the medulla** (pp. 704–706) — DRG (dorsal, in NTS) and VRG (ventral column).
- **The pontine respiratory group fine-tunes the rhythm** (p. 706) — Kölliker–Fuse and parabrachial nuclei (pneumotaxic center); apneustic center.
- **The pre-Bötzinger complex (preBötC) is necessary and sufficient for inspiratory rhythm** (pp. 706–709)
- **Pacemaker properties and synaptic interactions both contribute** (pp. 709–710)

### Core claims — anatomy and identity (Table 32-2 territory)
- **Dorsal respiratory group (DRG)** — neurons in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS); primarily inspiratory; integrates afferent input from lung stretch receptors and chemoreceptors via the glossopharyngeal (IX) and vagus (X).
- **Ventral respiratory group (VRG)** — column along the ventrolateral medulla including (rostral → caudal): Bötzinger complex (expiratory premotor), pre-Bötzinger complex (preBötC; inspiratory rhythm generator), rostral VRG / NA (inspiratory premotor and motor), caudal VRG (expiratory premotor).
- **Pontine respiratory group (PRG)** = Kölliker–Fuse + medial parabrachial nucleus (formerly "pneumotaxic center"). Lesion → apneustic breathing, especially after vagal section.
- **Apneustic center** in the caudal pons promotes prolonged inspiratory bursts; tonically inhibited by the pneumotaxic center and by vagal stretch input.

### Core claims — the preBötC
- A small bilateral cluster of neurons immediately ventrolateral to the nucleus ambiguus that generates the inspiratory rhythm in mammals.
- **Necessary**: bilateral lesion causes irreversible apnea.
- **Sufficient**: isolated brainstem slices containing preBötC produce rhythmic inspiratory bursts in vitro.
- Two coexisting mechanisms produce rhythm:
  1. **Pacemaker properties** — a subset of preBötC neurons fire bursts intrinsically owing to a persistent Na⁺ current ($I_{Na,P}$) plus Ca²⁺-activated nonspecific cation current ($I_{CAN}$).
  2. **Network properties** — recurrent excitatory glutamatergic connectivity plus reciprocal inhibition with expiratory neurons (Bötzinger complex) generates rhythm by emergent synchronization.
- The rhythm is modulated by TRH, substance P (NK1R), and serotonin from the raphé.
- **Phasic reciprocal inhibition**: early-inspiratory and late-inspiratory neurons take turns firing (Fig. 32-6 — viewed), with mutual GABA/glycine-mediated inhibition coordinating the three-phase respiratory cycle (inspiration → post-inspiration / early expiration → late expiration).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Two anatomically distinct respiratory groups have been identified in the medulla." (p. 704)
- > "The pre-Bötzinger complex contains neurons that generate the inspiratory rhythm in mammals." (p. 707)
- > "A small set of preBötC neurons have pacemaker-like firing properties capable of generating intrinsic bursts even when synaptic transmission is blocked." (p. 709)
- > "The rhythm is enhanced by neuropeptides such as TRH and substance P, and by serotonin from the raphé." (p. 709)
- > "Pacemaker properties and synaptic interactions may both contribute to the generation of the respiratory rhythm." (p. 709)

## Section 3 — Central chemoreceptors (pp. 710–714)

### Subsection headings
- **Central chemoreceptors sense brain interstitial pH/PCO₂ and drive the chronic ventilatory response to CO₂** (pp. 710–712)
- **Multiple medullary nuclei contribute: the retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN), serotonergic raphé, and NTS** (pp. 712–713)
- **CO₂ acts via the H⁺ it generates in brain interstitial fluid** (p. 713)

### Core claims
- CO₂ crosses the blood–brain barrier freely; HCO₃⁻ and H⁺ do not. A rise in arterial PCO₂ acidifies brain ISF and CSF within seconds.
- **Central chemoreceptors** sense this H⁺ change. They account for ~70% of the steady-state ventilatory response to PaCO₂ and provide the entire chronic CO₂ drive.
- **Retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN)** — ventral medullary glutamatergic neurons expressing **PHOX2B**. Identified by Mulkey, Guyenet, and colleagues as primary CO₂/H⁺ sensors. Drive the preBötC via direct projection.
- **Medullary raphé serotonergic neurons** — Richerson's group: 5-HT neurons increase firing with falling pH; project to preBötC, motor pools, and forebrain arousal nuclei. Important for arousal from sleep apnea.
- **NTS neurons** in the dorsal medulla also have intrinsic CO₂/H⁺ sensitivity, in addition to receiving peripheral chemoreceptor input.
- The CSF compensation for chronic respiratory acidosis (via choroid plexus HCO₃⁻ secretion) explains why a chronic COPD patient with PaCO₂ 60 mm Hg has a normal pH-driven ventilation: their CSF [HCO₃⁻] rises in compensation.

### Hypercapnic ventilatory response (HCVR)

The relationship between alveolar (or arterial) PCO₂ and minute ventilation is approximately linear above an apneic threshold:

$$\dot V_E = S \cdot (P_{aCO_2} - B)$$

with slope $S \approx 2$–$5\,\text{L/min per mm Hg}$ and apneic threshold $B \approx 35$–$40\,\text{mm Hg}$. The slope steepens with hypoxia (synergism) and is depressed by sleep (especially REM), opioids, and benzodiazepines.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Central chemoreceptors are sensitive to the H⁺ concentration in brain ISF, which depends on arterial PCO₂." (p. 710)
- > "The retrotrapezoid nucleus is a major site of central chemoreception." (p. 712)
- > "Medullary raphé serotonergic neurons increase their firing rate in response to a fall in pH and contribute to chemoreception and arousal from sleep apnea." (p. 713)
- > "Central chemoreceptors account for about 70% of the steady-state ventilatory response to PaCO₂." (p. 712)

## Section 4 — Peripheral chemoreceptors (pp. 714–717)

### Subsection headings
- **The carotid bodies sense PaO₂, PaCO₂, and pH** (pp. 714–716)
- **Glomus (type I) cells transduce hypoxia through K⁺-channel inhibition** (p. 716)
- **The aortic bodies are smaller contributors** (p. 716)
- **The hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR) is hyperbolic in PaO₂ and rises sharply below 60 mm Hg** (pp. 716–717)

### Core claims
- **Carotid body** — at the bifurcation of the common carotid artery, on the medial side. Innervated by the **carotid sinus nerve** (branch of CN IX). Highest blood flow per unit mass of any organ; arteriovenous O₂ difference negligible — they sense arterial blood directly.
- **Glomus (type I) cells** — dopamine, ATP, and acetylcholine-containing chemoreceptive cells. Surrounded by sustentacular (type II) glial-like cells.
- **Aortic bodies** — at the aortic arch; innervated by the vagus (CN X); contribute less than carotid bodies in adults but more in fetal/neonatal life.
- **Mechanism of O₂ sensing** in glomus cells (still debated, several candidate sensors):
  - Hypoxia inhibits a K⁺ current ($K_{2P}$ TASK channels and/or maxi-K) → membrane depolarizes → voltage-gated Ca²⁺ entry → vesicular release of dopamine, ATP, ACh onto carotid-sinus-nerve afferents → ↑ afferent firing → brainstem respiratory and arousal responses.
  - Heme-protein O₂ sensors (NADPH oxidase products, AMPK activation) and mitochondrial signals also implicated.
- **Hypoxic ventilatory response (HVR)** — minute ventilation as a function of PaO₂ is approximately hyperbolic; minimal change for PaO₂ > 60 mm Hg; sharp rise below 60 mm Hg. A Lloyd-form descriptive equation:

  $$\dot V_E = \dot V_{E,0} + \frac{A}{P_{aO_2} - C}$$

  with parameters fit empirically. **Synergism with CO₂**: the HVR is greatly enhanced when PaCO₂ is also elevated (e.g., during apnea); the carotid body responds to the *product* of hypoxic and hypercapnic stimuli.
- **Threshold and timing**: PaO₂ < 60 mm Hg (the steep portion of the Hb-O₂ curve where saturation drops). Carotid-body firing rises within seconds, faster than central chemoreceptors.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The carotid bodies have the highest blood flow per unit mass of any tissue, allowing them to sense arterial blood directly." (p. 714)
- > "Hypoxia inhibits a K⁺ current in glomus cells, leading to depolarization, Ca²⁺ entry, and transmitter release." (p. 716)
- > "The aortic bodies contribute less to the hypoxic ventilatory response than the carotid bodies." (p. 716)
- > "The hypoxic ventilatory response is hyperbolic; threshold for stimulation is approximately PaO₂ 60 mm Hg." (p. 717)

## Section 5 — Integrated responses, sleep, altitude, and disease (pp. 717–721)

### Subsection headings
- **Cortical voluntary control can override the brainstem** (p. 717)
- **Sleep depresses ventilatory drive** (p. 718)
- **Sleep apnea is obstructive, central, or mixed** (pp. 718–719)
- **High-altitude acclimatization involves a slow rise in HVR over days** (p. 719)
- **Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS / "Ondine's curse") is caused by PHOX2B mutations** (p. 720)
- **Opioids and benzodiazepines depress ventilation** (pp. 720–721)

### Core claims
- **Voluntary breath-hold** is limited by PaCO₂ accumulation, hypoxia, and the rising drive from peripheral chemoreceptors. Voluntary hyperventilation extends breath-hold by lowering starting PaCO₂.
- **Sleep**:
  - NREM: HCVR slope drops ~30%, HVR drops modestly.
  - REM: greater depression of HCVR; muscle atonia of intercostals (sparing diaphragm) makes obstructive apnea more likely.
  - All sleep stages: ventilation falls slightly, PaCO₂ rises 2–5 mm Hg.
- **Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)**: pharyngeal collapse during REM; high BMI, anatomic narrowing; treatment CPAP. Repetitive arousal, sympathetic surges, daytime hypertension, cardiovascular risk.
- **Central sleep apnea (CSA)**: brainstem fails to drive respiratory motor pools; seen in heart failure (Cheyne–Stokes), high altitude, CCHS, opioid use, stroke.
- **Periodic breathing and loop gain**: cycles of crescendo–decrescendo ventilation with intervening apnea — happen when the control loop is unstable (high gain × long delay). Loop gain $LG = \text{response}/\text{disturbance}$; loop becomes unstable as LG approaches 1.
- **High-altitude acclimatization**:
  - First hours: HVR kicks in; ventilation rises; PaCO₂ falls → respiratory alkalosis → blunts further hyperventilation.
  - Days: renal HCO₃⁻ excretion compensates the alkalosis → pH approaches normal → ventilation can rise further. Choroid plexus also lowers CSF HCO₃⁻, allowing central chemoreceptor to drive more ventilation.
  - Weeks: HVR sensitivity increases ("ventilatory acclimatization").
- **CCHS** (PHOX2B mutation — a polyalanine expansion in most cases): loss of central CO₂ sensitivity; normal breathing while awake (cortical drive), apnea or hypoventilation during sleep. Requires lifelong ventilator support. Same PHOX2B mutation underlies aspects of autonomic dysfunction in CCHS.
- **Opioid respiratory depression**: μ-opioid receptors on preBötC neurons silence inspiratory bursts; primary cause of opioid overdose death. Reversed by naloxone.
- **Benzodiazepines and barbiturates**: GABA_A enhancement at premotor neurons and preBötC; depress breathing especially in combination with opioids.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Sleep depresses ventilatory drive; the hypercapnic ventilatory response slope falls by about 30% during NREM sleep and more during REM sleep." (p. 718)
- > "Obstructive sleep apnea is caused by collapse of the pharyngeal airway during sleep." (p. 718)
- > "Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine's curse) results from mutations in the PHOX2B gene." (p. 720)
- > "Opioids cause respiratory depression by acting on μ-opioid receptors on neurons of the pre-Bötzinger complex." (p. 720)

## Equations summary

| Quantity | Equation | Use |
|---|---|---|
| HCVR slope | $\dot V_E = S(P_{aCO_2} - B)$ | central + peripheral CO₂ drive |
| HVR (Lloyd) | $\dot V_E = \dot V_{E,0} + A/(P_{aO_2} - C)$ | hypoxic drive |
| Combined chemoreceptor drive | $\dot V_E \propto (P_{aCO_2} - B) \cdot f(P_{aO_2})$ | synergism |
| Loop gain | $LG = \frac{\Delta \dot V_E}{\Delta \dot V_E^{disturbance}}$ | periodic-breathing stability |
| Bohr Vd/Vt | $V_D/V_T = (P_{aCO_2} - P_{ECO_2})/P_{aCO_2}$ | dead space (Ch 31) |
| Alveolar ventilation | $\dot V_A = 0.863 \dot V_{CO_2}/P_{aCO_2}$ | reused (Ch 31) |

## Glossary

- **Eupnea, sigh, tachypnea, hyperpnea, hyperventilation, Kussmaul, Cheyne–Stokes, apneustic, gasping, cluster, ataxic, Biot** — breathing patterns.
- **DRG / VRG / preBötC / Bötzinger / pontine respiratory group (PRG)** — brainstem nuclei.
- **Pre-Bötzinger complex** — inspiratory rhythm generator.
- **Pacemaker (intrinsic) vs. network (synaptic) rhythm** — two complementary mechanisms.
- **TRH, substance P (NK1R)** — preBötC neuromodulators.
- **Central chemoreceptors / RTN / raphé serotonergic / NTS / PHOX2B**.
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors / carotid body / aortic body / glomus (Type I) cell / sustentacular (Type II) cell / CN IX (carotid sinus nerve) / CN X (aortic)**.
- **HCVR (hypercapnic ventilatory response)** — linear in PaCO₂; primary chronic drive.
- **HVR (hypoxic ventilatory response)** — hyperbolic in PaO₂; threshold ~60 mm Hg; synergistic with CO₂.
- **Loop gain** — control-loop stability metric; high LG → periodic breathing.
- **NREM / REM sleep** — both depress ventilation; REM more so.
- **OSA / CSA / Cheyne–Stokes** — apnea subtypes.
- **CCHS / Ondine's curse / PHOX2B** — congenital loss of central chemoreception.
- **Naloxone / μ-opioid receptor / opioid respiratory depression**.
- **Hering–Breuer inflation reflex** — slow-adapting stretch receptors via vagus.
- **Ventilatory acclimatization to altitude** — days to weeks; CSF HCO₃⁻ correction.
