## Section 1 — Overview of liver physiology (p. 944)

### Subsection headings
- **The liver biotransforms and degrades substances taken up from blood and either returns them to the circulation or excretes them into bile** (p. 944)
- **The liver stores carbohydrates, lipids, vitamins, and minerals; it synthesizes carbohydrates, protein, and intermediary metabolites** (p. 944)

### Core claims
- The liver is the largest internal organ after skin and brain: ~1200–1500 g, 2%–5% of adult body weight (~4%–5% in newborns). It sits in the circulation to receive portal blood draining the stomach, intestines, pancreas, and spleen.
- Four functional identities: **chemical factory, excretory system, exocrine gland, and endocrine gland.**
- Major roles: (1) metabolize/detoxify/inactivate endogenous (steroids, hormones) and exogenous (drugs, toxins) compounds; (2) filter the blood via **Kupffer cells**, which constitute 80%–90% of the fixed macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system, removing bacteria, endotoxins, parasites, and senescent RBCs; (3) activate hormones/vitamins (initial hydroxylation of vitamin D; deiodination of T₄ → T₃); (4) store and synthesize carbohydrate, protein, lipid; (5) produce **bile**.
- Biliary secretion has two principal functions: **elimination of endogenous/exogenous waste** (bilirubin, cholesterol) and **promotion of lipid digestion and absorption.**
- The liver spares ~50% of CNS glucose use by producing **ketone bodies** during fasting — a unique role in whole-body energy metabolism.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "However, the liver's role is far more diverse; it serves as a chemical factory, an excretory system, an exocrine gland, and an endocrine gland." (p. 944)
- > "Kupffer cells constitute 80% to 90% of the fixed macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system." (p. 944)
- > "Biliary secretion has two principal functions: (1) elimination from the body of many endogenous and exogenous waste products … and (2) promotion of digestion and absorption of lipids from the intestine." (p. 944)
- > "Thus, the liver has a critical and unique role in the energy metabolism of all nonhepatic organs." (p. 944)

## Section 2 — Functional anatomy of the liver and biliary tree (pp. 944–949)

### Subsection headings
- **Hepatocytes are secretory epithelial cells separating the lumen of bile canaliculi from the fenestrated endothelium of sinusoids** (pp. 944–946)
- **The liver contains endothelial cells, macrophages (Kupffer cells), and stellate cells (Ito cells) within the sinusoidal spaces** (p. 946)
- **The liver has a dual blood supply, but a single venous drainage system** (p. 946)
- **Hepatocytes can be thought of as being arranged as classic hepatic lobules, portal lobules, or acinar units** (p. 946)
- **Periportal hepatocytes specialize in oxidative metabolism, whereas pericentral hepatocytes detoxify drugs** (pp. 946–948)
- **Bile drains from canaliculi into small terminal ductules, then into larger ducts, and eventually, via a single common duct, into the duodenum** (pp. 948–949)

### Core claims
- **Hepatocytes** account for ~80% of liver parenchymal volume. They form a one-cell-thick epithelium separating two fluids of differing ionic composition — the tiny **canalicular lumen** (bile) and the much larger **sinusoid** (blood) — and they alter both by polarized (vectorial) transport.
- **Membrane domains:** the **apical (canalicular) membrane** faces the bile canaliculus; the **basolateral (sinusoidal) membrane** faces the space of Disse. The basolateral membrane, amplified by microvilli, is ~85% of total surface area; the apical belt, though only ~1 µm wide, is amplified by microvilli to ~15% of surface area. Total apical surface for solute/water movement exceeds 10.5 m².
- **Bile canaliculi** (~1 µm diameter) are formed by the grooved apical membranes of two adjacent hepatocytes; they anastomose into a chicken-wire-like 3-D tubular meshwork.
- **Junctions:** apical tight junctions (intermediate tightness — between tight toad bladder and leaky proximal tubule) and desmosomes seal the canaliculus; **gap junctions** couple adjacent hepatocytes.
- **Space of Disse** (perisinusoidal space): extracellular gap between fenestrated sinusoidal endothelium and the hepatocyte basolateral membrane. Hepatocytes lack a true basement membrane and rest on extracellular matrix (collagens I, III, IV, V, VI; fibronectin, undulin, laminin, proteoglycans).
- **Sinusoidal non-parenchymal cells** (>6% of parenchymal volume): **endothelial cells** (2.8%; fenestrae let plasma but not blood cells into the space of Disse), **Kupffer cells** (2.1%; fixed macrophages), **stellate (Ito/fat-storing) cells** (1.4%; store ~80% of body vitamin A; transform into fibrogenic, contractile myofibroblasts on injury → cirrhosis via type I collagen deposition).
- **Dual blood supply:** the **portal vein** supplies ~75% of liver blood flow; the **hepatic artery** ~25%. Blood mixes in sinusoids → terminal hepatic venules (central veins) → hepatic veins. The triad (portal venule + hepatic arteriole + bile duct), plus lymphatics and nerves, travels in the **portal tract.** A **peribiliary plexus** around the bile ducts allows bidirectional exchange that modifies biliary secretions.
- **Three views of liver organization:** (a) **classic hepatic lobule** — hexagon centered on a central vein, triads at corners (drains blood to central vein); (b) **portal lobule** — centered on a triad/bile ductule (drains bile); (c) **portal acinus** (Rappaport) — centered on the terminal portal venule + arteriole, emphasizing the oxygenation gradient.
- **Zonation:** **zone 1 (periportal)** hepatocytes are perfused first, see the highest O₂/solute, and are most resistant to circulatory/nutritional injury and first to regenerate; **zone 3 (pericentral)**, near the terminal hepatic venule, sees progressively lower O₂/nutrients. Zonal enzyme heterogeneity (Table 46-1): zone 1 favors **oxidative metabolism, β-oxidation, gluconeogenesis, ureagenesis, cholesterol/bile-acid synthesis**; zone 3 favors **glycolysis, glycogen synthesis from glucose, liponeogenesis, ketogenesis, glutamine synthesis, and xenobiotic/drug biotransformation.** **Glutamine synthetase** is expressed in only one or two hepatocytes immediately adjacent to the hepatic venule. Zone 3 localization of biotransformation enzymes explains the zonal pattern of drug-induced centrilobular necrosis.
- **Biliary tree:** >2 km of ductules/ducts, ~20 cm³ volume, ~400 cm² surface (×5.5 by microvilli). Flow path: canaliculi → **canals of Hering** (terminal ductules) → perilobular → interlobular → septal → lobar → right + left hepatic ducts → **common hepatic duct.** This joins the **cystic duct** (from gallbladder) → **common bile duct** (~7 cm long, 0.5–1.5 cm diameter) → usually merges with the **pancreatic duct** as the **ampulla of Vater** → duodenum. **Cholangiocytes** (biliary epithelial cells) are heterogeneous along the tree and contribute to bile formation.
- **Sphincter of Oddi:** smooth-muscle thickening around the common channel through the duodenal wall; constricts the duct and regulates bile flow. **CCK relaxes the sphincter of Oddi** via a nonadrenergic, noncholinergic (VIP) pathway.
- **Gallbladder:** distensible pear-shaped organ (capacity 30–50 mL) under the right lobe; absorptive surface amplified by folds; connected via cystic duct (with the **valve of Heister** spiral valve at its neck instead of a sphincter).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Hepatocytes account for ~80% of the parenchymal volume in human liver." (p. 944)
- > "The portal vein contributes ~75% of the total circulation to the liver; the hepatic artery contributes the other 25%." (p. 946)
- > "These periportal hepatocytes are said to reside in zone I, and as a consequence of their location, they are the most resistant to the effects of circulatory compromise or nutritional deficiency." (pp. 946–947)
- > "The hormone cholecystokinin (CCK) relaxes the sphincter of Oddi via a nonadrenergic, noncholinergic neural pathway involving vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)." (p. 949)
- > "Instead of a sphincter, the gallbladder has, at its neck, a spiral valve—the valve of Heister—formed by the mucous membrane." (p. 949)

## Section 3 — Uptake, processing, and secretion of compounds by hepatocytes (pp. 949–957)

### Subsection headings
- **An Na-K pump at the basolateral membranes of hepatocytes provides the energy for transporting a wide variety of solutes via channels and transporters** (p. 951)
- **Hepatocytes take up bile acids, other organic anions, and organic cations across their basolateral (sinusoidal) membranes** (pp. 951–954)
- **Inside the hepatocyte, the basolateral-to-apical movement of many compounds occurs by protein-bound or vesicular routes** (p. 954)
- **In phase I of the biotransformation of organic anions and other compounds, hepatocytes use mainly cytochrome P-450 enzymes** (pp. 954–955)
- **In phase II of biotransformation, conjugation of phase I products makes them more water soluble for secretion into blood or bile** (pp. 955–956)
- **The interactions of xenobiotics with nuclear receptors control phase I, II, and III** (p. 956)
- **In phase III of biotransformation, hepatocytes excrete products of phase I and II into bile or sinusoidal blood** (p. 956)
- **Hepatocytes secrete bile acids, organic anions, organic cations, and lipids across their apical (canalicular) membranes** (pp. 957)
- **Hepatocytes take up proteins across their basolateral membranes by receptor-mediated endocytosis and fluid-phase endocytosis** (pp. 957)

### Core claims — the four-step vectorial pathway
The hepatocyte handles a compound in four steps (Fig. 46-5A): (1) **import** across the basolateral/sinusoidal membrane; (2) **intracellular transport**; (3) **chemical modification/degradation**; (4) **export** into bile across the apical/canalicular membrane.

**Housekeeping & driving forces:**
- The basolateral **Na-K pump** keeps [Na⁺]ᵢ low and [K⁺]ᵢ high; a basolateral **Ca pump** keeps [Ca²⁺]ᵢ ~100 nM. The inward Na⁺ gradient fuels Na-H exchange, Na/HCO₃ cotransport, Na⁺-coupled amino-acid transport, and the bile-acid cotransporter NTCP.
- **GLUT2** mediates insulin-insensitive facilitated glucose uptake. Resting Vₘ ≈ −30 to −40 mV (more positive than E_K because of leak pathways).

**Basolateral uptake transporters:**
- **Bile acids/salts.** Primary bile acids = **cholic acid** and **chenodeoxycholic acid** (made by hepatocytes); secondary acids form by bacterial dehydroxylation in the gut. Conjugation to **glycine/taurine** (Z) or **sulfate/glucuronate** (Y) raises water solubility. Uptake is dominated by **NTCP (Na/taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide, SLC10A1)** — a 50-kDa, 7-TM glycoprotein with high affinity for conjugated bile acids. Up to ~50% of **unconjugated** bile acids enter by passive **nonionic diffusion** of the neutral H·BA form.
- **Organic anions.** **OATPs** (organic anion-transporting polypeptides, SLC21/SLCO family; 12-TM) mediate Na⁺-independent uptake of a broad range — bile acids, bilirubin, eicosanoids, steroid/thyroid hormones, statins, methotrexate, xenobiotics — likely exchanging for intracellular HCO₃⁻. Liver-specific isoforms **OATP1B1, OATP1B3, OATP2B1** are basolateral; expression is controlled by nuclear receptors (FXR, LXR, SXR, CAR) and HNF4.
- **Bilirubin.** Senescent RBCs → macrophage heme degradation → unconjugated bilirubin released to blood (Box 46-1). Hepatic uptake mechanism is still debated (electroneutral, electrogenic, and Cl-dependent transport proposed); **OATP1B1/OATP1B3** transport conjugated (and possibly unconjugated) bilirubin. Loss of both causes **Rotor syndrome** (conjugated hyperbilirubinemia) — hepatocytes secrete glucuronidated bilirubin back into the space of Disse, and OATP1B1/1B3 normally re-uptake it.
- **Organic cations.** ~40% of drugs are organic cations at physiological pH. **OCT1 (SLC22A1)** and **OCT3 (SLC22A3)** mediate electrogenic, Na⁺/H⁺-independent, bidirectional sinusoidal uptake. OCT1 substrates: acyclovir, lidocaine; OCT3 exclusive substrates: epinephrine, norepinephrine, histamine.
- **Neutral organic compounds.** Na⁺-independent, energy-dependent; prototype substrate ouabain (OATP1B1 carries some).

**Intracellular transport:** bile salts move bound to **binding proteins** (hepatic dihydrodiol dehydrogenase is the main one; also glutathione-S-transferase B and fatty acid-binding protein), or by vesicular/diffusional routes. Bilirubin glucuronide moves by direct **membrane-to-membrane transfer** (biased toward higher cholesterol/phospholipid membranes, directing flux to the ER).

**Phase I (CYP450):** oxidation/reduction (hydroxylation, dealkylation, dehalogenation) inserting one O atom; monooxygenases make the substrate more polar. **57 human CYP genes** encode hundreds of variants; genetic polymorphisms vary by population. Microsomal P-450 holoenzymes (48–56 kDa) = apoprotein (confers substrate specificity) + heme; named for 450-nm absorbance bound to CO.

**Phase II (conjugation):** three major reactions — (1) **glucuronidation** by UGTs in the SER (UGT1 family for phenols/bilirubin; UGT2 for steroids/bile acids); UGT1A1 is essential for bilirubin conjugation; (2) **sulfation** by cytosolic sulfotransferases; (3) **glutathione conjugation** by glutathione-S-transferases (liver GSH ~5 mM) → bile or kidney → **mercapturic acid** derivatives in urine. Also methylation, acetylation, and amino-acid conjugation (taurine/glycine/glutamine).

**Nuclear-receptor control of all three phases:** xenobiotic receptors **SXR/PXR, CAR, and AhR** (heterodimerizing with RXR or ARNT) coordinately induce phase I/II/III genes. **SXR** upregulates CYP3A (metabolizes >50% of drugs), GST, and MDR1; **CAR** regulates all of bilirubin metabolism (uptake, UGT1A1 conjugation, MRP2 excretion).

**Phase III / apical (canalicular) efflux:**
| Transporter | Gene | Substrate role |
|---|---|---|
| **BSEP** | ABCB11 | bile-salt export pump; high affinity for conjugated bile salts; only negatively charged salts excreted; 100:1–1000:1 cell-to-canaliculus gradient. Mutations → progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC2). |
| **MRP2** | ABCC2 | exports divalent amphipathic phase II conjugates (glutathione, glucuronide, sulfate), conjugated bilirubin. Defect → Dubin-Johnson syndrome (conjugated hyperbilirubinemia). |
| **MDR1** | ABCB1 | bulky organic cations, xenobiotics, anticancer drugs (colchicine, quinidine, verapamil, cyclosporine). |
| **MATE1** | SLC47A1 | pH-gradient-driven organic-cation efflux. |
| **MDR3** | ABCB4 | phosphatidylcholine flippase; deficiency → low biliary phospholipid, progressive liver disease. |
| **ABCG5/ABCG8** | — | heterodimeric cholesterol export; mutations → sitosterolemia. |
| **ATP8B1** | — | aminophospholipid (PS, PE) inward flippase maintaining canalicular lipid asymmetry (detergent resistance); mutations → PFIC1. |
| **BCRP** | ABCG2 | sulfated conjugates (estrone-3-sulfate, DHEA-sulfate). |
| **MRP4/MRP6** | ABCC4/ABCC6 | basolateral efflux of bile-salt conjugates etc. into sinusoidal blood; upregulated in cholestasis. |

**Protein uptake:** macromolecules (insulin, polymeric IgA, asialoglycoproteins, EGF) enter by **fluid-phase**, **adsorptive**, and **receptor-mediated endocytosis** (the quantitatively dominant route); receptor recycles, ligand goes to bile (exocytosis) or lysosome.

### Equations
- **Bile-acid weak-acid dissociation** (Eq. 46-1) — determines what fraction is the membrane-permeant neutral form:
  $$\text{H·BA} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{BA}^-$$
  Conjugation lowers the pKₐ (taurine more than glycine), raising [BA⁻] and reducing passive nonionic diffusion.
- **Phase I → Phase II biotransformation scheme** (Eq. 46-2):
  $$\text{RH} \xrightarrow{\text{Phase I}} \text{ROH} \xrightarrow{\text{Phase II}} \text{RO–Conjugate}$$
  Phase I exposes/adds a functional group (e.g., hydroxyl); phase II conjugates it (glucuronate, sulfate, glutathione) for excretion; phase III exports the conjugate.

## Section 4 — Bile formation (pp. 957–962)

### Subsection headings
- **The secretion of canalicular bile is active and isotonic** (pp. 957–958)
- **Major organic molecules in bile include bile acids, cholesterol, and phospholipids** (pp. 958–959)
- **Canalicular bile flow has a constant component driven by the secretion of small organic molecules and a variable component driven by the secretion of bile acids** (pp. 959–960)
- **Secretin stimulates the cholangiocytes of ductules and ducts to secrete a watery, HCO₃⁻-rich fluid** (pp. 960–961)
- **The gallbladder stores bile and delivers it to the duodenum during a meal** (p. 961)
- **The relative tones of the gallbladder and sphincter of Oddi determine whether bile flows from the common hepatic duct into the gallbladder or into the duodenum** (pp. 961–962)

### Core claims
- **Three steps of bile formation:** (1) hepatocytes actively secrete bile into canaliculi; (2) intra-/extrahepatic ducts transport it and add a watery, HCO₃⁻-rich fluid (→ ~900 mL/day **hepatic bile**); (3) between meals ~half (~450 mL/day) is diverted to the **gallbladder**, which isosmotically removes salts/water and **concentrates** bile salts, bilirubin, cholesterol, and lecithin **10- to 20-fold.** ~500 mL/day reaches the duodenum.
- **Bile formation is active, not ultrafiltration** — canalicular hydrostatic pressure exceeds sinusoidal perfusion pressure (opposite of glomerular filtration). It is temperature- and metabolic-inhibitor-sensitive: active secretion of solutes drives passive water flow.
- **Canalicular bile is isosmotic** (~300 mOsm). Water enters paracellularly (solvent drag through tight junctions) and transcellularly via **AQP9** (sinusoidal) and **AQP8** (canalicular; cAMP recruits AQP8 from intracellular vesicles, the rate-limiting step for transcellular water during choleresis).
- **Two functions of bile:** sole excretory route for many non-renally-excreted solutes; supply of bile salts/acids for lipid digestion and absorption. Predominant cation Na⁺; major anions Cl⁻ and HCO₃⁻. Functionally important solutes: micelle-forming bile acids, phospholipids, IgA (inhibits bacterial growth). Bile is also the main route of **cholesterol elimination** (directly and as bile acids).
- **Bile-acid synthesis from cholesterol** is rate-limited by **cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1)**, an SER P-450 enzyme — 14 reactions across four organelles yield the primary acids cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid. Hepatic basal synthesis ~600 mg/day (replacing fecal loss).
- **Canalicular flow = bile-acid-dependent + bile-acid-independent** (Fig. 46-10). Bile-acid-dependent flow rises ~linearly with bile-acid secretion; the counterions accompanying micellar (low-osmotic-coefficient) bile salts provide the main osmotic driving force. Bile-acid-independent flow is driven largely by secretion of small organics (e.g., **glutathione**, present at high bile concentration). Bile acids also trap/sequester cholesterol and phospholipid in luminal micelles, so their excretion rises with bile-acid output. **Ursodeoxycholic acid** boosts flow by stimulating biliary HCO₃⁻ excretion.
- **Ductular (cholangiocyte) secretion:** cholangiocytes have an apical **Cl-HCO₃ exchanger (AE2)**, apical Cl channels including **CFTR**, and aquaporins (AQP1). In a pancreatic-duct-like mechanism, CFTR recycles Cl⁻ for AE2 to secrete an HCO₃⁻-rich fluid. **Secretin** (receptors basolateral on cholangiocytes), **glucagon**, and **VIP** raise cAMP → watery, alkaline, bile-acid-poor **choleresis**. **Somatostatin** lowers cAMP and inhibits bile flow (opposite of secretin). Cholangiocytes can also reabsorb fluid/electrolytes (basis of post-cholecystectomy ductal adaptation) and recycle solutes (e.g., ursodeoxycholic acid) back to hepatocytes via the peribiliary plexus.
- **Gallbladder:** during fasting up to 50% of hepatic bile is diverted in and concentrated 10–20× by isotonic NaCl/NaHCO₃ reabsorption through the leaky epithelium (parallel apical Na-H + Cl-HCO₃ exchange; basolateral Na-K pump). Na-H outstrips Cl-HCO₃ → net H⁺ secretion **acidifies** bile, increasing solubility of calcium salts and reducing pigment-stone formation. **CCK** (duodenal I cells) contracts the gallbladder and ejects bile during a meal; **FGF19** (induced in ileum by bile acids) relaxes gallbladder smooth muscle to allow refilling — CCK + FGF19 set the periodicity of emptying/filling. Mucus protects the epithelium from bile-salt detergency.
- **Sphincter of Oddi:** a 4–6 mm zone; basal duct pressure 5–10 mm Hg (vs. ~0 in duodenum); basal tone prevents duodenal reflux and, with phasic peristaltic contractions, acts as an adjustable occluder regulating bile flow. Bile partitions into gallbladder vs. duodenum by the relative resistances of the cystic-duct and sphincter pathways.

### Equations / quantitative relations
- **Bile-acid mass balance:** hepatic basal synthesis ≈ 600 mg/day ≈ daily fecal loss; total gut bile-acid pool ≈ 3 g; the pool recirculates ~4–12 times/day. The enterohepatic circulation must reclaim ≥95% of secreted bile salts.
- **Daily bile volumes:** hepatic bile ~900 mL/day; ~450 mL/day diverted to gallbladder during fasting; ~500 mL/day delivered to duodenum; gallbladder concentrates solutes 10–20×.

## Section 5 — Enterohepatic circulation of bile acids (pp. 962–964)

### Subsection headings
- **The enterohepatic circulation of bile acids is a loop consisting of secretion by the liver, reabsorption by the intestine, and return to the liver in portal blood for repeat secretion into bile** (pp. 962–963)
- **Efficient intestinal conservation of bile acids depends on active apical absorption in the terminal ileum and passive absorption throughout the intestinal tract** (pp. 963–964)

### Core claims
- The liver secretes ~12–36 g of bile acid per day (varies with meals/fat), but synthesizes only ~600 mg/day de novo — so the gut must recycle bile acids extremely efficiently. The ~3 g pool recirculates ~4–12 times/day; if ileal reabsorption fails (e.g., ileal resection), de novo synthesis can rise to 4–6 g/day.
- **Passive absorption** occurs along the whole small intestine and colon by ionic or **nonionic diffusion** (nonionic ~10× greater). Nonionic diffusion favors the neutral protonated form — maximal at low luminal pH and high bile-acid pKₐ. At intestinal pH 5.5–6.5, **~50% of unconjugated** acids, a small fraction of glycine-conjugates, and **few taurine-conjugates** are protonated. So absorbability ranks unconjugated > glycine-conjugated > taurine-conjugated; among unconjugated, the more lipophilic (chenodeoxycholate, deoxycholate) diffuse better than cholic acid.
- **Active absorption** is confined to the **terminal ileum** and preferentially recovers the negatively charged conjugated salts that passive diffusion misses. The apical step uses the Na⁺-dependent **ASBT (apical Na/bile-salt transporter, SLC10A2)** — a close relative of hepatocyte NTCP — with saturation kinetics and Na⁺ requirement; basolateral exit is via the heteromeric **OSTα-OSTβ.**
- In portal blood, bile acids ride **albumin** (and some lipoproteins). Hepatic extraction (% removed per pass) is highest for hydrophilic bile acids and lowest for protein-bound hydrophobic ones.
- **Colonic bacterial modification:** unabsorbed bile acids are **deconjugated** (BA-Z → H·BA) and **7α-dehydroxylated** to secondary acids (**deoxycholate, lithocholate**). Up to a third of deoxycholate is reabsorbed by nonionic diffusion; relatively insoluble lithocholate is largely excreted. Returning secondary acids may be re-conjugated.
- The loop is driven by **two mechanical pumps** (gallbladder motility; intestinal peristalsis) and **two chemical pumps** (ileal energy-dependent transporters; hepatocyte energy-dependent transporters).
- **FXR (farnesoid X receptor)** feedback: primary bile acids are potent FXR agonists. FXR produces negative feedback by four mechanisms — (1) hepatic FXR → **SHP** → inhibits **LRH-1** → represses **CYP7A1** (less bile-acid synthesis); (2) ileal FXR → **FGF19** → FGFR4 in liver → represses CYP7A1; (3) hepatic FXR upregulates **BSEP** and downregulates **NTCP** (less intracellular bile acid); (4) ileal FXR via SHP downregulates **ASBT** and upregulates basolateral **OSTα-OSTβ.** The bile-acid GPCR **TGR5** (on cholangiocyte/gallbladder apical membrane) raises cAMP and increases Cl⁻ secretion.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "This recycling, known as the enterohepatic circulation, occurs as the terminal ileum and colon reabsorb bile acids and return them to the liver in the portal blood." (p. 962)
- > "However, the enterohepatic circulation must eventually reclaim 95% or more of these secreted bile salts." (p. 962)
- > "The major component of bile acid absorption is active and occurs only in the terminal ileum." (p. 962)
- > "The Na⁺-dependent transporter responsible for the apical step of active absorption is known as the apical Na/bile-salt transporter (ASBT or SLC10A2), a close relative of the hepatocyte transporter NTCP." (p. 963)
- > "Primary bile acids are potent agonists of FXR, which transcriptionally regulates several genes involved in bile acid homeostasis, producing negative feedback…" (p. 964)

## Section 6 — The liver as a metabolic organ (pp. 964–971)

### Subsection headings
- **The liver can serve as either a source or a sink for glucose** (pp. 964–965)
- **The liver synthesizes a variety of important plasma proteins … and metabolizes dietary amino acids** (pp. 965–966)
- **The liver obtains dietary triacylglycerols and cholesterol by taking up remnant chylomicrons via receptor-mediated endocytosis** (pp. 966–968)
- **Cholesterol, synthesized primarily in the liver, is an important component of cell membranes and serves as a precursor for bile acids and steroid hormones** (pp. 968–969)
- **The liver is the central organ for cholesterol homeostasis and for the synthesis and degradation of LDL** (pp. 969–970)
- **The liver is the prime site for metabolism and storage of the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K** (pp. 970–971)
- **The liver stores copper and iron** (p. 971)

### Core claims
- **Glucose homeostasis:** fasting [glucose] ~4–5 mM. Between meals (low insulin, high glucagon) the liver is a **source** via gluconeogenesis (from lactate, pyruvate, amino acids) and glycogenolysis (glycogen ~7%–10% of liver weight; yields free glucose, unlike muscle which yields lactate). After meals (high insulin) it is a **sink**, taking up glucose for glycolysis, glycogen synthesis, or conversion to fat.
- **Protein synthesis:** the liver exports plasma proteins at 15–50 g/day (Table 46-3) — albumin (colloid osmotic pressure), coagulation/fibrinolysis factors, carriage/binding proteins, prohormones (angiotensinogen), apolipoproteins.
- **Amino-acid metabolism:** dietary amino acids are taken up (Na⁺-dependent and -independent transporters, often on both membranes). Excess amino acids are **deaminated** to α-keto acids + NH₄⁺; α-keto acids feed pyruvate / citric-acid-cycle intermediates / acetyl-CoA / acetoacetyl-CoA. The liver detoxifies ~95% of NH₄⁺ via the **urea cycle** (Fig. 46-14) or makes **glutamine**; urea exits via a urea channel (**AQP9**) to be renally excreted. Urea-cycle enzyme deficiencies → life-threatening hyperammonemia. The liver makes >90% of circulating **glutathione.**
- **Lipid/lipoprotein metabolism:** dietary fat → **chylomicrons** (Table 46-4) → LPL on capillary endothelium of adipose/muscle releases fatty acids/glycerol → **remnant chylomicrons** (cholesterol-enriched) are cleared by hepatic **receptor-mediated endocytosis** (syndecan-1, heparan-sulfate proteoglycans, LDL-related receptors; apoE-mediated). Hepatocytes take up LCFAs via **FAT/CD36**, **FATP5/SLC27A5** (~50% of hepatic FA uptake), and cytosolic **FABP1.** Fatty acids undergo mitochondrial **β-oxidation** → acetyl-CoA → citric-acid cycle or → **ketone bodies** (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone); the liver is the only organ that makes acetoacetate for export but does not use it itself. Re-esterified fat is exported as **VLDL.**
- **Cholesterol homeostasis:** the liver is the hub. Sources: dietary (remnant chylomicrons), de novo synthesis, LDL uptake. Exports: bile acids + biliary cholesterol, and VLDL. The most important elimination route is hepatic conversion to bile acids. De novo synthesis (Fig. 46-16) goes acetyl-CoA → … → cholesterol; the **rate-limiting step is HMG-CoA reductase** (HMG-CoA → mevalonate), under negative feedback by cellular cholesterol and the target of **statins.** SREBPs (esp. SREBP-2) transcriptionally control the biosynthetic and uptake genes (Box 46-4).
- **LDL/HDL trafficking:** the liver degrades ~40%–60% of LDL (LDL half-life 2–3 days; no other tissue >10%). **HDL** (apoA-I, apoA-II) acquires cholesterol from peripheral cells via **ABCA1**; **LCAT** esterifies it; HDL-CE is taken up by hepatic **SR-B1** (selective uptake, no endocytosis) and the cholesterol is targeted to bile. **CETP** transfers CE from HDL to VLDL/IDL/LDL. The HDL→liver→bile route = **reverse cholesterol transport** (atheroprotective).
- **Fat-soluble vitamins:** **A** — stored mainly in stellate (Ito) cells (>80% of hepatic vitamin A), carried by RBP; **D** — first activation step (25-hydroxylation) is hepatic, then renal 1-hydroxylation; **E** — α-/γ-tocopherol; liver discriminates and re-secretes α-tocopherol in VLDL; **K** — bacterial origin; essential cofactor for ER γ-glutamyl carboxylase that carboxylates coagulation factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C and S; deficiency (cholestasis, fat malabsorption, antibiotics) causes bleeding.
- **Copper:** ~half of dietary Cu absorbed in jejunum, reaches liver bound to albumin; imported via **CTR1 (SLC31A1)**; chaperone **Atox1** delivers it to the Wilson-disease P-type ATPase **ATP7B** (trans-Golgi) for incorporation into **ceruloplasmin** (secreted to blood) or biliary excretion. >80% of absorbed copper is excreted in bile (1.2–2.4 mg/day). **Wilson disease** (Box 46-5): ATP7B mutation → impaired biliary Cu excretion → toxic Cu accumulation (liver, brain, kidney, cornea — Kayser-Fleischer rings); low serum ceruloplasmin; treated by chelation (penicillamine).
- **Iron:** absorbed by duodenum, carried on liver-made **transferrin**; hepatocytes take up Fe via transferrin receptors, store it as **ferritin** (Fe is toxic in excess; overload → **hemochromatosis**). Hepatocytes synthesize **hepcidin** (HAMP gene), which lowers plasma iron by downregulating the efflux pump **FPN1** in intestine and macrophages; hepcidin rises with iron loading/inflammation and falls with anemia/hypoxia.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The de novo synthesis of glucose from lactate, pyruvate, and amino acids—gluconeogenesis—is one of the liver's most important functions; it is essential for maintaining a normal plasma concentration of glucose…" (p. 964)
- > "The liver synthesizes plasma proteins at a maximum rate of 15 to 50 g/day." (p. 965)
- > "The liver detoxifies ~95% of the NH₃ through a series of reactions known as the urea cycle…" (p. 965)
- > "The rate-limiting step in cholesterol synthesis is the conversion of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) to mevalonate by HMG-CoA reductase…" (p. 968)
- > "The HDL-mediated removal of cholesterol from peripheral tissues via both SR-B1 and CETP for transport to the liver and excretion in bile is known as reverse cholesterol transport." (p. 970)
- > "More than 80% of the copper absorbed each day is excreted in bile, for a total of 1.2 to 2.4 mg/day." (p. 971)

### Equations / quantitative relations
- **Cholesterol balance (Table 46-5, g/day for a 70-kg human):** intestinal absorption 0.1–0.5; synthesis 1.05; biliary secretion 0.9; cholesterol consumed in bile-acid synthesis 0.5; cholesterol secreted in VLDLs 0.25–1.75. Steady state: hepatic sterol excretion (cholesterol + bile acids) = synthesis + dietary absorption.
- **Cholesterol synthesis (Fig. 46-16):** acetyl-CoA → acetoacetyl-CoA → HMG-CoA → (**HMG-CoA reductase, rate-limiting**) → mevalonate → (8 steps) → lanosterol → (19 steps) → cholesterol.

## Glossary

- **Hepatocyte** — polarized secretory epithelial cell; ~80% of liver volume; apical (canalicular) vs. basolateral (sinusoidal) membranes.
- **Bile canaliculus** — ~1 µm channel between apical membranes of two hepatocytes; first compartment of bile.
- **Space of Disse** — perisinusoidal space between fenestrated endothelium and hepatocyte basolateral membrane.
- **Kupffer cell** — fixed sinusoidal macrophage; 80%–90% of RES fixed macrophages.
- **Stellate (Ito) cell** — vitamin A storage; activates to fibrogenic myofibroblast → cirrhosis.
- **Portal triad / portal tract** — hepatic artery + portal vein + bile duct (+ lymphatics, nerves).
- **Classic lobule / portal lobule / portal acinus** — three organizing units (blood-drainage / bile-drainage / oxygenation-gradient).
- **Zonation (zone 1 periportal → zone 3 pericentral)** — O₂/solute gradient driving enzyme heterogeneity.
- **Canals of Hering** — terminal bile ductules linking canaliculi to ducts.
- **Sphincter of Oddi** — regulates bile + pancreatic flow into duodenum; relaxed by CCK (VIP).
- **NTCP (SLC10A1)** — Na⁺-dependent basolateral bile-acid uptake.
- **OATP1B1/1B3/2B1** — Na⁺-independent basolateral organic-anion uptake; loss → Rotor syndrome.
- **BSEP (ABCB11)** — canalicular bile-salt export pump; defect → PFIC2.
- **MRP2 (ABCC2)** — canalicular conjugate/bilirubin export; defect → Dubin-Johnson syndrome.
- **MDR3 (ABCB4)** — canalicular phosphatidylcholine flippase.
- **ABCG5/ABCG8** — canalicular cholesterol export; defect → sitosterolemia.
- **ASBT (SLC10A2)** — apical Na/bile-salt transporter, terminal ileum; basolateral OSTα-OSTβ.
- **CYP7A1 (cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase)** — rate-limiting enzyme of bile-acid synthesis.
- **Primary vs. secondary bile acids** — cholic/chenodeoxycholic (hepatic) vs. deoxycholic/lithocholic (bacterial 7α-dehydroxylation).
- **Conjugation** — glycine (pKₐ ~3.7) or taurine (pKₐ ~1.5) raises water solubility and ionization.
- **Enterohepatic circulation** — secretion → ileal/colonic reabsorption → portal return → re-secretion; reclaims ≥95%.
- **FXR / FGF19 / SHP** — bile-acid feedback repressing CYP7A1 and tuning transporters.
- **UGT1A1** — bilirubin glucuronidation; deficiency → Gilbert (mild) / Crigler-Najjar (severe) syndromes.
- **Phase I / II / III** — CYP450 oxidation / conjugation / ABC-transporter efflux.
- **SXR/PXR, CAR, AhR** — xenobiotic nuclear receptors coordinating biotransformation.
- **HMG-CoA reductase** — rate-limiting cholesterol-synthesis enzyme; statin target; SREBP-controlled.
- **SR-B1 / ABCA1 / LCAT / CETP** — reverse cholesterol transport machinery.
- **Ceruloplasmin / ATP7B / CTR1** — copper handling; ATP7B defect → Wilson disease.
- **Hepcidin (HAMP) / ferritin / transferrin / FPN1** — iron homeostasis; overload → hemochromatosis.
