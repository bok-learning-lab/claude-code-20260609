## Section 1 — The islets of Langerhans (pp. 1035–1036)

### Subsection headings
- **The islets of Langerhans are endocrine and paracrine tissue** (pp. 1035–1036)

### Core claims
- The pancreas has two glandular systems: **exocrine** (digestive enzymes + HCO₃⁻ into the gut lumen) and **endocrine** (the islets of Langerhans). Islets are only **1%–2%** of pancreatic mass; the normal human pancreas holds **500,000 to several million** islets, each **50–300 µm** in diameter.
- **Cell types** (Table 51-1): **β cells** (most numerous, central) secrete insulin, proinsulin, C peptide, and amylin/IAPP; **α cells** secrete glucagon; **δ cells** secrete somatostatin; **F (PP) cells** secrete pancreatic polypeptide. (The book's catalogue predates routine listing of the ε/ghrelin cell, so this chapter names four secretory types.)
- Islets are **richly perfused** (>5× myocardial blood flow per gram) and receive both sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation.
- **Three communication modes** integrate islet output:
  1. **Humoral** — blood courses from islet **center → periphery**, carrying secretagogues; cells downstream sense the hormonal products of upstream cells. Glucagon is a potent insulin secretagogue; insulin modestly inhibits glucagon release; somatostatin potently inhibits both.
  2. **Cell–cell** — gap and tight junctions couple islet cells; gap junctions may coordinate insulin and glucagon secretion.
  3. **Neural** — cholinergic (vagal) stimulation augments insulin; adrenergic stimulation is stimulatory (β) or inhibitory (α) depending on which receptor dominates.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The islets are spread throughout the pancreas and in aggregate comprise only 1% to 2% of its tissue mass." (p. 1035)
- > "B cells are the most numerous type of secretory cell within the islets; they are located throughout the islet but are particularly numerous in the center." (p. 1035)
- > "glucagon is a potent insulin secretagogue, insulin modestly inhibits glucagon release, and somatostatin potently inhibits the secretion of both insulin and glucagon." (p. 1035)
- > "Cholinergic stimulation augments insulin secretion. Adrenergic stimulation can have either a stimulatory or inhibitory effect, depending on whether β-adrenergic (stimulatory) or α-adrenergic (inhibitory) stimulation dominates." (pp. 1035–1036)

## Section 2 — Insulin: synthesis and secretion (pp. 1036–1042)

### Subsection headings
- **Insulin replenishes fuel reserves in muscle, liver, and adipose tissue** (pp. 1036–1037)
- **B cells synthesize and secrete insulin** (pp. 1037–1038)
- **Glucose is the major regulator of insulin secretion** (p. 1038)
- **Metabolism of glucose by the β cell triggers insulin secretion** (pp. 1039–1041)
- **Neural and humoral factors modulate insulin secretion** (pp. 1041–1042)

### Core claims — biosynthesis
- Insulin comes only from islet **β cells**; encoded by a single gene on the short arm of **chromosome 11**. Glucose stimulates both synthesis and secretion, and the glucose **must be metabolized** to act.
- **Processing cascade** (Fig. 51-2): mRNA encodes **preproinsulin** = leader (~24 aa) + domains **B–C–A**. The leader is cleaved co-translationally in the rough ER → **proinsulin** (B–C–A). In the trans-Golgi and secretory granules, proteases excise the **31-amino-acid C peptide**, leaving mature **insulin** = A chain (21 aa) + B chain (30 aa), **51 aa total**, joined by **two disulfide bonds**. Insulin associates with **zinc** in the granule.
- **C peptide** has no established biological action but is secreted **1:1 molar** with insulin, is **not extracted by the liver**, and is excreted in urine — making it a faithful marker of insulin secretion. **Proinsulin** has ~1/10 the potency of insulin and is secreted at only ~5% the rate.
- **First-pass hepatic extraction**: ~60% of secreted insulin is removed on first pass through the liver; portal [insulin] is 3–4× systemic. C peptide is not extracted, so systemic C-peptide tracks secretion while systemic insulin does not.

### Core claims — glucose sensing and GSIS
- Glucose is the dominant secretagogue; arginine, leucine, small keto acids (α-ketoisocaproate, α-ketoglutarate), and fructose are weak stimulators. Non-metabolizable hexoses (3-O-methylglucose, 2-deoxyglucose) **do not** stimulate — confirming that **metabolism**, not mere transport, drives secretion.
- **The seven-step glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) cascade** (Fig. 51-4), the central mechanism of the chapter:

$$
\underbrace{\text{glucose} \xrightarrow{\text{GLUT2}} \text{cytosol}}_{\text{Step 1}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\text{glycolysis} + \text{citric acid cycle}}_{\text{Step 2 (glucokinase rate-limiting)}}
\;\Rightarrow\;
\uparrow \frac{[\mathrm{ATP}]_i}{[\mathrm{ADP}]_i},\ \uparrow\frac{[\mathrm{NADH}]_i}{[\mathrm{NAD^+}]_i}
$$

$$
\underbrace{\uparrow\tfrac{[\mathrm{ATP}]}{[\mathrm{ADP}]} \Rightarrow \text{close } K_{\mathrm{ATP}} \text{ channel}}_{\text{Step 3}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\downarrow g_K \Rightarrow \text{depolarization}}_{\text{Step 4}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\text{open voltage-gated } \mathrm{Ca^{2+}} \text{ channel}}_{\text{Step 5}}
$$

$$
\underbrace{\uparrow[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i \;(+\ \mathrm{Ca^{2+}\text{-induced }Ca^{2+}\ release})}_{\text{Step 6}}
\;\to\;
\underbrace{\text{exocytosis of insulin granules}}_{\text{Step 7 (Ca/calmodulin cascade)}}
$$

- **The KATP channel** is an **octamer**: four **Kir6.2** pore subunits + four **SUR1 (sulfonylurea receptor)** subunits (see p. 198). **Sulfonylureas bind SUR**, lower the channel's open probability, depolarize the β cell, and thereby enhance GSIS (Box 51-3) — the pharmacological hinge of type 2 diabetes therapy.
- **Other modulators** feed in parallel: glucagon, CCK, β-agonists, and acetylcholine raise secretion via **adenylyl cyclase→cAMP→PKA** or **phospholipase C→IP₃/PKC**; somatostatin, galanin, and α-agonists inhibit (largely by inhibiting adenylyl cyclase).

### Core claims — biphasic secretion and incretins
- An **IV glucose bolus** elicits **biphasic** secretion: an **acute (first) phase** lasting 2–5 min from **preformed, membrane-docked granules**, then a sustained **second phase** (preformed + newly synthesized insulin) as long as glucose stays elevated (Fig. 51-3C). **Loss of first-phase secretion** is one of the **earliest defects** in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes.
- **Incretin effect** (Box 51-2): oral glucose evokes **more** insulin than IV glucose producing the same glycemic curve, because gut hormones "prime" the β cell. The three incretins released on feeding: **CCK** (I cells), **GLP-1** (L cells, the most potent — half-life <2 min), and **GIP / glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide** (K cells).
- **Incretin pharmacology**: **exenatide** (a long-acting analog of exendin-4, from Gila monster saliva) is injected; **gliptins** (DPP-4 inhibitors) are oral agents that block GLP-1 degradation — both treat type 2 diabetes.
- **Neural modulation**: β-adrenergic (isoproterenol) augments, α-adrenergic (norepinephrine) suppresses. Because postganglionic sympathetics release **norepinephrine** (α > β at the islet), sympathetic celiac stimulation **inhibits** insulin; **vagal/cholinergic** stimulation **augments** it. During exercise, α-adrenergic inhibition prevents hypoglycemia.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Most of the insulin (~60%) that is secreted into the portal blood is removed in a first pass through the liver. In contrast, C peptide is not extracted by the liver at all." (p. 1038)
- > "Other hexoses that are transported into the B cell but that cannot be metabolized (e.g., 3-O-methylglucose or 2-deoxyglucose) do not stimulate insulin secretion." (p. 1039)
- > "The K⁺ channel (K_ATP …) is an octamer of four Kir6.2 channels … and four sulfonylurea receptors (SURs)." (p. 1039)
- > "The acute-phase or first-phase insulin response lasts only 2 to 5 minutes, whereas the second-phase insulin response persists as long as the blood glucose level remains elevated." (p. 1038)
- > "GLP-1 … perhaps the most important incretin yet discovered, has a very short half-life in plasma (<2 min)." (p. 1041)
- > "The sulfonylureas enhance insulin secretion by binding to the SUR subunits … of K_ATP channels, thereby decreasing the likelihood that these channels will be open." (Box 51-3, p. 1039)

## Section 3 — The insulin receptor and signal transduction (pp. 1041–1044)

### Subsection headings
- **The insulin receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase** (pp. 1041–1043)
- **High levels of insulin lead to downregulation of insulin receptors** (p. 1044)

### Core claims
- The **insulin receptor** is a **heterotetramer**: two extracellular **α chains** (insulin binds the cysteine-rich region) and two membrane-spanning **β chains**, assembled β-α-α-β and joined by disulfide bonds (Fig. 51-5). It is **~50% homologous** to the **IGF-1 receptor** overall, **>80%** in the tyrosine-kinase region — so very high insulin can cross-activate the IGF-1 receptor and vice versa.
- The **β-chain intracellular domain has tyrosine kinase activity** that rises sharply on insulin binding. The receptor autophosphorylates and phosphorylates **IRS-1/2/3/4** and **SHC** at tyrosines; these are **docking proteins** that recruit SH2-domain effectors.
- **Three downstream pathways** (Fig. 51-6):
  1. **PI3K → Akt (PKB) → mTOR**: PI3K binds phospho-IRS, converts **PIP₂ → PIP₃**; this is the **metabolic limb** — GLUT4 translocation, glycogen synthesis (via inhibiting GSK-3, activating glycogen synthase), lipid synthesis, protein synthesis, and **suppression of gluconeogenic genes** (↓G6Pase, ↓PEPCK via FOXO1 phosphorylation/export).
  2. **Ras → MEK → MAPK**: triggered by SHC or GRB2; the **mitogenic limb** → gene expression and growth. **IRS-2 deletion** causes diabetes (partly via impaired β-cell secretion); IRS-1 deletion merely makes small mice.
  3. **Other SH2-protein binding** to phosphotyrosines on the receptor/IRS.
- **Downregulation**: surface receptors greatly exceed the number needed (in adipocytes, maximal glucose response at only **~5% receptor occupancy** — "spare receptors"). Chronic high insulin → fewer receptors (↓synthesis, ↑degradation), lowering **sensitivity** without lowering the **maximal effect** (Fig. 51-7). In **type 2 diabetes**, the dominant defect is **post-receptor signaling impairment** (↓receptor kinase, ↓PI3K, impaired GLUT4 recruitment), not merely receptor number; serine phosphorylation of receptor/IRS by inflammatory p38-MAPK/NF-κB pathways contributes (Box 51-4).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The insulin receptor … is a heterotetramer, with two identical α chains and two identical β chains." (p. 1042)
- > "The intracellular domain of the β chain possesses tyrosine kinase activity, which increases markedly when insulin binds to sites on the α chains of the receptor." (p. 1042)
- > "IRS-2 deletion does cause diabetes, in part because of impaired insulin secretion by the pancreatic β cell!" (p. 1043)
- > "the glucose response to insulin is maximal when only ~5% of the receptors are occupied; that is, the target cells have many 'spare' receptors for insulin." (p. 1044)
- > "the major effects in type 2 diabetes apparently are not the result of a decrease in receptor number, but rather are caused by impairment in signaling downstream from the receptor." (p. 1044)

#### Figure 51-6 — Insulin signal-transduction system *(viewed)*

The detailed signaling map. Insulin binds the RTK; the **β-chain tyrosine-kinase domains** autophosphorylate and phosphorylate **IRS-1/2/3/4** and **SHC**.

- **Left limb (PI3K/Akt — metabolic)**: PI3K converts **PI-4,5-P₂ (PIP₂) → PI-3,4,5-P₃ (PIP₃)**; PIP₃ recruits **PDK1**, which activates **Akt** and **mTOR**.

- **Right limb (Ras/MAPK — mitogenic)**: **SHC / GRB2 → Ras → Raf → MEK → MAPK**, plus **JNK** and **p38 kinase** branches, converging on **modulation of transcription ⇒ ↑gene expression and growth**. p38 also feeds inflammatory serine-phosphorylation (insulin resistance).

## Section 4 — Insulin actions on target tissues (pp. 1044–1050)

### Subsection headings
- **In liver, insulin promotes conversion of glucose to glycogen stores or to triacylglycerols** (pp. 1044–1047)
- **In muscle, insulin promotes the uptake of glucose and its storage as glycogen** (pp. 1047–1048)
- **In adipocytes, insulin promotes glucose uptake and conversion to TAGs for storage** (pp. 1048–1050)

### Core claims — liver (Fig. 51-8)
Glucose enters hepatocytes via **GLUT2** (insulin-independent, always abundant). Insulin has **four major effects**:
1. **Glycogen synthesis**: ↑glucokinase (box 1), activate **glycogen synthase** by net dephosphorylation (box 2), inhibit **glycogen phosphorylase** (box 3) and **G6Pase** (box 4). The same phosphatase that activates glycogen synthase inhibits phosphorylase ⇒ net glycogen formation.
2. **Glycolysis (↑) / gluconeogenesis (↓)**: ↑glucokinase (1), ↑phosphofructokinase (5, via the allosteric activator **fructose-2,6-bisphosphate**), ↑pyruvate kinase (6), ↑hexose monophosphate shunt (7), ↑pyruvate dehydrogenase (8); ↓**PEPCK** transcription (9) and ↓**fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase** (10).
3. **Lipogenesis**: ↑acetyl-CoA carboxylase (11, → malonyl CoA) and ↑fatty-acid synthase (12); malonyl CoA **inhibits CAT I (13)**, blocking fatty-acid entry into mitochondria ⇒ esterification to **TAGs**, stored or exported as **VLDL**.
4. **Protein metabolism**: ↑protein synthesis (14), ↓proteolysis (15).

Net: insulin makes the liver take up glucose and store it as glycogen or convert it to fat, while suppressing fat oxidation — the liver burns carbohydrate preferentially.

### Core claims — muscle (Fig. 51-9)
Four effects: (1) **GLUT4 recruitment** — glucose enters muscle principally via the **insulin-sensitive GLUT4** transporter (recruited from cytosolic vesicles, ↑Vmax). (2) **Glycogen synthesis** via **hexokinase** (1, not glucokinase) and glycogen synthase (2). (3) **Glycolysis/oxidation** via phosphofructokinase (3) and pyruvate dehydrogenase (4); essentially **no gluconeogenesis** in muscle. (4) **Protein synthesis** (5) and **↓proteolysis** (6) — preserving muscle mass. **Exercise and insulin act synergistically** on GLUT4 recruitment and glucose oxidation (exploited therapeutically).

### Core claims — adipose (Fig. 51-10)
Four effects: (1) **GLUT4 recruitment** for glucose entry. (2) Glycolysis to **α-glycerol phosphate** (the glycerol backbone for TAGs) and onward to fatty acids via **pyruvate dehydrogenase (1)** and **acetyl-CoA carboxylase (2)**; little glycogen made. (3) **Esterification** of α-glycerol phosphate with fatty acids → **TAGs** stored in lipid droplets; insulin **inhibits HSL/ATGL (3)** to block lipolysis. (4) Insulin induces **lipoprotein lipase (LPL)**, exported to the capillary endothelial surface where it hydrolyzes chylomicron/VLDL TAGs to release fatty acids for adipocyte uptake.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Glucose enters the hepatocyte from the blood via GLUT2 … GLUT2 is present in abundance in the liver plasma membrane, even in the absence of insulin, and its activity is not influenced by insulin." (p. 1044)
- > "in muscle, unlike in the liver, glucose crosses the plasma membrane principally via GLUT4, an insulin-sensitive glucose transporter." (p. 1047)
- > "Insulin markedly stimulates GLUT4 in both muscle … and fat … by a process involving recruitment of preformed transporters from a membranous compartment in the cell cytosol out to the plasma membrane." (p. 1047)
- > "insulin acts to promote the synthesis and storage of fat and diminish its oxidation in liver." (p. 1047)
- > "insulin induces the synthesis of a different enzyme—lipoprotein lipase (LPL). This lipase does not act on the lipid stored within the adipose cell. Rather, the adipocyte exports the LPL to the endothelial cell." (p. 1050)

## Section 5 — Glucagon (pp. 1050–1053)

### Subsection headings
- **Pancreatic α cells secrete glucagon in response to ingested protein** (pp. 1050–1051)
- **Glucagon, acting through cAMP, promotes the synthesis of glucose by the liver** (pp. 1051–1052)
- **Glucagon promotes oxidation of fat in the liver, which can lead to ketogenesis** (pp. 1052–1053)

### Core claims
- **Glucagon** is a **31-amino-acid** peptide (~3500 Da) from islet **α cells** (gene on chromosome 2). It is the major counter-regulatory hormone; its principal target is the **liver**, reached first via the portal blood.
- **Proglucagon processing is tissue-specific** (Fig. 51-11): in pancreatic **α cells**, proteases yield **GRPP + glucagon + the major proglucagon fragment**; in intestinal **L cells**, the same precursor yields **glicentin, GLP-1, IP-2, and GLP-2** — i.e., the **incretin** GLP-1 rather than glucagon. (Glicentin contains the glucagon sequence but does not bind glucagon receptors.)
- **Secretion**: stimulated by **amino acids** (ingested protein) and by **low glucose**; **glucose inhibits** glucagon secretion. Note the asymmetry — both glucose and amino acids stimulate β cells, but **only amino acids** stimulate α cells. GLP-1 inhibits glucagon secretion.
- **Mechanism** (Fig. 51-12): glucagon → **Gαs-coupled receptor → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA**, which phosphorylates regulatory enzymes — the **mirror image of insulin** (insulin dephosphorylates the same key enzymes; glucagon phosphorylates them).
- **Glycogenolysis cascade**: PKA → phosphorylase kinase → converts **phosphorylase b → active phosphorylase a**; PKA also phosphorylates **inhibitor-1**, which blocks **PP1** (preventing dephosphorylation/inactivation of the glycogenolytic enzymes and inactivating glycogen synthase). Net: **glycogen breakdown**.
- **Gluconeogenesis (genomic)**: PKA phosphorylates **CREB**, which (with **PGC-1α**) raises transcription of **G6Pase** and **PEPCK** — opposed by insulin (which raises SREBP-1 and phosphorylates/exports FOXO1).
- **Ketogenesis**: glucagon **inhibits acetyl-CoA carboxylase → ↓malonyl CoA → de-represses CAT I**, allowing fatty acids into mitochondria for β-oxidation. When fatty-acid flux exceeds ATP demand, partial oxidation yields the ketone bodies **β-hydroxybutyrate** and **acetoacetate**, which the CNS can use (fatty acids cannot cross the blood-brain barrier).
- **Insulin:glucagon ratio** governs the direction of hepatic fuel flux. Conceptually, hepatic output is set by the **ratio**, not either hormone alone:

$$
\frac{[\text{insulin}]}{[\text{glucagon}]}\ \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{anabolic liver: glycogenesis, lipogenesis, } \downarrow\text{glucose output}
$$

$$
\frac{[\text{insulin}]}{[\text{glucagon}]}\ \downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{catabolic liver: glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, ketogenesis, } \uparrow\text{glucose output}
$$

  After an **overnight fast** (low insulin), glucagon drives hepatic glucose production for the brain; after a **protein meal**, glucagon secreted alongside insulin **prevents hypoglycemia** that insulin alone (with no dietary carbohydrate) would cause.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Glucagon is particularly important in stimulating glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, and ketogenesis." (p. 1050)
- > "only amino acids stimulate glucagon secretion by α cells; glucose inhibits glucagon secretion." (pp. 1050–1051)
- > "Whereas insulin leads to the dephosphorylation of certain key enzymes (i.e., glycogen synthase, acetyl CoA carboxylase, phosphorylase), glucagon leads to their phosphorylation." (p. 1051)
- > "During fasting, the decline in insulin and the increase in glucagon promote ketogenesis; this process is of vital importance to the CNS, which can use keto acids but not fatty acids as fuel." (p. 1053)
- > "glucagon secreted in response to a protein meal balances insulin's action on the liver and thus maintains glucose production and avoids hypoglycemia." (p. 1052)

## Section 6 — Somatostatin and other islet products (p. 1053)

### Subsection headings
- **Somatostatin inhibits the secretion of growth hormone, insulin, and other hormones** (p. 1053)

### Core claims
- **Somatostatin** is made in islet **δ cells** (also D cells of the GI tract, hypothalamus, and CNS). It exists as **14- and 28-amino-acid** forms; the 14-aa form (C-terminal portion of the 28-aa form) carries the biological activity and predominates in the hypothalamus, while the 28-aa form predominates in the GI tract.
- It **broadly inhibits** secretion of growth hormone, insulin, glucagon, gastrin, VIP, and TSH. The analog **octreotide** treats GH-secreting (acromegaly), insulin-secreting (insulinoma), and serotonin-secreting (carcinoid) tumors.
- **Paracrine caveat**: pancreatic-venous somatostatin is high enough to inhibit basal insulin secretion, but because blood flows **center (β) → periphery (δ)**, somatostatin's feedback onto its own islet's β cells is minimized; whether it has important paracrine action on β or α cells remains controversial.
- **Pancreatic polypeptide** (F cells) varies with dietary intake but has no clearly established role in mammalian fuel metabolism. Islet tumors may aberrantly secrete gastrin, VIP, or GHRH, but these have no normal islet function.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Somatostatin inhibits the secretion of multiple hormones, including growth hormone, insulin, glucagon, gastrin, vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), and thyroid-stimulating hormone." (p. 1053)
- > "This spatial arrangement minimizes the effect of somatostatin on the islet from which it is secreted." (p. 1053)

## Glossary

- **Islets of Langerhans** — endocrine pancreas; 1–2% of mass; α/β/δ/F cells.
- **β cell** — insulin/proinsulin/C-peptide/amylin; central in islet; GLUT2 + glucokinase glucose sensor.
- **α cell** — glucagon; stimulated by amino acids, inhibited by glucose.
- **δ cell** — somatostatin; broad secretory inhibitor.
- **F (PP) cell** — pancreatic polypeptide.
- **Preproinsulin → proinsulin → insulin + C-peptide** — leader cleaved in ER; C peptide excised in Golgi; insulin = A(21) + B(30), 2 disulfides, 51 aa.
- **C peptide** — 1:1 marker of insulin secretion; not hepatically extracted.
- **GLUT2** — insulin-independent transporter in β cell, hepatocyte (high Km, glucose sensor).
- **GLUT4** — insulin-recruited transporter in muscle and adipose.
- **Glucokinase** — rate-limiting β-cell/hepatic hexokinase; the glucose sensor.
- **KATP channel (Kir6.2/SUR1)** — octameric ATP-sensitive K⁺ channel; closed by ↑ATP/ADP; sulfonylurea target.
- **GSIS** — glucose-stimulated insulin secretion: GLUT2 → glycolysis → ↑ATP/ADP → close KATP → depolarize → Ca²⁺ entry → exocytosis.
- **Biphasic secretion** — acute (preformed, 2–5 min) + sustained second phase; first-phase loss is early in diabetes.
- **Incretins** — GLP-1 (L cells, most potent), GIP (K cells), CCK (I cells); amplify GSIS after oral nutrients.
- **Exenatide / gliptins** — GLP-1 analog / DPP-4 inhibitors for type 2 diabetes.
- **Insulin receptor** — α₂β₂ heterotetramer RTK; autophosphorylates; IRS-1/2/3/4, SHC.
- **PI3K/Akt** — metabolic limb: GLUT4, glycogen synthesis, ↓gluconeogenesis (FOXO1, PEPCK, G6Pase).
- **Ras/MAPK** — mitogenic limb: growth, gene expression.
- **Downregulation / spare receptors** — ~5% occupancy gives maximal effect; chronic insulin lowers receptor number.
- **Insulin:glucagon ratio** — sets direction of hepatic carbohydrate/lipid flux.
- **Glucagon** — 31-aa α-cell hormone; Gαs/cAMP/PKA; glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, ketogenesis.
- **Proglucagon** — α-cell → glucagon; L cell → GLP-1, GLP-2, glicentin.
- **CREB / PGC-1α / FOXO1 / SREBP-1** — transcriptional control of gluconeogenic enzymes.
- **Ketone bodies** — β-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate; CNS-usable fasting fuel.
- **Somatostatin** — δ-cell 14/28-aa inhibitor; octreotide analog.
- **Type 1 diabetes (IDDM)** — autoimmune β-cell destruction; insulin-deficient; ketoacidosis-prone.
- **Type 2 diabetes (NIDDM)** — insulin resistance + relative secretory defect; obesity; metabolic syndrome.
