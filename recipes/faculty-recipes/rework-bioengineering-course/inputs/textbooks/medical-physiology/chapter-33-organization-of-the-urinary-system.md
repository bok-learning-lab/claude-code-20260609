## Section 1 — Functional anatomy of the kidney (pp. 722–730)

### Subsection headings
- **The kidneys are paired, retroperitoneal organs with vascular and epithelial elements** (pp. 722–723)
- **The kidneys have a very high blood flow and glomerular capillaries flanked by afferent and efferent arterioles** (pp. 722–723)
- **The functional unit of the kidney is the nephron** (pp. 723–724)
- **The renal corpuscle has three components: vascular elements, the mesangium, and Bowman's capsule and space** (pp. 724–727)
- **The tubule components of the nephron include the proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, and collecting duct** (pp. 727–729)
- **The tightness of tubule epithelia increases from the proximal to the medullary collecting tubule** (pp. 729–730)
- **The JGA is a region where each thick ascending limb contacts its glomerulus** (p. 730)

### Core claims
- **Three essential functions of the kidney**: (1) filter metabolic products and toxins from blood and excrete them in urine; (2) regulate fluid status, electrolyte balance, and acid-base balance; (3) produce or activate hormones for erythrogenesis, Ca²⁺ metabolism, and blood-pressure/blood-flow regulation.
- **Gross anatomy**: paired, bean-shaped, retroperitoneal organs spanning T12–L3. Together <0.5% of body weight; each kidney 125–170 g (men), 115–155 g (women). The **hilus** admits the renal artery and nerves and is the exit for renal vein, lymphatics, and ureter; it opens into the **renal sinus** holding the renal pelvis and major/minor calyces.
- **Cortex vs. medulla**: the **cortex** (granular outer region) owes its granularity to glomeruli and convoluted tubules; the **medulla** (darker inner region) lacks glomeruli and is a parallel array of tubules and small vessels. The medulla is subdivided into **8–18 conical renal pyramids** whose bases face the corticomedullary border and whose tips (papillae) terminate in the renal pelvis, perforated so urine drips into the **minor calyces**.
- **Blood flow**: kidneys receive **~20% of cardiac output** despite being <0.5% of body weight. The renal circulation has a **unique sequence**: high-resistance **afferent arteriole** → high-pressure **glomerular capillary** (filtration) → second high-resistance **efferent arteriole** → low-pressure **peritubular capillary** network (reabsorptive uptake). *(Note: the chapter's prose at p. 722 names the efferent arteriole first then the afferent; physiologically blood flows afferent → glomerulus → efferent.)*
- **Vascular tree**: renal artery → anterior/posterior branches → interlobar → arcuate (skirting the corticomedullary junction) → interlobular arteries → **afferent arterioles** → glomerular capillaries → **efferent arterioles**. Superficial-cortex efferents supply a dense **peritubular capillary** network. Juxtamedullary efferents descend as hairpin **vasa recta** supplying the medulla. **~90% of renal blood perfuses superficial glomeruli and cortex; only ~10% perfuses juxtamedullary glomeruli and medulla.**
- **Lymphatics** drain cortical interstitial fluid (carrying renal hormones such as erythropoietin) toward the hilus; they are **absent from the medulla**, which would otherwise dissipate the high-osmolality interstitium required for concentrating urine.
- **The nephron** is the functional unit, repeated **800,000–1,200,000 times** per kidney (~1 million). Each nephron is independent until its initial collecting tubule merges with another. A nephron = **glomerulus** (source of plasma filtrate) + **tubule** (epithelial structure that converts filtrate to urine), meeting at **Bowman's (glomerular) capsule** surrounding **Bowman's space**.
- **Two nephron populations**: **superficial nephrons** (short loops reaching the outer/inner-medulla boundary) and **juxtamedullary nephrons** (~15% of nephrons; long loops descending to the papillary tip, essential for concentrating urine).
- **The glomerular filtration barrier** has four layers (Fig. 33-3G): (1) endothelial **glycocalyx** of negatively charged GAGs; (2) **fenestrated endothelial cells** with ~70-nm holes (no restriction to water/small solutes; limit only cellular elements like erythrocytes); (3) the **glomerular basement membrane** with three sublayers (lamina rara interna, lamina densa, lamina rara externa), restricting solutes >1 kDa and — via heparan sulfate proteoglycans — large anions; (4) **podocyte foot processes** with **filtration slits** bridged by **slit diaphragms** (pores 4–14 nm). The slit diaphragm is built from **nephrin** and **NEPH1** (extracellular domains "zipping" together), **podocin**, and others.
- **Nephrotic syndrome**: genetic defects in slit-diaphragm proteins make the barrier leaky to large proteins (albuminuria). Absence of nephrin → congenital nephrotic syndrome of the Finnish type (severe proteinuria).
- **Mesangium & JGA**: contractile **mesangial cells** secrete extracellular matrix and are continuous with arteriolar smooth muscle; **extraglomerular mesangial cells** are part of the **juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)**, which also includes the **macula densa** (specialized, closely packed, large-nucleus epithelial cells at the TAL→distal-tubule transition contacting the cell's own glomerulus) and the **granular (juxtaglomerular/epithelioid) cells** in the afferent arteriole wall that synthesize, store, and release **renin**.
- **Tubule segments** (Table 33-1): PCT → PST (ultrastructurally S1, S2, S3) → tDLH → tALH → TAL (ends at macula densa) → DCT → CNT → ICT → CCT → OMCD → IMCD → duct of Bellini (papillary collecting duct). The **proximal tubule** has an amplified apical **brush border** and interdigitated, mitochondria-rich basolateral membranes; complexity and reabsorptive rate decline S1→S3. The **thin limbs** are simple (few mitochondria, little amplification). The **TAL** has tall interdigitations and many mitochondria — it makes the medullary interstitium hyperosmotic. The **CNT** has CNT cells (release renal kallikrein) and intercalated cells. **Intercalated cells** (lack a central cilium): A/α-type secrete H⁺ and reabsorb K⁺; B/β-type secrete HCO₃⁻. **Principal cells** reabsorb Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and secrete K⁺.
- **Epithelial tightness** increases proximal → distal: the **leaky** proximal tubule has shallow junctions with few membrane strands (low resistance, high permeability); the **tight** collecting tubule has deep multi-strand tight junctions (high resistance, low permeability). **Gap junctions** electrically couple proximal-tubule cells but not the heterogeneous CNT/collecting cells.

## Section 2 — Main elements of renal function (pp. 730–731)

### Subsection headings
- **The nephron forms an ultrafiltrate of the blood plasma and then selectively reabsorbs the tubule fluid or secretes solutes into it** (pp. 729–730)
- **Sympathetic nerve fibers to the kidney regulate renal blood flow, glomerular filtration, and tubule reabsorption** (p. 730)
- **The kidneys, as endocrine organs, produce renin, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, erythropoietin, prostaglandins, and bradykinin** (pp. 730–731)

### Core claims
- **The four basic processes**: **filtration** (Starling forces drive an ultrafiltrate from glomerular capillary into Bowman's space) → **reabsorption** (tubules recover most filtered water and solutes) → **secretion** (tubules add solutes to the lumen) → **excretion** (what leaves in urine). If filtrate were not recovered, the kidney would excrete the entire plasma volume in **under half an hour**.
- **Segmental jobs**: the **proximal tubule** reabsorbs the bulk of filtered fluid — NaCl, NaHCO₃, glucose, amino acids, divalent ions (Ca²⁺, HPO₄²⁻, SO₄²⁻), and water — and secretes NH₄⁺ and endogenous/exogenous solutes. The **loop of Henle** (tDLH, tALH, TAL) pumps NaCl into the medullary interstitium with little water flow, making it hypertonic. The **medullary collecting duct** exploits that hypertonicity, permitting (or not) osmotic water exit so urine osmolality can far exceed plasma. **TAL cells secrete Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein (uromodulin)** — 30–50 mg/day in urine, the matrix of urinary casts, with roles in defense against UTIs and inhibition of stone formation.
- **Innervation** is **entirely sympathetic** (no parasympathetic). Fibers from the celiac plexus follow the vessels; varicosities release **norepinephrine and dopamine** near vascular smooth muscle and proximal tubules. Three effects: (1) **vasoconstriction**; (2) strong **stimulation of proximal Na⁺ reabsorption**; (3) **stimulation of renin secretion** from JGA granular cells. Afferent (sensory) fibers carry **baroreceptor** (perfusion-pressure) and **chemoreceptor** (K⁺/H⁺, ischemia) signals.
- **Endocrine roles**: granular cells make **renin**; proximal-tubule cells convert 25-hydroxyvitamin D to active **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D** (regulating Ca²⁺/phosphorus on gut, kidney, bone); cortical/outer-medullary fibroblast-like cells secrete **erythropoietin (EPO)** in response to low local PO₂ (deficiency in chronic renal failure → anemia, treatable with recombinant EPO); the kidney releases vasodilatory **prostaglandins** and **kinins (bradykinin)** that protect flow when perfusion is compromised.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The main function of renal tubules is to recover most of the fluid and solutes filtered at the glomerulus. If the fluid were not recovered, the kidney would excrete the volume of the entire blood plasma in less than half an hour." (p. 729)
- > "The autonomic innervation to the kidneys is entirely sympathetic; the kidneys lack parasympathetic nerve fibers." (p. 730)
- > "Fibroblast-like cells in the interstitium of the cortex and outer medulla secrete erythropoietin (EPO) in response to a fall in the local tissue Po₂." (p. 731)
- > "TAL cells secrete the Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein (THP), also known as uromodulin." (p. 729)

## Section 3 — Measuring renal clearance and transport (pp. 731–735)

### Subsection headings
- **The clearance of a solute is the virtual volume of plasma that would be totally cleared of a solute in a given time** (pp. 731–732)
- **A solute's urinary excretion is the algebraic sum of its filtered load, reabsorption by tubules, and secretion by tubules** (pp. 732–733)
- **Microscopic techniques make it possible to measure single-nephron rates of filtration, absorption, and secretion** (pp. 733–735)

### Core claims
- **Clearance concept**: the **clearance of a solute** is the virtual volume of plasma that, per unit time, supplies exactly the amount of solute appearing in urine. Worked example: 700 mL/min plasma flow carries ~100 mmol Na⁺, of which the kidney excretes only ~0.14 mmol — which could have come from just **1 mL of plasma**, so Na⁺ clearance = 1 mL/min even though 700 mL flowed through.
- **Three measurements suffice** to compute clearance of X: urine concentration **U_X**, urine flow **V̇**, and plasma concentration **P_X**.
- **GFR via inulin**: a solute that is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed, secreted, synthesized, degraded, nor accumulated has clearance = GFR. **Inulin** qualifies, so **C_in = GFR**.
- **RPF via PAH**: a solute that is essentially completely cleared in one pass (P in renal vein ≈ 0) has clearance = RPF. **Para-aminohippurate (PAH)** approximately qualifies, so **C_PAH ≈ RPF**. Clearance ranges from 0 (e.g., glucose, fully reabsorbed) to ~700 mL/min (total RPF, e.g., PAH).
- **Excretion = filtration − reabsorption + secretion** (the mass-balance core). For inulin, no reabsorption/secretion. For most solutes one or the other dominates; K⁺, uric acid, and urea undergo **both** reabsorption and secretion, so clearance alone cannot resolve their handling.
- **Caveats**: (1) the filtered load GFR·P_X assumes P_X is the *freely filterable* concentration — protein-bound solutes (Ca²⁺, phosphate, Mg²⁺, PAH) need correction; (2) mass balance requires the kidney not synthesize/degrade/accumulate the solute (e.g., NH₄⁺ is synthesized; glutamine/glutamate are degraded).
- **Fractional excretion (FE)** = (amount excreted)/(filtered load) = C_X/GFR = C_X/C_in. The clearance ratio.
- **Single-nephron methods** (Fig. 33-9): free-flow micropuncture, stopped-flow microperfusion, continuous microperfusion, and isolated perfused tubule. From tubule-fluid concentration TF_X, collection rate, and plasma P_X one computes single-nephron GFR (**SNGFR**) and fractional water/solute handling. In the rat example (Fig. 33-10) SNGFR = (3 mg/mL × 10 nL/min)/(1 mg/mL) = **30 nL/min**; the proximal tubule reabsorbed two-thirds of filtered water, leaving inulin concentrated 3× (fraction remaining ≈ 0.33).
- **Secretion vs. reabsorption diagnostic**: comparing the tubule-fluid/plasma ratio of X against that of inulin, (TF_X/P_X)/(TF_in/P_in) > 1 indicates **secretion**, < 1 indicates **reabsorption**. A rising luminal concentration alone does not prove secretion — water reabsorption alone would also raise it.

### Equations

- **Mass balance (input = output)** — Eq. 33-1:

  $$P_{X_a}\cdot RPF_a = P_{X_v}\cdot RPF_v + U_X\cdot \dot V$$

  with $P_{X_a}, P_{X_v}$ the renal-artery and renal-vein plasma concentrations of X, $RPF_a, RPF_v$ the arterial/venous renal plasma flows, $U_X$ the urine concentration, and $\dot V$ the urine flow rate. $U_X\cdot\dot V$ is the **urinary excretion rate**.

- **Classic clearance equation** — Eq. 33-3 (setting virtual venous output = 0):

  $$C_X = \frac{U_X\cdot \dot V}{P_X}$$

  the virtual volume of plasma totally cleared of X per unit time (Table 33-2A).

- **PAH clearance ≈ RPF** — Eq. (Table 33-2B):

  $$(C_{PAH})\approx RPF = \frac{U_{PAH}\cdot \dot V}{P_{PAH}}$$

- **Inulin clearance = GFR** — Eqs. 33-4/33-5 and Table 33-2C. Because freely filtered, non-transported X satisfies $P_X\cdot GFR = U_X\cdot\dot V$:

  $$GFR = \frac{U_X\cdot \dot V}{P_X} = C_{in} = \frac{U_{in}\cdot \dot V}{P_{in}}$$

- **Excretion as algebraic sum** — Eq. 33-6:

  $$E_X = F_X - R_X + S_X$$

  excreted = filtered (filtered load $F_X = GFR\cdot P_X$) − reabsorbed + secreted.

- **Reabsorption-only** — Eq. 33-7: $R_X = GFR\cdot P_X - U_X\cdot\dot V$.
- **Secretion-only** — Eq. 33-8: $S_X = U_X\cdot\dot V - GFR\cdot P_X$.

- **Fractional excretion** — Eqs. 33-9 to 33-11:

  $$FE_X = \frac{U_X\cdot\dot V}{P_X\cdot GFR} = \frac{C_X}{GFR} = \frac{C_X}{C_{in}}$$

- **Single-nephron clearance / SNGFR** — Eqs. 33-12, 33-13:

  $$C_X = \frac{TF_X\cdot (\text{volume collection rate})}{P_X}, \qquad SNGFR = \frac{TF_{in}\cdot(\text{volume collection rate})}{P_{in}}$$

- **Fraction of filtered water reabsorbed** — Eq. 33-17:

  $$\text{Fraction reabsorbed} = 1 - \frac{P_{in}}{TF_{in}}$$

- **Fractional solute delivery / reabsorption** — Eqs. 33-20, 33-21:

  $$\text{Fractional solute delivery} = \frac{TF_X/P_X}{TF_{in}/P_{in}}, \qquad \text{Fractional reabsorption} = 1 - \frac{TF_X/P_X}{TF_{in}/P_{in}}$$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The clearance of a solute is defined as the virtual volume of blood plasma (per unit time) needed to supply the amount of solute that appears in the urine." (p. 731)
- > "Renal clearance methods are based on the principle of mass balance and the special anatomy of the kidney." (p. 731)
- > "This is the classic clearance equation that describes the virtual volume of plasma that would be totally cleared of a solute in a given time." (p. 731)
- > "Because para-aminohippurate (PAH) is just such a special solute, its clearance is a good estimate of RPF." (p. 732)
- > "Renal excretion rate (E_X) depends on three factors: the rate of filtration of X … the rate of reabsorption of X by the tubules … the rate of secretion of X by the tubules." (p. 732)
- > "Complex combinations of reabsorption and secretion occur with K⁺, uric acid, and urea." (p. 733)

## Section 4 — The ureters and bladder (pp. 735–738)

### Subsection headings
- **The ureters propel urine from the renal pelvis to the bladder by peristaltic waves conducted along a syncytium of smooth-muscle cells** (pp. 735–736)
- **Sympathetic, parasympathetic, and somatic fibers innervate the urinary bladder and its sphincters** (pp. 736–737)
- **Bladder filling activates stretch receptors, initiating the micturition reflex, a spinal reflex under control of higher central nervous system centers** (pp. 738)

### Core claims
- **No further modification of urine**: unlike the GI tract, the urinary "plumbing" (renal pelvis, ureters, bladder, urethra) does **not** substantially change urine volume or composition once it leaves the collecting duct.
- **Ureters** are retroperitoneal conduits that enter the bladder obliquely at the **ureterovesical junction**; the oblique passage plus a mucosal flap-valve prevents reflux during bladder contraction. The two ureteral orifices and the urethral orifice form the **bladder trigone**. The ureter wall is **transitional epithelium** over connective tissue, inner longitudinal + outer circular smooth muscle.
- **Ureteral peristalsis**: ureteral smooth muscle is a **unitary (syncytial)** type; **gap junctions** conduct excitation at 2–6 cm/s; stretch or depolarization triggers **plateau-type action potentials** (Ca²⁺ channels mainly responsible; Na⁺ channels speed the upstroke). Pacemakers in the proximal renal pelvis drive **2–6 peristaltic waves/min**. Intraureteral pressure 0–5 cm H₂O at baseline rises to 20–80 cm H₂O during waves. Contraction uses the standard smooth-muscle Ca²⁺-calmodulin→MLCK pathway; PKA phosphorylates MLCK to relax (the cAMP relaxing effect).
- **Obstruction (e.g., kidney stone)**: ureter dilates and baseline pressure climbs to **70–80 cm H₂O over 1–3 h**; pressure transmits retrograde to nephrons, producing a **stopped-flow** state in which filtration nearly halts. **Hydronephrosis** (dilated pelvis/calyces) develops over hours–days; **renal colic** is severe pain from distention; unrelieved obstruction can cause renal failure. Even with anuria, filtration continues at a markedly reduced rate (balance of filtration and tubular reabsorption).
- **Autonomic modulation of ureters**: sympathetic NE acts via excitatory α- and inhibitory β-adrenergic receptors; parasympathetic ACh enhances contractility via muscarinic receptors. Some afferent fibers are pain fibers — renal colic is among the most severe pains in clinical practice.
- **Bladder structure**: body (collects urine) + neck (funnels to urethra), lined by **transitional epithelium**; three layers of smooth muscle form the **detrusor muscle**. The **internal sphincter** is detrusor smooth muscle + elastic tissue at the bladder neck/posterior urethra; the **external sphincter** is voluntary, mainly slow-twitch striated muscle. Human bladder smooth muscle appears to **lack gap junctions** → probably **multiunit** with ~1:1 nerve-to-muscle ratio.
- **Bladder innervation** (Fig. 33-12; Table 33-3): **sympathetic** from T10–L2 (intermediolateral column) → lumbar splanchnic → superior hypogastric plexus → hypogastric nerves → inferior hypogastric/pelvic plexus → bladder/internal sphincter. **Parasympathetic** from S2–S4 → pelvic splanchnic nerve → postganglionic neurons in bladder wall (→ detrusor). **Somatic** from S2–S4 motor neurons → **pudendal nerve** → voluntary external sphincter.
- **Bladder tone & cystometrogram** (Fig. 33-13): pressure rises moderately 0→~50 mL, then stays nearly flat to ~300 mL (high compliance from smooth-muscle relaxation), then climbs steeply >400 mL. Tone up to the micturition threshold is independent of extrinsic innervation.
- **Micturition reflex**: a spinal reflex coordinated by the **pontine micturition center (PMC)** and normally inhibited by cortical/suprapontine centers. Storage: stretch receptors signal via pelvic splanchnic afferents; urge first sensed at **~150 mL**, fullness at **400–500 mL**; a learned reflex inhibits sacral parasympathetic preganglionics and the external sphincter is held closed. Voiding: voluntary relaxation of external then internal sphincter; urine entering the posterior urethra signals the cortex; the PMC stops inhibiting parasympathetic preganglionics → detrusor contracts and expels urine; contraction recruits further stretch-receptor firing in a **self-regenerating** loop, with abdominal-muscle contraction aiding emptying. Higher centers set the voiding threshold.
- **Sterility**: continuous urine flow, sphincter function, and near-complete emptying keep the normal urinary system sterile.

## Glossary

- **Nephron** — functional unit; ~1 million/kidney; glomerulus + tubule.
- **Cortex / medulla** — granular outer (glomeruli + convoluted tubules) vs. striated inner (pyramids, loops, vasa recta).
- **Renal pyramid / papilla / calyx** — medullary cone draining urine into minor → major calyx → renal pelvis.
- **Afferent / efferent arteriole** — the two resistance vessels flanking the glomerular capillary.
- **Peritubular capillaries / vasa recta** — low-pressure reabsorptive networks of cortex / medulla.
- **Renal corpuscle** — glomerulus + Bowman's capsule + Bowman's space.
- **Glomerular filtration barrier** — glycocalyx, fenestrated endothelium, basement membrane, podocyte slit diaphragm.
- **Podocyte / slit diaphragm / nephrin / podocin** — visceral epithelial cells and the molecular filter; mutations → nephrotic syndrome.
- **Mesangial cells** — contractile matrix-secreting cells; extraglomerular ones join the JGA.
- **Juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)** — macula densa + granular (renin) cells + extraglomerular mesangium.
- **Tubule segments** — PCT, PST (S1–S3), tDLH, tALH, TAL, DCT, CNT, ICT, CCT, OMCD, IMCD, duct of Bellini.
- **Principal vs. intercalated cells** — Na⁺ reabsorb/K⁺ secrete vs. H⁺/HCO₃⁻ and K⁺ handling.
- **Tight vs. leaky epithelium** — collecting duct (tight, multi-strand junctions) vs. proximal tubule (leaky).
- **Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein (uromodulin)** — TAL secretory protein; matrix of urinary casts.
- **Four processes** — filtration, reabsorption, secretion, excretion.
- **Clearance (C_X)** — virtual plasma volume cleared of X per unit time = U_X·V̇/P_X.
- **GFR = inulin clearance; RPF ≈ PAH clearance.**
- **Filtered load (F_X = GFR·P_X); fractional excretion (FE_X = C_X/C_in).**
- **SNGFR** — single-nephron GFR by micropuncture.
- **Micturition reflex / PMC** — spinal voiding reflex coordinated by the pontine micturition center.
- **Detrusor / internal sphincter / external sphincter** — bladder muscle and its two sphincters.
- **Hydronephrosis / renal colic** — consequences of ureteral obstruction.
