# Chapter 23 — Regulation of Arterial Pressure and Cardiac Output

> Section IV · The Cardiovascular System · pp. 533–555 · Author: Emile L. Boulpaep
> This chapter takes the **single-chamber pump** of Ch 22 (its preload/afterload/contractility triad, Fig. 22-14) and embeds it in the closed loop of the circulation. It develops three nested control time scales — **short-term** neural reflexes (baroreceptor and chemoreceptor; seconds to minutes), **intermediate-term** humoral signals (RAAS, AVP, ANP, endothelins; minutes to hours), and **long-term** renal control of effective circulating volume (hours to days) — and it introduces the chapter's central diagram, the **Guyton intersection** of the **cardiac function curve** (Starling-derived) with the **vascular function curve** (venous return as a function of RAP). The operating point of the whole circulation is the unique RAP at which CO equals VR; perturbations slide along the curves, and persistent changes require *shifting* one or both curves.

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Short-term regulation of arterial pressure** (pp. 533–545) — the negative-feedback architecture (detector, afferent, coordinating center, efferent, effector); high-pressure baroreceptors of the carotid sinus and aortic arch (TRPC stretch transducers; dynamic and static pressure-spike-rate curves; differing properties of carotid vs. aortic receptors); medullary coordinating center (NTS → CVLM / nucleus ambiguus / dorsal motor n. vagus → RVLM C1 → IML preganglionic sympathetic + vagal preganglionic parasympathetic); sympathetic and parasympathetic efferents to heart, vessels, adrenal medulla; adrenergic / cholinergic / NANC receptor pharmacology in heart and vessels; chemoreceptor backup (carotid + aortic bodies for hypoxia / hypercapnia / acidosis; central chemoreceptors for brain pH); the integrated chemoreceptor response (intrinsic bradycardia overridden by lung-stretch tachycardia).
2. **Regulation of cardiac output** (pp. 545–549) — intrinsic and extrinsic determinants of HR and SV (EDV from filling pressure / time / compliance; ESV from preload, afterload, HR, contractility); low-pressure (atrial / pulmonary / ventricular) baroreceptors and their **opposite** response polarity to the high-pressure set (stretch → tachycardia + renal vasodilation + diuresis + ↓AVP + ↑ANP); the **Bainbridge reflex** vs. the baroreceptor reflex; the biphasic dependence of HR and SV — but monotonic dependence of CO — on effective circulating volume (Fig. 23-8).
3. **Matching of venous return and cardiac output** (pp. 549–551) — the **vascular function curve** (RAP on the x-axis, VR on the y-axis; intercept = **mean systemic filling pressure**, MSFP ≈ 7 mm Hg; plateau at negative RAP from venous collapse); shifts from blood-volume change (parallel x-shift) vs. arteriolar tone change (slope change); the **cardiac function curve** (Starling-law CO vs. RAP); their intersection as the **unique operating point** (Fig. 23-11A); transient mismatch resolution (B → C → A); persistent shifts via transfusion (curve right-shifts; Fig. 23-11B) or inotropy (cardiac curve up-shifts; Fig. 23-11C).
4. **Intermediate- and long-term control** (pp. 551–555) — endocrine and paracrine vasoactive compounds (Table 23-3): biogenic amines (epinephrine, serotonin, histamine); peptides (ANG II, AVP, endothelins, ANP, kinins); arachidonic-acid derivatives (PGI₂, PGE₂, TXA₂); NO; the **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system** (RAAS) and the Goldblatt model of hypertension (Box 23-1); renal control of ECF volume as the dominant long-term regulator of MAP, integrated by four parallel pathways (RAAS, ANS, AVP, ANP).

## Section 1 — Short-term regulation of arterial pressure (pp. 533–545)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Systemic mean arterial blood pressure is the principal variable that the cardiovascular system controls** (pp. 533–534)
- **Neural reflexes mediate the short-term regulation of mean arterial blood pressure** (p. 534)
- **High-pressure baroreceptors at the carotid sinus and aortic arch are stretch receptors that sense changes in arterial pressure** (pp. 534–536)
- **Increased arterial pressure raises the firing rate of afferent baroreceptor nerves** (pp. 536–537)
- **The medulla coordinates afferent baroreceptor signals** (p. 537)
- **The efferent pathways of the baroreceptor response include both sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions of the autonomic nervous system** (pp. 537–539)
- **The principal effectors in the neural control of arterial pressure are the heart, the arteries, the veins, and the adrenal medulla** (pp. 539–541)
- **The unique combination of agonists and receptors determines the end response in cardiac and vascular effector cells** (pp. 541–543)
- **The medullary cardiovascular center tonically maintains blood pressure and is under the control of higher brain centers** (pp. 543–544)
- **Secondary neural regulation of arterial blood pressure depends on chemoreceptors** (pp. 544–545)

### Core claims

#### Why MAP is the controlled variable (the water-tower analogy, pp. 533–534)
- The body could in principle deliver fixed CO to each organ, but instead it controls a single shared **driving pressure head** (MAP) and lets each organ pull its own demand by adjusting local arteriolar resistance (Ch 20).
- Anatomic justification: a branched arterial tree delivers approximately **mean aortic pressure to every organ**, so a constant MAP guarantees both adequate perfusion (e.g., glomerular filtration, intraocular flow) and **independence of beds** (one organ's flow change does not steal flow from another, as long as the heart can hold MAP).
- The relation underpinning everything that follows is the cardiovascular Ohm's law (Ch 17, Eq. 17-7):

$$\text{MAP} = \text{CO} \times \text{TPR}$$

#### Five-element negative-feedback architecture (p. 534)
Every short-term loop has: **(1) detector** (baroreceptor / chemoreceptor / stretch receptor) → **(2) afferent neural pathway** → **(3) coordinating center** (medullary CV center) → **(4) efferent pathway** (sympathetic and parasympathetic) → **(5) effectors** (SA-node pacemaker, ventricular myocytes, VSMCs, adrenal medulla). The primary sensors are **baroreceptors** (stretch / mechanoreceptors); the secondary sensors are **chemoreceptors** (P_O₂, P_CO₂, pH).

#### High-pressure baroreceptors (pp. 534–537; Figs. 23-1, 23-2, 23-3)
- **Sites:** **carotid sinus** (a thin-walled, elastic, distensible dilation of the internal carotid artery just above the common-carotid bifurcation) and **aortic arch** (a highly compliant segment that distends with each LV ejection). Figure 23-2 distinguishes the carotid **sinus** (baroreceptor, on the artery) from the carotid **body** (chemoreceptor, lying beside it).
- **Receptor cell:** branched / varicose terminals of myelinated and unmyelinated sensory nerve fibers, intermeshed within elastic layers. Terminals express several nonselective cation channels — **TRPC1, TRPC3, TRPC4, TRPC5**. TRPC1 is stretch-sensitive.
- **Stretch vs. pressure:** the receptors transduce **deformation of the vessel wall**, not pressure per se. The classical "plaster cast" experiment: encasing the arterial wall in plaster prevents distention and abolishes the response to a transmural pressure rise; removing the cast restores it.
- **Receptor potential (Fig. 23-3A):** a step pressure increase produces a **biphasic depolarization** — a large **dynamic** initial deflection followed by a smaller **static** steady depolarization. Action-potential spike frequency tracks the depolarization: large transient burst at the dynamic phase, lower steady rate at the static phase.
- **Encoding:** because both dynamic and static components matter, the baroreceptor encodes **both pulse pressure (waveform information) and mean pressure (amplitude)** as a frequency-modulated signal.
- **Static pressure-activity curve (Fig. 23-3B):** sigmoidal. Threshold ≈ 40–60 mm Hg; saturation ≈ 200 mm Hg.
- **Pulsatile vs nonpulsatile pressure-activity curve:** for the **same mean pressure**, pulsatile input elicits a **higher mean discharge frequency** than nonpulsatile input — the reason peripheral baroreceptors detect the difference between a normal pulsatile arterial trace and a non-pulsatile machine-perfused tree.
- **Population recruitment:** different single units have different individual thresholds, so the global afferent volley is the sum of (i) increased firing of active units plus (ii) progressive recruitment of new units, up to ~200 mm Hg.
- **Carotid sinus vs. aortic arch — five differences (p. 536):**
  1. Higher static threshold (~110 mm Hg vs. ~50 mm Hg).
  2. Higher dynamic threshold likewise.
  3. Aortic baroreceptor continues to respond beyond the carotid saturation pressure.
  4. Less sensitive to rate of pressure change.
  5. Responds **less** effectively to pressure decreases than to increases over the same range.
  In a given individual, a change at the **carotid sinus has a greater effect on systemic arterial pressure than the same change at the aortic arch.**

#### Afferent pathways and central processing (pp. 537–538; Fig. 23-4)
- **Carotid sinus → sinus nerve (nerve of Hering) → glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) → petrosal (inferior) ganglion → NTS**.
- **Aortic arch → depressor branch of vagus (CN X) → superior laryngeal nerves → nodose (inferior) ganglion → NTS**.
- **NTS** (nucleus tractus solitarii) is the obligatory first-relay nucleus in the dorsal medulla. The afferent transmitter onto NTS neurons is **glutamate** (binding GluA2 AMPA subunits); some NTS neurons also express **P2X purinoceptors** (ATP).
- Downstream of NTS, the **medullary cardiovascular center** has two functional subdivisions:
  - **Vasomotor area** in the **rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM)** containing the **C1** (adrenergic) and A1 noradrenergic neurons. C1 neurons are **tonically active and tonically vasoconstrictive** (their bursting is locked to the cardiac cycle); NTS interneurons **inhibit** C1, so an arterial-pressure rise → baroreceptor firing → NTS inhibition of C1 → vasodilation. The clinical correlate: the antihypertensive **clonidine** acts on imidazole receptors on C1 neurons.
  - **Cardioinhibitory area** comprising the **nucleus ambiguus** and the **dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus**. Excitatory NTS interneurons drive these vagal preganglionic neurons → bradycardia.
- A **cardioacceleratory** projection from NTS to a dorsal-medullary area increases HR and contractility via descending sympathetic excitation.

#### Efferent pathways: sympathetic side (pp. 537–539)
- **Bulbospinal neurons** in the vasomotor and cardioacceleratory areas descend to the **intermediolateral (IML) column** of T1–L3, where they synapse onto **preganglionic sympathetic neurons** (ACh on Nₙ nicotinic receptors). Most useful release is **glutamatergic** (NMDA and non-NMDA), with adrenergic (from C1), peptidergic (NPY) co-transmission.
- **Postganglionic** sympathetic neurons in **paravertebral chain ganglia** + **prevertebral ganglia** release **norepinephrine** to VSMCs and to the SA node, atria, ventricles via the **cardiac nerves** (middle cervical, stellate, upper thoracic ganglia).
- **Right cardiac nerve → SA node → HR.** **Left cardiac nerve → contractility.**
- Some preganglionic splanchnic fibers bypass ganglia and directly innervate **chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla** — the adrenal medulla is functionally a sympathetic ganglion, releasing **epinephrine (predominant) + norepinephrine** into the bloodstream as **global circulating effectors**.
- **Severing the cord above T1** abolishes sympathetic outflow and produces profound hypotension; severing below L3 has no such effect.

#### Efferent pathways: parasympathetic side (p. 538)
- Vagal preganglionic neurons from the **nucleus ambiguus** and **dorsal motor n. vagus** project through CN X to **small ganglia in the atrial walls** → short postganglionic fibers releasing **ACh** to **SA node, atria, and (to a lesser extent) ventricles**.
- The vagus normally exerts **intense tonic bradycardic activity**; cutting the vagus or giving **atropine** raises HR.
- **Right vagus → SA node** dominance. **Left vagus → AV node** dominance.

#### Effector pharmacology (pp. 539–543; Tables 23-1, 23-2)
- **Sympathetic input to the heart.** NE → β₁ → Gαs → AC → cAMP → PKA. In the **pacemaker**, PKA augments Iₕ (HCN, "funny current") and Iᶜₐ (T- and L-type) → steeper phase 4 → ↑HR. In **myocytes**, PKA phosphorylates Cav1.2 (more Ca²⁺ entry), RYR2 / dissociates FKBP12.6 / calstabin 2 (more SR release), PLN (faster SERCA2a uptake → larger store, faster relaxation), TnI (faster Ca-TnC dissociation → faster relaxation). The α subunit of Gs also opens Cav1.2 directly. Net: positive inotropy + positive lusitropy + briefer contraction (Table 23-2).
- **Parasympathetic input to the heart.** ACh → M₂ → Gαi₀. Three SA-node mechanisms: (1) βγ subunits directly open **GIRK1 (Kir3.1)** inward-rectifier K⁺ channels → maximum diastolic potential more negative; (2) ↓Iₕ; (3) ↓Iᶜₐ → less-steep phase 4 + more positive threshold. Net: ↓HR. In myocytes, M₂ also ↓cAMP and M₃ generates NO/cGMP → small negative inotropic effect.
- **Sympathetic input to vessels (vasoconstriction).** NE → α₁ on VSMCs → Gq → PLC → IP₃ → [Ca²⁺]ᵢ → contraction. Sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers are most abundant in **kidney and skin**, sparse in coronary and cerebral beds, **absent in placenta**.
- **Parasympathetic input to vessels (vasodilation).** Limited distribution — **salivary glands, some GI glands, erectile tissue of external genitalia**. ACh acts indirectly: (a) at endothelial M₃ → IP₃/Ca²⁺ → eNOS → NO → VSMC sGC → cGMP → relaxation; (b) co-released NO, VIP, CGRP; (c) presynaptic M₂ on noradrenergic varicosities → ↓NE release → disinhibition.
- **Sympathetic cholinergic vasodilator system in skeletal muscle (nonprimates).** Originates in cerebral cortex → hypothalamus / mesencephalon → bypasses the medullary CV center → IML → postganglionic sympathetic fibers release **ACh** at muscarinic receptors on VSMCs → rapid vasodilation. Activated during fight-or-flight + anticipatory exercise; accompanied by widespread α₁ vasoconstriction in non-muscle beds and cardiac β₁ stimulation.
- **Adrenal medulla.** Epinephrine in the circulation hits **β₂ on most VSMCs** (vasodilation in muscle, coronary, hepatic beds) but **α₁ where β₂ is sparse** (skin → vasoconstriction even with epinephrine). In the heart, epinephrine acts on β₁ → ↑HR + ↑inotropy. β₂ has higher affinity for epinephrine than for NE — epinephrine is thus the system's β₂ "tool."
- **Receptor-subtype rule of thumb for the bedside:** *in the vasculature, only α₁ and β₂ matter clinically.*

#### Higher-center modulation (pp. 543–544)
- **Hypothalamus** integrates many CV responses: the **dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus** drives RVLM during exercise and acute stress; the **paraventricular nucleus** synthesizes AVP for posterior pituitary release.
- **Cerebral cortex** can produce vasovagal hypotension and syncope under emotional stress; reward conditioning can train HR up or down.
- **Spinal-segmental reflexes** (e.g., skin blanching in response to pain or inflammation) become **most powerful after cord transection**, when the descending medullary input is gone.

#### Secondary regulation — chemoreceptors (pp. 544–545; Fig. 23-6)
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors.** **Carotid bodies** (glomus caroticum) lie between the external and internal carotid arteries; tiny (~1 mm³) but with extraordinary blood flow per unit mass and minute arteriovenous differences for P_O₂, P_CO₂, pH — ideal arterial-blood samplers. **Glomus cells** synapse with afferents in **CN IX**. **Aortic bodies** lie on the underside of the aortic arch and in the angle between right subclavian + carotid arteries; afferents in **CN X**. Primary stimulus is **low P_O₂**; firing also rises with ↑P_CO₂ and ↓pH.
- **Central chemoreceptors** in the medulla sense **low brain ECF pH** (which reflects **high arterial P_CO₂**).
- **Intrinsic CV response** to peripheral-chemoreceptor stimulation (Fig. 23-6A): **vasoconstriction + bradycardia** (the bradycardia is observable only with **fixed or prevented ventilation**, e.g., breath-holding).
- **Integrated response** in spontaneously breathing animals (Fig. 23-6B): hypoxia simultaneously drives the **medullary respiratory centers** → ↑ventilation. Lung-stretch receptors then **inhibit the cardioinhibitory center → tachycardia**, and the rise in alveolar P_O₂ + fall in brain P_CO₂ further inhibit the cardioinhibitory center. Net: **tachycardia overrides intrinsic bradycardia**.
- **Cooperation between peripheral and central chemoreceptors:** low arterial P_O₂ acts on the peripheral side and high arterial P_CO₂ on the central side; both **disinhibit the vasomotor area** and reinforce vasoconstriction.

### Equations

- **Cardiovascular Ohm's law (recalled from Ch 17, Eq. 17-7):**

$$\text{MAP} = \text{CO} \times \text{TPR}$$

- **Cardiac output (Ch 17, Eq. 17-6; Ch 22):**

$$\text{CO} = \text{HR} \times \text{SV}, \qquad \text{SV} = \text{EDV} - \text{ESV}$$

- **Sigmoidal baroreceptor static pressure-activity curve (conceptual fit):**

$$f(P) = f_{\max} \cdot \frac{(P - P_0)^n}{K^n + (P - P_0)^n}, \quad P_0 \approx 40\text{–}60 \text{ mm Hg}, \quad f \to f_{\max} \text{ at } P \approx 200 \text{ mm Hg}$$

### Box 23-1 — Hypertension (p. 554; placed in this section's intellectual flow even though printed later)
- Hypertension is found in ~20% of the adult population. Long-term consequences: arteriosclerosis (endothelial-cell damage drives proliferative responses), coronary disease, MI, heart failure, stroke, renal failure.
- The dysfunction is generally in **long-term** (not short-term) regulatory mechanisms; chronically hypertensive patients may have **diminished arterial baroreceptor sensitivity** (a resetting of the loop).
- **Primary (essential) hypertension** — no identifiable single cause. **Secondary hypertension** — identifiable: most commonly **renal artery stenosis** (atherosclerotic plaque, fibromuscular dysplasia in young women, space-occupying lesions). The classic experimental model is **Goldblatt's "one-clip two-kidney"**: clipping one renal artery → ↓renal perfusion pressure → renin release → ANG II rises → systemic vasoconstriction + Na⁺ retention → hypertension. Surgical relief of the stenosis can resolve the hypertension.
- Other causes of secondary hypertension: cumulative small-artery obstruction (chronic renal parenchymal disease); coarctation of the aorta; chronic volume overload (primary hyperaldosteronism — adenoma or bilateral hyperplasia; pheochromocytoma; mendelian forms such as **Liddle disease**, pseudohypoaldosteronism type 2).

## Section 2 — Regulation of cardiac output (pp. 545–549)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Mechanisms intrinsic to the heart modulate both heart rate and stroke volume** (pp. 545–546)
- **Mechanisms extrinsic to the heart also modulate heart rate and stroke volume** (pp. 546–547)
- **Low-pressure baroreceptors in the atria respond to increased "fullness" of the vascular system, triggering tachycardia, renal vasodilation, and diuresis** (pp. 547–548)
- **Cardiac output is roughly proportional to effective circulating blood volume** (pp. 548–549)

### Core claims

#### Intrinsic determinants of HR (p. 545)
- HR is set by the SA-node AP shape: **maximum diastolic potential, slope of phase 4 (Iₕ + Iᶜₐ + I_K), and threshold potential**.
- **Intrinsic modifiers**: [K⁺]₀ and [Ca²⁺]₀ shift these pacemaker currents but are not part of cardiovascular feedback loops per se. Read alongside Ch 21 Fig. 21-6.

#### Intrinsic determinants of SV (pp. 545–546)
**SV = EDV − ESV** (Eq. 22-2). The chapter unpacks each side.

**EDV depends on three things:**
1. **Filling pressure** — atrial pressure tracks venous return; ↑venous return → ↑atrial pressure → ↑EDV.
2. **Filling time** — at higher HR, **diastole shortens preferentially** (Ch 22, p. 507), so EDV falls.
3. **Ventricular compliance** — reciprocal of the slope of the diastole (EDPVR) curve of Fig. 22-12B. A more compliant ventricle reaches a higher EDV at the same filling pressure. Diastolic heart failure (HFpEF) is a steepening of this curve.

**ESV depends on four things (the now-familiar triad plus HR):**
1. **Preload (EDV) → Starling's law:** ↑EDV → ↑force → ↑SV → typically ↓ESV.
2. **Afterload (MAP for LV, mean PAP for RV):** ↑afterload impedes ejection → ↑ESV.
3. **HR — the Bowditch staircase (Ch 22, p. 528):** ↑HR → ↑Ca²⁺ entry per minute → ↑[Ca²⁺]ᵢ → ↑contractility → ↓ESV.
4. **Contractility itself:** positive inotropes → ↑[Ca²⁺]ᵢ → ↓ESV (Ch 22, Fig. 22-14B).

Because a single variable (HR) can affect EDV (by shortening filling time) and ESV (by Bowditch) in opposite directions, the net effect on SV is non-trivial and depends on operating point.

#### Extrinsic regulation of HR and SV via baroreceptor and chemoreceptor reflexes (pp. 546–547)
- Baroreceptors **do not sense CO**; they sense MAP. A perfectly compensated CO change (↑CO matched by proportional ↓TPR) is invisible to them.
- Chemoreceptor response (Fig. 23-6B integrated tachycardia) **is a useful feedback mechanism for CO**: a fall in CO lowers tissue P_O₂ and raises P_CO₂ and ↓pH → peripheral chemoreceptor fires → integrated tachycardia → restores CO. The detector senses **metabolic consequences** of low CO, not CO itself.
- A high P_CO₂ acidifies myocardial cells, which **right-shifts the [Ca²⁺]ᵢ-tension curve** (TnC affinity drops) → ↓myocardial force. The chemoreflex-driven tachycardia partially offsets this direct negative effect of acidosis on the heart.

#### Low-pressure (venous / atrial / ventricular) baroreceptors (pp. 547–548; Fig. 23-7)
- Located at strategic **low-pressure sites**: pulmonary artery, atrium-vena cava junction, atrial walls, ventricular walls.
- They sense "**fullness**" of the central circulation — effective circulating volume (Ch 40).
- **Atrial receptors (most studied):** bare endings of A and B vagal afferents.
  - **A fibers** fire in synchrony with **atrial systole** → monitor **HR**.
  - **B fibers** fire during ventricular systole and crescendo with atrial filling, peaking at the **v wave** of the jugular venous pulse (Ch 22, Fig. 22-7A) → monitor **rising atrial volume / CVP / venous return**.
- Afferents travel via vagus (CN X) → NTS, in parallel with the high-pressure pathway.
- Polarity is **opposite to the high-pressure receptors in HR / vascular tone outflows:**

| Receptor | Stretch → HR | Stretch → vascular tone |
|---|---|---|
| High-pressure (carotid sinus, aortic arch) | ↓ HR (bradycardia) | generalized vasodilation |
| Low-pressure atrial B-type | **↑ HR** (Bainbridge reflex) | renal vasodilation **only** (decreased renal sympathetic outflow) |

- The atrial-receptor outflow also has a **non-neural arm:** atrial stretch directly causes **atrial myocytes to release ANP**, a vasodilator with potent diuretic and natriuretic actions (Chs 40, 41).
- Additionally, atrial stretch projects via NTS → **PVN of hypothalamus** magnocellular neurons → posterior pituitary → **decreased AVP release** → water diuresis → ↓total body water (Ch 38).
- **Three parallel diuretic mechanisms of atrial stretch** (p. 548): (1) reflex tachycardia + ↓renal sympathetic tone → ↑renal blood flow; (2) ↓AVP; (3) ↑ANP.
- **Ventricular low-pressure receptors:** stretch → bradycardia + vasodilation (like the high-pressure carotid set). They contribute little to CO homeostasis but generate clinically important reflexes (Bezold-Jarisch-like patterns in inferior MI).

#### The Bainbridge reflex (p. 548; Fig. 23-8)
- Volume loading → ↑venous return → ↑atrial filling → ↑low-pressure B-fiber firing → mixed parasympathetic/sympathetic outflow to **SA node only** → **tachycardia** (negligible effect on contractility or SV).
- Bainbridge **saturates**; its effect is largest at low baseline HR.
- The Bainbridge reflex **counterbalances the baroreceptor reflex** on HR. **Around normal volume both reflexes are small** — HR is at its minimum (Fig. 23-8, orange curve U-shape).
- **At low blood volume**, the baroreceptor reflex dominates → HR rises (left ascending limb of the orange curve).
- **At high blood volume**, the Bainbridge reflex dominates → HR rises (right ascending limb of the orange curve).

#### Biphasic HR, biphasic SV, monotonic CO (p. 548; Fig. 23-8)
- **SV vs. volume (blue curve)** is biphasic with a maximum near normal. Below normal, Starling's law (which would predict ↓SV) is offset by baroreceptor-driven **↑sympathetic tone → ↑contractility**, **steepening** the Starling response; SV is held up. Above normal, **↓baroreceptor sympathetic tone flattens** the Starling response, and SV plateaus.
- **CO = HR × SV** integrates the two biphasic responses into a **monotonically rising red curve** vs. effective circulating volume — the chapter's key teaching plot: **CO rises smoothly with volume even though neither HR nor SV does so smoothly.**
- This monotonic dependence is the empirical statement that "**cardiac output is roughly proportional to effective circulating blood volume.**"

### Equations

- **Stroke volume identity** (Ch 22, Eq. 22-2):

$$\text{SV} = \text{EDV} - \text{ESV}$$

- **Cardiac output as a function of effective circulating volume (Fig. 23-8 schematic):**

$$\text{CO}(V_{\text{ecv}}) = \text{HR}(V_{\text{ecv}}) \times \text{SV}(V_{\text{ecv}})$$

where each factor is biphasic in $V_{\text{ecv}}$ but their product rises monotonically through the normal operating point.

- **Diastolic shortening dominance (Ch 22, p. 507 quote, applied here):** if cycle length $\tau = 60/\text{HR}$ partitions into systole $\tau_s$ + diastole $\tau_d$, then $\Delta \tau_d / \Delta \tau > \Delta \tau_s / \Delta \tau$ as HR rises — diastolic filling time falls faster than systolic time.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Because central venous pressure (CVP)—the pressure inside large systemic veins leading to the right heart—is the main determinant of right atrial filling, the B fibers also detect changes in CVP. By inference, the atrial B-type stretch receptors primarily monitor effective circulating volume and venous return." (pp. 547–548)
- > "Whereas increased stretch of the high-pressure receptors lowers heart rate, increased stretch of the atrial B-type receptors raises heart rate (the Bainbridge reflex)." (p. 548)
- > "Enhanced atrial filling with consequent stretching of the atrial mechanoreceptors promotes diuresis by at least three efferent mechanisms … (1) Tachycardia in combination with a reduced sympathetic vasoconstrictor output to the kidney increases renal blood flow. (2) Atrial baroreceptors cause decreased secretion of AVP. (3) The stretch of the atrial myocytes themselves enhances the release of ANP." (p. 548)
- > "By different mechanisms, volume loading and volume depletion both cause a graded increase in heart rate. In general, during volume loading, the Bainbridge reflex prevails, whereas during volume depletion, the high-pressure baroreceptor reflex dominates. Heart rate is at its minimum when effective circulating volume is normal." (p. 548)
- > "The dependence of cardiac output on effective circulating volume is the result of the complex interplay among three responses: (1) the Bainbridge reflex, (2) the baroreceptor reflex, and (3) Starling's law." (p. 549)

## Section 3 — Matching of venous return and cardiac output (pp. 549–551)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Increases in cardiac output cause right atrial pressure to fall** (pp. 549–550)
- **Changes in blood volume shift the vascular function curve to different RAPs, whereas changes in arteriolar tone alter the slope of the curve** (pp. 550–551)
- **Because vascular function and cardiac function depend on each other, cardiac output and venous return match at exactly one value of RAP** (p. 551)

### Core claims

#### The Guyton heart-lung machine thought experiment (pp. 549–550; Fig. 23-9)
- Replace the heart and lungs with a pump of adjustable flow that lifts blood from the right atrium and pushes it into the aorta. Now treat **pump flow** as the controlled (independent) variable and record **RAP** as the dependent variable.
- **Starting point** A: at normal pump flow (5 L/min), RAP = 2 mm Hg.
- **Stop the pump (cardiac arrest):** flow falls to zero. Aortic potential energy stored in elastic recoil continues to push blood from arteries into capillaries and veins until **all intravascular pressures equalize**. The equalized value is the **mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP), ≈ 7 mm Hg** (point B).
- **Why is MSFP not zero?** Because the vascular system is an **elastic container with a finite blood volume**: by Eq. 19-5 (cardiac compliance form), pressure inside depends on volume and compliance (cf. Box 22-2 Windkessel reasoning).
- **Restart the pump at progressively higher flows.** Pump suction lowers RAP (blood is pulled out of the right atrium); CVP exceeds RAP, so venous return flows from the periphery toward the atrium. At pump = 5 L/min, RAP is back to +2 mm Hg. At higher flows, RAP **falls below zero**.
- **At sufficiently negative RAP**, the transmural pressure of the great veins becomes negative and the **veins collapse** — no further increment in venous return is possible. The curve **plateaus at RAP ≈ −1 mm Hg**.

#### The vascular function curve (Fig. 23-9C)
Flip the axes so RAP is on the x-axis (the textbook convention going forward). The result is the **vascular function curve**:
- y-intercept (RAP = 0): a high venous return.
- x-intercept (VR = 0): **MSFP ≈ 7 mm Hg**.
- left plateau at negative RAP (~−1 mm Hg) from venous collapse.
- approximately linear in the physiological range between these limits.

Two complementary mechanisms set its slope (N23-11 in the source): (a) the **high venous compliance** of the capacitance vessels, (b) the **small but finite venous viscous resistance**. Both matter.

The driving identity is:

$$\text{VR} = \frac{\text{MSFP} - \text{RAP}}{R_{\text{venous}}} \quad \text{(linear regime)}$$

which captures: zero VR at RAP = MSFP; rising VR as RAP falls; plateau when veins collapse.

#### Shifts of the vascular function curve (pp. 550–551; Fig. 23-10)
**Two orthogonal interventions, two distinct curve transformations:**

| Intervention | Mechanism | Curve transformation |
|---|---|---|
| ↑ blood volume (transfusion) | More volume in a container of given compliance → ↑MSFP | Parallel **rightward shift** (x-intercept moves from 7 → 9 mm Hg); slope unchanged |
| ↓ blood volume (hemorrhage) | Less volume → ↓MSFP | Parallel **leftward shift** |
| ↑ venomotor tone | Squeezes the venous reservoir against the same blood volume → ↑MSFP | Equivalent to transfusion: rightward shift |
| ↓ venomotor tone | Loosens the reservoir → ↓MSFP | Equivalent to hemorrhage: leftward shift |
| Arteriolar **constriction** | Drops CVP (less blood crosses arterioles per unit time at any given pressure head) → ↓driving force (CVP − RAP) | Curve **flattens** (lower slope) at same x-intercept |
| Arteriolar **dilation** | Raises CVP for the same arterial pressure → ↑driving force | Curve **steepens** (higher slope) at same x-intercept |

The geometric intuition for arteriolar effects: hold RAP at +2 mm Hg; normal CVP = 5 mm Hg gives ΔP = 3 mm Hg → VR = 5 L/min. Arteriolar constriction lowers CVP to 4 mm Hg → ΔP = 2 mm Hg → VR drops to ⅔ × 5 = 3.3 L/min — the curve at that x is lower. Conversely arteriolar dilation raises CVP to 6 mm Hg → ΔP = 4 mm Hg → VR rises to 4/3 × 5 = 6.7 L/min.

In real life (e.g., hemorrhage followed by reflex arteriolar constriction), interventions combine: both x-intercept and slope shift.

#### The cardiac function curve (p. 551)
The **cardiac function curve** is Starling's law replotted with **RAP on the x-axis** and **CO on the y-axis** — a transformation of the ventricular performance curve of Fig. 22-12C (stroke work vs. LAP):
- LAP ≈ proxy for filling pressure ≈ proxy for RAP (in steady state, both atria see the venous return).
- Stroke work × HR ∝ CO at fixed arterial pressure.
So the cardiac function curve plots CO as a monotonically rising function of RAP, in **exactly the same units (L/min and mm Hg)** as the vascular function curve. The whole family of curves of Fig. 22-12C (depending on HR, afterload, contractility) becomes a whole family of cardiac function curves.

#### The matching condition: unique intersection (p. 551; Fig. 23-11A)
In steady state, **CO must equal VR** (continuity at the right atrium). Therefore the operating point is the **unique intersection** of the cardiac function curve with the vascular function curve in the (RAP, flow) plane. At resting baseline this lies at RAP ≈ 2 mm Hg, CO = VR ≈ 5 L/min — point A.

#### Self-stabilization of transient mismatches (p. 551; Fig. 23-11A)
If RAP is transiently bumped up from 2 to 4 mm Hg:
- Move up the cardiac function curve: CO jumps from 5 L/min (A) to 7.3 L/min (B).
- Move along the vascular function curve at the same RAP: VR falls from 5 L/min (A) to 3 L/min (B').
- The mismatch (CO > VR) "sucks the atrium dry" (lowering RAP) and ships blood centrifugally (raising CVP); both effects reduce RAP.
- The system slides along both curves through points C (cardiac) and C' (vascular); the gap narrows; eventually they re-converge at A.

This is the chapter's mechanical statement of **circulatory self-stabilization**: at any RAP not equal to the intersection, the mismatch generates a flow imbalance whose effect on intravascular pressures **drives RAP back toward the intersection.**

#### Permanent shifts require curve translations (Fig. 23-11B, C)
- **Permanent volume increase (transfusion):** vascular curve shifts right (Fig. 23-11B). New intersection sits at **higher RAP** and (typically) higher CO.
- **Permanent contractility increase (e.g., digitalis):** cardiac curve shifts up and to the left (Fig. 23-11C). New intersection sits at **lower RAP** and higher CO — the digitalis effect explains both the increase in CO and the **fall in atrial / pulmonary venous pressure** that relieves pulmonary congestion in heart failure (cf. Box 22-4).
- **Permanent afterload increase (hypertension):** cardiac curve flattens → intersection at higher RAP and lower CO.
- **Permanent venomotor tone increase (sympathetic stress):** vascular curve shifts right → intersection at higher RAP and higher CO.

In general, **the operating point is the intersection of two families of curves**; physiological state and pathology select the curve members; the body sees only the intersection.

### Equations

- **Vascular function (linear regime, MSFP-driven venous return):**

$$\text{VR}(\text{RAP}) = \frac{\text{MSFP} - \text{RAP}}{R_{\text{venous}}}$$

  with the constraint $\text{VR}(\text{RAP}) \le \text{VR}_{\max}$ where $\text{VR}_{\max}$ is the venous-collapse plateau.

- **Mean systemic filling pressure:**

$$\text{MSFP} = \frac{V_{\text{vascular}} - V_0}{C_{\text{vascular}}}$$

  where $V_{\text{vascular}}$ = total intravascular volume, $V_0$ = unstressed volume, $C_{\text{vascular}}$ = overall compliance. **Transfusion ↑ V_vascular; venomotor tone ↓ V₀ and ↓ C_vascular; both raise MSFP.**

- **Steady-state matching condition (the chapter's central diagram):**

$$\text{CO}\bigl(\text{RAP}^*; \, \text{HR}, \, \text{afterload}, \, \text{contractility}\bigr) \;=\; \text{VR}\bigl(\text{RAP}^*; \, V_{\text{vascular}}, \, \text{venomotor tone}, \, \text{arteriolar tone}\bigr)$$

  with $\text{RAP}^*$ the unique intersection.

- **Resting reference values:**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| RAP | 2 mm Hg |
| CO = VR at rest | 5 L/min |
| MSFP | ~7 mm Hg |
| Plateau RAP (venous collapse) | ~−1 mm Hg |

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "When blood flow finally ceases after a cardiac arrest, the pressures in the arteries, capillaries, veins, and right atrium are uniform. This pressure is called the mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP) and is ~7 mm Hg." (p. 549)
- > "MSFP depends on the blood volume and the overall compliance of the entire vascular system. Now that blood flow has ceased, there is obviously no longer any force … driving the blood from the veins into the pump. Therefore, RAP equals MSFP." (p. 549)
- > "As RAP becomes less positive, it provides a greater driving pressure (i.e., greater ΔP = CVP − RAP) for the return of blood from the periphery to the right atrium, as it must for cardiac output to increase. Thus, the cardiac output steadily rises as RAP falls." (pp. 549–550)
- > "Changes in blood volume do not affect the slope of the linear portion of the vascular function curve as long as there is no change in either vessel compliance or resistance." (p. 550)
- > "Change in the tone of the arterioles has a very different effect on the vascular function curve. Because the arterioles contain only a minor fraction of the blood volume, changes in the arteriolar tone have little effect on MSFP and thus on the x-intercept. However, changes in the arteriolar tone can have a marked effect on the CVP." (p. 550)
- > "Venous return (from the vascular function curve) and cardiac output (from the cardiac function curve) can be equal only at the single point where the two curves intersect." (p. 551)
- > "The only way to produce a permanent change in cardiac output, venous return, and RAP is to change at least one of the two function curves." (p. 551)

## Section 4 — Intermediate- and long-term control of the circulation (pp. 551–555)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Endocrine and paracrine vasoactive compounds control the circulatory system on an intermediate- to long-term basis** (pp. 551–554)
- **Pathways for the renal control of ECF volume are the primary long-term regulators of mean arterial pressure** (pp. 554–555)

### Core claims

#### Two classes of humoral controls (p. 551)
1. **Vasoactive substances** — modulate the tone of arteries and veins → direct effect on BP and flow distribution.
2. **Non-vasoactive substances** — act on **kidneys** to set ECF volume → indirect effect on BP via the matching diagram.

#### Vasoactive substances (Table 23-3; pp. 552–554)

**Biogenic amines:**
- **Epinephrine** (adrenal medulla). Mixed agonist: β₂ on most vessels → vasodilation; α₁ where β₂ is sparse (skin) → vasoconstriction. β₁ in heart → ↑HR + ↑inotropy. For the cardiovascular system, epinephrine effects are generally **minor compared with NE released from sympathetic varicosities**.
- **Serotonin (5-HT)** — synthesized by serotonergic neurons, enterochromaffin cells, adrenal chromaffin cells; stored in platelets and mast cells. Acts on 5-HT₁B / 5-HT₂A on VSMCs → vasoconstriction. **Local** role in hemostasis at sites of vessel damage; not a systemic BP regulator.
- **Histamine** — from mast cells (injury, inflammation) and some neurons. **H₁ on VSMCs → vasodilation**; on visceral smooth muscle (bronchi) → contraction.

**Peptides:**
- **Angiotensin II (ANG II)** — the RAAS centerpiece (pp. 552–553). Cascade: **liver → angiotensinogen → (renin, from kidney) → ANG I (decapeptide) → (ACE, on lung endothelium) → ANG II (octapeptide) → (aminopeptidases) → ANG III**.
  - ANG II → **AT₁ₐ on VSMCs → Gq → PLC → ↑Ca²⁺ᵢ → vasoconstriction**.
  - Plasma ANG II is normally too low for systemic vasoconstriction; **the system is recruited in low-renal-perfusion states** (blood loss, exercise, CHF).
  - **Positive-feedback failure mode:** ↓renal perfusion → ↑renin → ↑ANG II → splanchnic + renal vasoconstriction → further ↓renal perfusion → … → acute renal failure. **Goldblatt one-clip two-kidney model** of hypertension (Box 23-1) is the chronic analogue.
  - ANG II's six extra mechanisms (p. 553): (1) ↑cardiac contractility; (2) ↓renal plasma flow → ↑Na⁺ reabsorption; (3) ↑aldosterone release from adrenal cortex; (4) ↑thirst + ↑AVP release; (5) facilitation of NE release at postganglionic sympathetic terminals; (6) cardiac growth factor (Ch 22 Box 22-3 hypertrophy).
- **Arginine vasopressin (AVP)** (posterior pituitary) — V₁ₐ on VSMCs → vasoconstriction, but only at concentrations **higher than the antidiuretic threshold** (Ch 38). In **hemorrhagic shock**, AVP rises enough to contribute substantially to transient BP recovery (Ch 25).
- **Endothelins (ET-1)** — endothelial-cell-derived (Ch 20); ET_A on VSMCs → vasoconstriction. **On a per-mole basis, the most powerful vasoconstrictors known.** Unclear systemic role; clinically targeted in pulmonary hypertension.
- **Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)** — 28-aa peptide released by stretched atrial myocytes → NPR1 (membrane-bound guanylyl cyclase) on VSMCs → ↑cGMP → vasodilation. Also has potent **renal natriuretic + diuretic** actions. Its systemic role in arterial-pressure control is doubtful, but it is a major effector of the **low-pressure receptor → ↓ECF volume** loop.
- **Kinins** — bradykinin (plasma nonapeptide), lysyl-bradykinin (tissue decapeptide), methionyl-lysyl-bradykinin (urine). Generated from **kininogens** by **kallikreins** (plasma, salivary glands, pancreas, sweat glands, intestine, kidney). Degraded by **kininase II = ACE** — so **ACE inhibitors raise bradykinin (vasodilator) and lower ANG II (vasoconstrictor) simultaneously, the basis of their twin BP-lowering actions and the cough side effect.** Bradykinin → B₂ on endothelium → NO + PGI₂ → vasodilation.

**Arachidonic-acid derivatives:**
- **Prostacyclin (PGI₂)** — IP receptor on VSMCs → strong vasodilation.
- **PGE₂** — EP₂, EP₄ → vasodilation.
- **Thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂)** — venous endothelial cyclooxygenase metabolite → contraction; cyclooxygenase inhibitors abolish.

**Nitric oxide (NO)** — eNOS in endothelial cells converts arginine to NO → diffuses to VSMCs → sGC → ↑cGMP → vasodilation (Ch 20 pp. 480 ff). Powerful paracrine vasodilator; its systemic-BP role is contested.

#### Renal control of ECF volume (pp. 554–555)
- **Plasma volume is ≈ 20% of ECF volume.** Capillary Starling forces (Ch 20) maintain rapid equilibration between plasma and interstitium, so the ECF compartment is a single osmotic pool.
- **At constant vascular compliance, ↑plasma volume → ↑transmural pressure** (Eq. 19-5).
- The controlled variable for long-term regulation is **not** ECF volume, blood volume, or interstitial volume per se, but **effective circulating volume** — the functional volume that reflects tissue perfusion as **sensed** by the various stretch receptors. (Ch 40 is the full treatment.)
- **Detectors of effective circulating volume** (Table 40-2 preview): (1) high-pressure baroreceptors (Ch 23 Section 1) — **double duty**: short-term loop in seconds, long-term BP regulation by ECF tuning over days; (2) low-pressure baroreceptors (Ch 23 Section 2); (3) renal-artery baroreceptors; (4) hepatic stretch receptors; (5) atrial myocytes themselves (ANP); (6) CNS osmoreceptors (cross-talk via AVP).
- **Effector — the kidney — receives four parallel signaling pathways** (p. 555):
  1. **Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS).** The dominant pathway. Renal-juxtaglomerular renin → ANG II / III → adrenal aldosterone → distal-nephron Na⁺ retention; ANG II also directly enhances proximal Na⁺ reabsorption.
  2. **Autonomic nervous system.** Renal sympathetic outflow → renal arteriolar tone → renal blood flow → glomerular filtration; also directly stimulates Na⁺ reabsorption.
  3. **Posterior pituitary AVP.** Water retention via principal-cell aquaporin-2 insertion (Ch 38).
  4. **Atrial ANP.** Counters the above: vasodilation + natriuresis + diuresis.
- **Long-term MAP control is therefore renal Na⁺ excretion control.** The renal-body-fluid balance machinery (Guyton's pressure natriuresis: ↑MAP → ↑renal Na⁺ excretion → ↓ECF → ↓blood volume → vascular function curve left-shift → ↓MAP) is the chapter's last word and the topic of Ch 40 in detail. The kidney is "the principal agent in the long-term control of mean arterial pressure" (p. 555).

### Equations

- **RAAS cascade (conceptual):**

$$\text{angiotensinogen} \xrightarrow{\text{renin (kidney)}} \text{ANG I} \xrightarrow{\text{ACE (lung endothelium)}} \text{ANG II} \xrightarrow{\text{aminopeptidases}} \text{ANG III}$$

- **ACE = kininase II identity:**

$$\text{ACE} \;\; \text{generates the vasoconstrictor ANG II} \;\; \text{and} \;\; \text{degrades the vasodilator bradykinin}$$

  → ACE inhibitors lower BP by both arms simultaneously.

- **Long-term operating point (closed-loop sketch, integrating Sections 1–4):**

$$\text{MAP}^* = \text{CO}^* \cdot \text{TPR}^*, \quad \text{with} \quad \text{CO}^* = \text{VR}^* \text{ at } \text{RAP}^*$$

where $\text{RAP}^*$, $\text{CO}^*$, $\text{TPR}^*$ are jointly determined by the intersection of the cardiac and vascular function curves, and the long-term curve positions are set by renal control of $V_{\text{vascular}}$ (RAAS, AVP, ANP) and by the standing autonomic tone (ANS).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In most instances, these control systems operate on a time scale of hours or days, far more slowly than the neurotransmitter-mediated reflex control by the CNS." (p. 551)
- > "ANG II is normally not present in plasma concentrations high enough to produce systemic vasoconstriction. In contrast, ANG II plays a major role in cardiovascular control during blood loss, exercise, and similar circumstances that reduce renal blood flow." (p. 553)
- > "Reduced perfusion pressure in the kidney causes the release of renin … Plasma ANG II levels rise, leading to an intense vasoconstriction in the splanchnic and renal circulations. The resulting reduced renal blood flow leads to even more renin release and higher ANG II levels, a dangerous positive-feedback system that can lead to acute renal failure." (p. 553)
- > "Kininase II is the same as ACE. Thus, the same enzyme (ACE) that generates a vasoconstrictor (ANG II) also disposes of vasodilators (bradykinin)." (p. 554)
- > "The parameter that the body controls in the intermediate- and long-term regulation of the mean arterial pressure is none of these but rather a more vague parameter termed the effective circulating volume." (p. 554)
- > "By regulating total body Na⁺ content, the kidney determines ECF volume. Therefore, the kidney ultimately governs the blood volume and is thus the principal agent in the long-term control of mean arterial pressure." (p. 555)

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Short-term / intermediate-term / long-term BP regulation** (timescales: seconds–minutes / minutes–hours / hours–days).
- **Negative-feedback architecture: detector, afferent, coordinating center, efferent, effector.**
- **High-pressure baroreceptors (carotid sinus, aortic arch) / sinus nerve (nerve of Hering) / depressor branch of vagus / petrosal (inferior) ganglion / nodose (inferior) ganglion**.
- **TRPC1, TRPC3, TRPC4, TRPC5** — nonselective stretch-sensitive cation channels in baroreceptor terminals.
- **Receptor potential — dynamic vs. static components / sigmoidal pressure-activity curve / pulsatile-vs-static curve difference / population recruitment.**
- **Nucleus tractus solitarii (NTS) — obligatory first relay; AMPA (GluA2) and P2X transmission.**
- **Medullary cardiovascular center: vasomotor area (C1, A1 in RVLM) + cardioinhibitory area (nucleus ambiguus + dorsal motor n. vagus) + cardioacceleratory area.**
- **C1 neurons (tonically vasoconstrictive; clonidine target via imidazole receptors).**
- **Bulbospinal pathway / intermediolateral (IML) column of T1–L3 / preganglionic sympathetic (ACh on Nₙ) → postganglionic sympathetic (NE).**
- **Cardiac nerves: middle cervical, stellate, upper thoracic ganglia → SA node, atria, ventricles.**
- **Adrenal medulla as a sympathetic ganglion (chromaffin cells; epinephrine > NE).**
- **Vagal preganglionic neurons → atrial-wall ganglia → postganglionic parasympathetic (ACh) → SA node (right vagus) + AV node (left vagus).**
- **Receptor pharmacology summary: cardiac β₁ (Gαs), M₂ (Gαi); vascular α₁ (Gq), α₂ (Gαi₀), β₂ (Gαs), M₃ (Gq via endothelial NO).**
- **Sympathetic cholinergic vasodilator system to skeletal muscle (nonprimates; cortex → hypothalamus → mesencephalon → bypasses medulla → IML → ACh on VSMCs).**
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors: carotid body (CN IX) + aortic bodies (CN X); glomus cells; senses ↓P_O₂, ↑P_CO₂, ↓pH.**
- **Central chemoreceptors (medulla): senses ↓brain pH ≈ ↑arterial P_CO₂.**
- **Intrinsic chemoreceptor response (vasoconstriction + bradycardia) vs. integrated response (vasoconstriction + tachycardia overriding intrinsic bradycardia via lung-stretch + brain-pH normalization).**
- **Hypothalamus (dorsomedial nucleus → exercise / acute-stress drive on RVLM; PVN → AVP magnocellular pathway).**
- **Spinal segmental CV reflexes; spinal shock and chronic supersensitivity.**
- **Hypertension (essential / primary vs. secondary; Goldblatt one-clip two-kidney model; renal artery stenosis; Liddle disease; coarctation; pheochromocytoma; primary hyperaldosteronism).**
- **Intrinsic determinants of EDV: filling pressure, filling time, ventricular compliance (EDPVR).**
- **Intrinsic determinants of ESV: preload, afterload, HR (Bowditch), contractility.**
- **Low-pressure baroreceptors: atrial (A fibers track HR via atrial systole; B fibers track CVP / venous return / atrial volume via the v wave), pulmonary-artery, ventricular.**
- **Bainbridge reflex (↑atrial stretch → ↑HR, opposite polarity to baroreflex; saturates).**
- **Three-arm atrial-stretch diuresis: reflex renal vasodilation, ↓AVP, ↑ANP.**
- **Biphasic HR and SV but monotonic CO vs. effective circulating volume (Fig. 23-8).**
- **Vascular function curve / MSFP / venous collapse plateau / right shift by transfusion or venomotor tone / slope change by arteriolar tone.**
- **Cardiac function curve (Starling-derived CO vs. RAP) / its family parameterization by HR, afterload, contractility.**
- **Guyton intersection / unique operating point at RAP*** / self-stabilization of transient mismatches / permanent shifts via curve translation (transfusion, inotropy, hypertension, venomotor tone).**
- **Vasoactive substances: amines (Epi, 5-HT, histamine), peptides (ANG II, AVP, ET-1, ANP, kinins), arachidonic-acid derivatives (PGI₂, PGE₂, TXA₂), NO.**
- **RAAS: angiotensinogen → ANG I (renin) → ANG II (ACE) → ANG III (aminopeptidase); AT₁ₐ on VSMC; six extra-vascular ANG II actions.**
- **ACE = kininase II identity → ACE-inhibitor dual mechanism.**
- **Effective circulating volume (functional, not anatomic; sensed by multiple detector classes).**
- **Four parallel renal effector pathways: RAAS, ANS, AVP, ANP.**
