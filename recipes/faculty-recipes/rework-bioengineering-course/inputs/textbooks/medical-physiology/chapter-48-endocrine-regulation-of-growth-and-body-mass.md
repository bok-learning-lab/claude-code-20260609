## Section 1 — Growth hormone (pp. 990–995)

### Subsection headings
- **GH, secreted by somatotrophs in the anterior pituitary, is the principal endocrine regulator of growth** (pp. 990–991)
- **GH is in a family of hormones with overlapping activity** (pp. 991–992)
- **Somatotrophs secrete GH in pulses** (p. 992)
- **GH secretion is under hierarchical control by GH-releasing hormone and somatostatin** (pp. 992–994)
- **Both GH and IGF-1 negatively feed back on GH secretion by somatotrophs** (p. 994)
- **GH has short-term anti-insulin metabolic effects as well as long-term growth-promoting effects mediated by IGF-1** (pp. 994–995)

### Core claims
- **Growth = hyperplasia (cell-number increase) + hypertrophy (cell-size increase) + turnover.** Capacity for division is tissue-specific: CNS neuronal division is essentially complete by age 1 yr; bone, muscle, and fat keep dividing into childhood; skin, blood, and liver retain hyperplastic capacity for life.
- **Genetic determinants matter** — midparental height (average of parents' heights) is among the best predictors of adult stature; achondroplasia is a single-gene example. The chapter focuses instead on nutritional/hormonal regulation.
- **GH is the principal endocrine driver of linear growth.** Childhood GH excess → **gigantism**; childhood GH deficiency → **pituitary dwarfism**. Both Tom Thumb (GH deficiency, adult height ~1.0 m) and the Alton giant (childhood GH-secreting tumor, >2.7 m) had abnormal secretion from early life. GH-deficient children are **normal size at birth** and only later fall behind.
- **Adult-onset GH excess after puberty → acromegaly** (Greek *akron* "top" + *megas" "large"): progressive thickening of bone and soft tissue of head, hands, feet; long bones do **not** lengthen because the epiphyseal plates have closed. Untreated, joint deformity, hypertension, pulmonary insufficiency, and heart failure shorten life.
- **Adult-onset GH deficiency** causes no major illness, but GH replacement (recombinant) increases lean body mass, decreases fat, and improves a sense of vigor.
- **GH synthesis** (Fig. 48-1): made by somatotrophs as a pre-prohormone; processed through ER/Golgi. The **22-kDa form (191 aa)** is dominant; alternative splicing of exon 3 yields a **20-kDa form (176 aa)**. Other forms: 45-kDa dimer and larger multimers. Stored in secretory granules until secreted.
- **GH gene family** (Table 48-1): GH is single-copy, but four homologs exist — three placental (**pvGH** 93% identical; **hCS1/hCS2** = human placental lactogens, 84%) plus pituitary **prolactin (PRL)** (16% homology). All map to the GH gene cluster on chromosome 17 (placental members) with shared intron structure.
- **pvGH** binds the hepatic GH receptor with nearly GH's affinity and may drive systemic IGF-1 in pregnancy. **hCS/hPL** affinity for GHR is 100–1000× lower; they are primarily **lactogenic**. **PRL** binds the PRL receptor (as does GH), but PRL has no affinity for GHR and thus no growth-promoting activity. Both PRL and GH receptors signal via the **JAK** tyrosine-kinase family.
- **Pulsatile secretion** (Fig. 48-2): growth is slow over years, but GH secretion is highly episodic minute-to-minute. **>70% of daily GH is secreted in volleys during the first hours of slow-wave sleep** (sleep-wake, not light-dark, dependent). Peak GH may be ~100× interpulse troughs. Exercise, stress, high-protein meals, and fasting raise mean GH by **increasing pulse frequency, not amplitude**. Daily integrated GH is higher during pubertal growth.
- **Hierarchical hypothalamic control** (Fig. 48-3):
  - **GHRH** — 43-aa peptide from small-bodied neurons of the **arcuate nucleus**; reaches somatotrophs via hypophyseal portal blood; **stimulatory**. Binds a Gαs-coupled GPCR → ↑cAMP → ↑GH gene transcription and synthesis; cAMP also opens plasma-membrane Ca²⁺ channels → ↑[Ca²⁺]ᵢ → release of preformed GH.
  - **Somatostatin (SS)** — 14-aa peptide from the **periventricular region**; secreted into portal blood; **potent inhibitor**. Binds Gαi-coupled SSTR → ↓adenylyl cyclase → ↓[Ca²⁺]ᵢ → reduced GHRH responsiveness. **When both GHRH and SS are present, SS prevails.**
  - **Ghrelin** — 28-aa peptide (octanoylated serine required for activity) from gastric fundus endocrine cells (also throughout GI tract, small amounts in arcuate nucleus); released in response to **fasting**; stimulates GH secretion and is **orexigenic**. Binds **GHSR1a** (GH secretagogue receptor 1a), a GPCR that does *not* bind GHRH.
- **Primary regulation is stimulatory**: pituitary-stalk section (interrupting portal flow) → GH falls (but PRL rises, because hypothalamic dopamine normally inhibits PRL). GH pulses are entrained by pulsatile GHRH, not by periodic loss of SS.
- **Negative feedback** (Fig. 48-4): GH stimulates IGF-1 release from peripheral targets; circulating IGF-1 then suppresses GH by **(1)** direct action on somatotrophs, **(2)** suppressing hypothalamic GHRH release, and **(3)** increasing SS secretion. Independently, **GH inhibits its own secretion ("short-loop" feedback)**. IGF-1 acts via a receptor tyrosine kinase, not cAMP/Ca²⁺.
- **GH in plasma**: ~40% of 22-kDa GH is bound to a **GH-binding protein** (proteolytic fragment of the GHR extracellular domain), which extends GH half-life (~25 min) and competes with target tissues.
- **GH receptor (GHR)**: 620-aa, single-pass; ~130 kDa (heavily glycosylated). A **type I cytokine receptor** — tyrosine-kinase-associated. One GH bridges two GHR monomers → **dimerization** → activation of associated **JAK2** → cascade of phosphorylations (→ STAT signaling).
- **Two distinct GH action classes:**
  - **Short-term, anti-insulin / diabetogenic (minutes–hours; Table 48-2)**: ↑lipolysis (fat), ↓glucose uptake (muscle), ↑gluconeogenesis (liver) → insulin resistance. Chronic GH excess (acromegaly) → insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, frank diabetes.
  - **Long-term, growth-promoting (via IGF-1)**: Salmon & Daughaday (1957) showed GH does **not** directly grow epiphyseal cartilage in vitro; serum from GH-treated animals does. This led to the **somatomedin hypothesis** — GH provokes secretion of a circulating mediator (later identified as IGF-1/IGF-2).

### Equations / quantitative relations
- **GH/IGF-1 axis (signed feedback summary):**
  $$\text{GHRH}\xrightarrow{+}\ \text{GH}\ \xrightarrow{+}\ \text{IGF-1}\ \dashv\ \text{GH (3 routes)};\qquad \text{SS}\dashv \text{GH};\qquad \text{GH}\dashv \text{GH (short loop)}$$
  where $\xrightarrow{+}$ is stimulation and $\dashv$ is inhibition. IGF-1's three inhibitory routes: direct on somatotroph; ↓GHRH; ↑SS.
- **GHRH signaling:** $\uparrow\text{cAMP} \Rightarrow \uparrow\text{(GH transcription)} \;\&\; \uparrow[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i \Rightarrow$ release of stored GH.
- **Circulating GH half-life:** $t_{1/2}\approx 25\ \text{min}$; ~40% of 22-kDa GH bound to GH-binding protein.

## Section 2 — Growth-promoting hormones: IGFs, life stages, permissive hormones, bone (pp. 995–1001)

### Subsection headings
- **IGF-1 is the principal mediator of the growth-promoting action of GH** (pp. 995–996)
- **IGF-2 acts similarly to IGF-1 but is less dependent on GH** (pp. 996–997)
- **Growth rate parallels plasma levels of IGF-1 except early and late in life** (pp. 998–999)
- **Thyroid hormones, steroids, and insulin also promote growth** (pp. 999–1000)
- **The musculoskeletal system responds to growth stimuli of the GHRH–GH–IGF-1 axis** (pp. 1000–1001)

### Core claims
- **IGF-1 integrates pulsatile GH.** Plasma [IGF-1] varies <2-fold over 24 h despite wildly fluctuating GH, because IGF-1 (like GH) circulates **bound to ≥6 IGF-binding proteins** (mostly hepatic); **>90% of serum IGF-1 is protein-bound**. The **free fraction** is the biologically active component. Binding proteins buffer the pool and aid transfer to tissue receptors; locally, they retain secreted IGF, keeping tissue concentrations well above plasma.
- **Sources & paracrine action:** IGF-1 and IGF-2 are made in liver, kidney, muscle, cartilage, bone. The **liver supplies most circulating IGF-1** (endocrine action); locally-made IGF-1 in muscle/cartilage/bone acts paracrine/autocrine.
- **IGF-1 receptor (IGF1R)** (Fig. 48-6): a heterotetramer (two extracellular α, two transmembrane β) **structurally homologous to the insulin receptor**, with intrinsic **β-chain tyrosine kinase**; ligand binding → autophosphorylation → downstream phosphorylation. Cross-reactivity: insulin binds IGF1R (~100× weaker) and vice versa; **hybrid receptors** (one insulin αβ + one IGF1R αβ) exist and prefer IGF-1. Despite this, **IGF-1 favors growth; insulin favors glucose/lipid metabolism.**
- **IGFs are insulin-like:** injected IGF-1 causes hypoglycemia (chiefly by ↑muscle glucose uptake); less effective than insulin on fat/liver (few IGF1R there). In muscle, IGF-1 promotes amino-acid uptake and protein synthesis at concentrations below those that stimulate glucose uptake — i.e., **growth occurs at lower IGF-1 levels than hypoglycemia** (Box 48-1).
- **IGF-2 vs. IGF-1:**
  - **Secretion** is less GH-dependent: in GH deficiency (pituitary dwarfism) IGF-1 falls but **IGF-2 does not**; in GH excess IGF-1 rises but IGF-2 does not.
  - **Receptor:** IGF-2 preferentially binds the **IGF-2 receptor (IGF2R) = mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) receptor**, a single-chain polypeptide with **no kinase domain**. IGF2R internalization clears IGF-2; in the *trans* Golgi the same receptor (different site) traffics M6P-tagged lysosomal hydrolases to lysosomes. IGF-2 also binds IGF1R, sharing IGF-1's growth-promoting and acute-hypoglycemic effects.
- **Growth across life stages** (Fig. 48-7):
  - **Puberty:** peak height velocity coincides with peak plasma IGF-1; GH pulse frequency rises sharply; **estradiol and testosterone promote GH secretion**.
  - **Early childhood / intrauterine:** rapid longitudinal growth despite **low IGF-1** — so non-GH/IGF-1 factors dominate. GH-deficient infants have low IGF-1 but **normal length/weight at birth**. Candidate fetal drivers: **insulin** and **IGF-2** (plasma IGF-2 peaks just before birth, plummets after, then doubles by age 1 yr and stays at adult level to ≥80 yr).
  - **Adulthood:** linear growth ceases; GH/IGF-1 persist (declining with age) and remain important for **body composition** — GH replacement in deficient adults ↑muscle mass, ↓fat, improves nitrogen balance.
  - **Nutrition:** high-protein intake triggers GH; but a GH rise alone is insufficient to fully stimulate IGF-1 — **fasting lowers IGF-1 even as GH rises** because insulin is low. Thus **insulin is required (in some tissues) for GH to stimulate IGF-1 effectively.**
- **Permissive growth hormones** (necessary but not sufficient alongside GH/IGF-1):
  - **Thyroid hormones (T₄/T₃):** next to GH the most prominent. Early severe deficiency → **cretinism** (dwarfism + mental retardation); hypothyroidism before epiphyseal fusion → growth retardation, partly recoverable as **catch-up growth** with treatment.
  - **Sex steroids:** androgen/estrogen excess before the growth spurt accelerates bone growth **but** hastens skeletal maturation and **narrows the growth window** → usually *reduced* ultimate height (e.g., precocious puberty, sex-steroid-secreting tumors, exogenous steroids).
  - **Glucocorticoids:** **excess inhibits growth** (Cushing, ACTH-secreting tumors, therapeutic steroids) — and GH administration does **not** correct it (GH/IGF-1 are not low), implicating direct action at the growth plate. In adults, excess → wasting (osteoporosis, muscle weakness, bruising). **Deficiency** does not substantially affect growth.
  - **Insulin:** an important growth factor, especially **in utero**. Maternal diabetes → fetal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperinsulinemia → **macrosomia**. Pancreatic agenesis / severe insulin resistance (e.g., **leprechaunism**, an insulin-receptor defect) → small at birth. Severe insulin deficiency → catabolism/wasting; mild deficiency (undertreated diabetes) diminishes child growth, reversible with good control.
- **The growth plate and endochondral ossification:**
  - Longitudinal growth = **hyperplasia of chondrocytes at the growth plates of long bones, followed by endochondral ossification.** Chondrocytes divide along the plate, migrate toward mature bone, and synthesize cartilage matrix (**type II collagen, hyaluronic acid, mucopolysaccharides**); they respond directly to GH (with local IGF-1 generation).
  - Matrix ossifies → calcified cartilage → remodeled by osteoclasts/osteoblasts → replaced by normal bone → becomes **metaphysis**. Growth ceases within years of puberty when chondrocytes stop dividing and **epiphyseal closure** occurs.
  - **After puberty, radial growth** continues: periosteal deposition exceeds endosteal resorption → bones widen.
  - **Genetic dwarfisms** (collagen/mucopolysaccharide defects) leave the **GHRH-GH-IGF-1 axis intact** with no compensatory GH rise — the axis responds to IGF-1, not to growth per se.
  - Other tissue growth factors (Table 48-3: NGF, FGF, angiogenesis factor, VEGF, EGF, HGF) act largely paracrine/autocrine with tissue-specific roles.

## Section 3 — Regulation of body mass (pp. 1001–1005)

### Subsection headings
- (opener) nutrient intake modulates the growth hormones; macro- and micronutrient effects (p. 1001)
- **The balance between energy intake and expenditure determines body mass** (pp. 1001–1002)
- **Energy expenditure comprises resting metabolic rate, activity-related energy expenditure, and diet-induced thermogenesis** (p. 1002)
- **Hypothalamic centers control the sensations of satiety and hunger** (p. 1002)
- **Leptin tells the brain how much fat is stored** (pp. 1002–1003)
- **Leptin and insulin are anorexigenic (i.e., satiety) signals for the hypothalamus** (pp. 1003–1004)
- **Ghrelin is an orexigenic signal for the hypothalamus** (p. 1004)
- **Plasma nutrient levels and enteric hormones are short-term factors that regulate feeding** (pp. 1004–1005)

### Core claims
- **Nutrition feeds back on the growth hormones:** amino acids + carbohydrates → insulin; amino acids → GH. Glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids directly regulate gene transcription and mRNA translation. Micronutrients matter too (e.g., iodine deficiency → dwarfism). Early-life nutritional deprivation limits growth **and** predisposes to **mid-life obesity** (WWII-deprivation epidemiology) — early "programming."
- **Energy balance sets body mass:** $\text{intake} > \text{expenditure}$ (positive balance) over time → mass gain. Small chronic excesses dominate the **obesity epidemic** — e.g., a surplus of 20 kcal/day (1 tsp sugar) → ~1 kg fat/yr, ~20 kg over 2 decades. A finely tuned system must monitor body mass and tune feeding to avoid both deficit and excess.
- **Three components of energy expenditure:**
  1. **Resting metabolic rate (RMR)** ≈ 2100 kcal/day for a young 70-kg adult; supports temperature, basal organ function, and basic cellular processes.
  2. **Activity-related expenditure** — exercise plus **non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT)** (fidgeting, posture, typing); NEAT varies 3–10× across people and can reach ≥500 kcal/day, plausibly explaining differential weight gain at equal intake.
  3. **Diet-induced thermogenesis** ≈ 10% of daily expenditure; **protein has a higher thermic effect** than carbohydrate or fat.
  - **Thyroid hormone** is the major regulator of thermogenesis: excess ↑RMR and NEAT; deficiency the reverse.
- **Hypothalamic feeding centers** (classic lesion/stimulation studies):
  - **Satiety center = ventromedial nucleus (VMN).** Stimulation → satiety even with food present; lesion → **hyperphagia**.
  - **Hunger (feeding) center = lateral hypothalamic area (LHA).** Stimulation → voracious appetite even when fed; lesion → **aphagia**.
- **Leptin** — a **17-kDa adipocyte hormone** (Greek *leptos* "thin"), the adiposity signal. **Ob/Ob mice lack leptin → morbid obesity; Db/Db mice lack the leptin receptor → leptin-resistant obesity** (and type 2 diabetes). **Parabiosis** (Fig. 48-8) localized the missing factor and receptor. Leptin (LEP) was positionally cloned by Friedman (1994); the receptor **LEP-R** (1995) is a **JAK2/STAT tyrosine-kinase-associated receptor**; its "long form" is enriched in the **arcuate nucleus**.
  - Plasma leptin **rises in proportion to adipose mass**; half-time ~75 min; acute food intake/fasting barely changes it → leptin is an **intermediate-to-long-term** signal, whereas **insulin (and GLP-1, CCK) are short-term** regulators. Leptin also promotes fuel utilization; leptin-deficient humans show starvation-like fuel conservation.
- **Arcuate melanocortin system** — two leptin/insulin-responsive neuron classes (Fig. 48-9):
  - **POMC/CART neurons (anorexigenic):** insulin and leptin **stimulate** them → release **α-MSH** (a POMC cleavage product) onto **MC3R/MC4R** on second-order neurons → satiety, ↓intake, **and** ↑energy expenditure (descending sympathetic activation). **CART** is likewise anorexigenic. ~4% of severe early-onset obesity = **MC3R/MC4R mutations** (MC4R is the most common monogenic human obesity).
  - **NPY/AgRP neurons (orexigenic):** insulin and leptin **suppress** them. **NPY** acts on Y1R/Y5R GPCRs → eating; **AgRP** is an **antagonist at MC4R**, blocking α-MSH's anorexigenic effect. The **agouti mouse** overexpresses agouti protein (an MC-receptor inhibitor) → yellow fur (MC1R block) + obesity (MC3R/MC4R block).
  - **Secondary neuron targets (5):** LHA (releases orexigenic MCH / orexins A,B), VMN (satiety), DMN, PVN (projects to cortex & brainstem), and **NTS** (visceral sensory integration).
- **Ghrelin** — the **orexigenic** gastric signal: made by gastric-mucosa endocrine cells in response to fasting; systemic ghrelin acutely ↑food intake at physiological doses. Counterintuitively, circulating ghrelin is **lower in obese** than lean people (so it does not drive their intake); **gastric bypass sharply lowers ghrelin** alongside weight/intake decline. Acts via **GHSR1a** on arcuate neurons and vagal afferents; also stimulates GH (links growth and body-mass control).
- **Short-term feeding control** — glucostatic/aminostatic/lipostatic theories: hypoglycemia → hunger, ↑firing of glucose-sensitive LHA neurons, ↓firing of VMN neurons, activates LHA orexin neurons. **GI distention** → vagal afferents → NTS → suppresses the hunger center. Meal-released **enteric peptides are anorexigenic** — GRP, **CCK** (most important; works best intraperitoneally, requires intact vagus), PYY, SS, glucagon, **GLP-1** — reducing meal size. An oropharyngeal (chewing/swallowing) reflex may meter intake.
- **Obesity** (Box 48-3): $\mathrm{BMI}=\dfrac{\text{weight (kg)}}{[\text{height (m)}]^2}$; categories: underweight <18.5, normal 18.5–24.9, overweight 25–29.9, obese ≥30. Most obese humans are **not** leptin-deficient — plasma leptin rises with BMI; they are **leptin-resistant** (~⅓ respond to high-dose exogenous leptin; ⅔ do not). Rare monogenic causes: leptin deficiency, leptin-receptor mutation, POMC mutation, **MC4R mutation** (most common). No satisfactory pharmacotherapy yet.

### Equations / quantitative relations
- **Energy balance → body mass:**
  $$\frac{d(\text{body mass})}{dt} \propto E_{\text{intake}} - E_{\text{expenditure}},\qquad E_{\text{expenditure}} = \text{RMR} + E_{\text{activity}} + E_{\text{diet-induced}}$$
  with typical shares RMR ≈ 60–70% (~2100 kcal/day, 70-kg adult), activity (incl. NEAT) variable, diet-induced ≈ 10%.
- **Chronic small imbalance:** $\Delta E = 20\ \text{kcal/day} \Rightarrow \approx 1\ \text{kg fat/yr} \Rightarrow \approx 20\ \text{kg / 20 yr}.$
- **Body mass index:**
  $$\mathrm{BMI} = \frac{\text{weight (kg)}}{[\text{height (m)}]^{2}}\quad(\text{obese} \ge 30).$$
- **Melanocortin balance at MC4R (net feeding drive):**
  $$\alpha\text{-MSH} \;\dashv\; \text{appetite} \quad\text{vs.}\quad \text{AgRP} \;\dashv\; \text{MC4R}\ (\Rightarrow \uparrow \text{appetite});\qquad \text{leptin/insulin} \xrightarrow{+}\text{POMC},\ \dashv\ \text{NPY/AgRP}.$$

### Figures

#### Figure 48-8 — Parabiosis experiments *(viewed)*
Three surgically conjoined mouse pairs sharing ~1% cross-circulation, used to deduce that the Ob locus encodes a **secreted factor** and the Db locus encodes its **receptor**.
- **A — Ob/Ob + wild-type:** the **Ob mouse loses weight** → Ob mice lack a blood-borne factor (leptin) supplied by the wild-type partner.
- **B — Db/Db + wild-type:** the **wild-type mouse starves** → Db mice overproduce the factor (no receptor to feel it) and flood the partner.
- **C — Ob/Ob + Db/Db:** the **Ob mouse loses weight; the Db mouse stays obese** → Db supplies factor to Ob, but Db itself cannot respond (no receptor).
- **Conclusions:** Db makes excess of the factor that cures Ob; Db lacks the receptor; absence of the receptor removes negative feedback → high circulating factor.

> Vision note: the classic genetic-logic figure establishing leptin (Ob = ligand) vs. leptin receptor (Db = receptor); anchor for any RAG query on how the leptin/LEP-R system was deduced.

#### Figure 48-9 — Control of appetite *(viewed)*
Integrated whole-body wiring diagram of energy-balance signaling.
- **Hypothalamus (center):** the **arcuate-nucleus** circuit — an **anorexigenic POMC/CART neuron** and (paired) an orexigenic NPY/AgRP neuron — projecting up to **higher CNS centers** and to **glucose-sensitive neurons**, with the labeled secondary nuclei (LHA, VMN, DMN, PVN, NTS).
- **Adipocyte (lower left):** secretes **leptin** into blood → crosses to the arcuate nucleus (anorexigenic).
- **GI tract (right):** **distention stimulates vagal afferents**; **intestinal cells secrete CCK**; the small intestine absorbs foodstuffs into blood (delivering glucose and other nutrients sensed centrally). Blood-borne signals (leptin, insulin, ghrelin, nutrients) converge on the arcuate nucleus; vagal/NTS pathways carry short-term gut signals.

> Vision note: the keystone body-mass figure — ties the adipocyte (leptin), pancreas (insulin), stomach (ghrelin), and gut (CCK/distention/vagus) signals to the arcuate POMC vs. NPY/AgRP decision and onward to the five secondary nuclei.

## Glossary

- **Hyperplasia / hypertrophy** — increase in cell number / cell size.
- **Somatotroph** — anterior-pituitary GH-secreting cell.
- **GH (growth hormone)** — 22-kDa (dominant) / 20-kDa isoforms; JAK2-STAT5 signaling via dimerized GHR.
- **GHRH** — 43-aa arcuate-nucleus releaser; Gs/cAMP; stimulates GH.
- **Somatostatin (SS)** — 14-aa periventricular inhibitor; Gi; SS prevails over GHRH.
- **Ghrelin** — 28-aa acylated gastric peptide; GHSR1a; stimulates GH and appetite (orexigenic).
- **GH-binding protein** — cleaved GHR ectodomain; extends GH half-life (~25 min).
- **Somatomedin hypothesis** — GH acts on growth by inducing a circulating mediator (IGF-1/2).
- **IGF-1 / IGF-2** — insulin-like growth factors; IGF-1 GH-dependent and growth-integrating; IGF-2 less GH-dependent, fetal.
- **IGF-binding proteins** — ≥6; bind >90% of serum IGF-1; free fraction is active.
- **IGF1R** — insulin-receptor-like RTK heterotetramer; **IGF2R / M6P receptor** — single chain, no kinase.
- **Acromegaly / gigantism / pituitary dwarfism / Laron dwarfism** — adult GH excess / childhood GH excess / GH deficiency / GH-receptor mutation.
- **Endochondral ossification** — chondrocyte hyperplasia at the growth plate → calcified cartilage → bone; ends at epiphyseal closure.
- **Catch-up growth** — recovery after transient hypothyroid or insulin-deficient growth arrest.
- **RMR / NEAT / diet-induced thermogenesis** — the three energy-expenditure components.
- **VMN (satiety center) / LHA (hunger center)** — classic hypothalamic feeding nuclei.
- **Leptin / LEP-R (Ob / Db)** — adipocyte adiposity hormone / its JAK2-STAT receptor.
- **POMC / α-MSH / CART** — anorexigenic arcuate peptides; **MC3R/MC4R** melanocortin receptors.
- **NPY / AgRP** — orexigenic arcuate peptides; AgRP is an MC4R antagonist.
- **Orexigenic / anorexigenic** — appetite-promoting / satiety-promoting.
- **CCK, PYY, GLP-1, GRP** — meal-released anorexigenic enteric peptides; vagus-dependent.
- **BMI** — weight/height²; obese ≥30; leptin resistance underlies common obesity.
