# Chapter 26 — Organization of the Respiratory System

> Section V · The Respiratory System · pp. 590–605 · Author: Walter F. Boron
> **First chapter of a new section (Section V).** This chapter opens respiratory physiology by recapitulating the diffusion–convection logic of Ch 17 in gas-exchange language: why a circulating organism still needs an external air pump, why an air pump still needs hemoglobin, and why both still need surface-area amplification. It also establishes the symbol conventions (Table 26-2) used in Chs 27–32, the partial-pressure conventions (Boxes 26-1, 26-2), the volume conventions BTPS / ATPS / STPD (Box 26-3), and the canonical lung volumes and capacities (TV, IRV, ERV, RV, TLC, FRC, IC, VC, FEV₁).

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Comparative physiology of respiration** (pp. 590–596) — external vs. internal respiration; the historical chemistry of O₂ and CO₂; diffusion in a unicellular organism; convection and surface-area amplification; respiratory pigments; "pathophysiology recapitulates phylogeny in reverse."
2. **Organization of the respiratory system in humans** (pp. 596–600) — the six functional components (air pump, gas carriage, gas-exchange surface, circulation, local V/Q control, central control); symbol conventions; conducting airways (generations 0–16) vs. respiratory zone (17–23); alveolar pneumocytes; pulmonary vs. bronchial circulation.
3. **Nonrespiratory functions of the lung** (pp. 600–601) — olfaction; warming/humidifying/filtering; left-ventricular reservoir; embolus filter; biochemical conversions (e.g., angiotensin I → II by ACE; selective handling of vasoactive amines, eicosanoids, peptides).
4. **Lung volumes and capacities** (pp. 601–605) — spirometry (TV, IRV, ERV, VC, IC, FEV₁); the residual volume problem; He-dilution; N₂-washout; body plethysmography by Boyle's law.

## Section 1 — Comparative physiology of respiration (pp. 590–596)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **External respiration is the exchange of O₂ and CO₂ between the atmosphere and the mitochondria** (pp. 590–591)
- **Diffusion is the major mechanism of external respiration for small aquatic organisms** (pp. 591–592)
- **Convection enhances diffusion by producing steeper gradients across the diffusion barrier** (pp. 592–594)
- **Respiratory pigments such as hemoglobin increase the carrying capacity of the blood for both O₂ and CO₂** (p. 595)
- **Surface area amplification enhances diffusion** (p. 595)
- **Pathophysiology recapitulates phylogeny … in reverse** (pp. 595–596)

### Core claims
- **External vs. internal respiration.** *External respiration* = the two-way transport chain that moves O₂ from atmosphere to mitochondria and CO₂ from mitochondria to atmosphere. *Internal respiration* = mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation itself (p. 1185). The Boron section (Chs 26–32) is about external respiration.
- **Five purposes of breathing.** (i) O₂ delivery; (ii) CO₂ elimination; (iii) **acid-base homeostasis** (CO₂ is one of the body's two principal acid-base degrees of freedom; see Ch 28); (iv) **vocalization / voice**; (v) **host defense** (mucociliary clearance, alveolar macrophages, cough, sneeze, immunoglobulins on the airway surface). Olfaction is a sixth, nonrespiratory side-effect (p. 600).
- **History (didactic).** A century-long arc from phlogiston to oxygen runs from Black (CO₂, 1750s), Cavendish, Scheele, Priestley, to **Lavoisier** (named "oxygen"; demolished phlogiston theory) and **Spallanzani** (showed O₂ consumption / CO₂ production occurs in *tissues*, not in the lungs). The chapter uses this to make the point that respiratory physiology has always been built from gas chemistry plus tissue biochemistry.
- **Diffusion baseline (Fig. 26-1A).** A unicellular organism in pond water meets metabolic demand by passive O₂/CO₂ diffusion alone. Profiles of $P_{O_2}$ and $P_{CO_2}$ are shallow gradients across an **extracellular unstirred layer**, an abrupt step across the plasma membrane (some membrane resistance), and a second shallow gradient across the **intracellular unstirred layer** to the mitochondria. Flow obeys a simplified Fick's law (Eq. 26-1):

$$\text{Flow} \propto \Delta P$$

- **The 1-mm rule.** Diffusion suffices until organism diameter exceeds ~1 mm; beyond that, an external convective system is required.
- **External convection.** Mechanisms in the animal kingdom (paramecium cilia → oyster filter feeding → fish gills → amphibian buccal pumping → reptile/bird/mammal negative-pressure thoracic pump) all serve the same purpose: keep the bulk-phase composition at the gas-exchange barrier as close as practical to the bulk-phase composition far from the organism, so that the across-barrier $\Delta P$ stays maximal (Fig. 26-1B).
- **Internal convection.** A circulatory system moves O₂-poor / CO₂-rich blood to the gas-exchange surface and O₂-rich / CO₂-poor blood to the mitochondria. A **single ventricle** (fish, amphibian) mixes systemic-venous and pulmonary-vein blood and lowers the effective $\Delta P$ at both the gas-exchange barrier and at the mitochondria. A **four-chambered heart in series with two separate circulations** (advanced reptile, bird, mammal — Fig. 26-1D) keeps the gradients maximal at both ends. Right-to-left intracardiac shunts (atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus) are the human pathophysiological reversion to the fish/amphibian state.
- **Surface-area amplification.** Equation 26-2:

$$\text{Flow} \propto \Delta P \cdot \text{Area}$$

  Gills *evaginate* the gas exchange barrier; lungs *invaginate* it. Numerical anchors: total human alveolar surface area ≈ **50–100 m²** (chapter text gives "50 to 100 m²"; commonly cited as ~70 m²); ≈ **300 million alveoli**; alveolar diameter 75–300 μm; aggregate alveolar volume 5–6 L; gas exchange barrier (type I pneumocyte + interstitium + endothelium) **0.15–0.30 μm thick**. A simple sphere of 5.5 L volume would have only 0.16 m² of surface area — invagination amplifies area >300-fold.
- **Excess capacity.** O₂ and CO₂ transport across the human alveolar wall is ~**3× faster than necessary at rest at sea level**. The redundancy is the reserve drawn on during exercise, altitude, lung disease, and ageing. Pneumonectomy (loss of ~½ of the surface area) or pulmonary edema (thickening of the barrier) can erode this reserve to a clinically symptomatic point.
- **Respiratory pigments.** Without hemoglobin, plasma O₂ solubility ($s \approx 0.0013$ mM/mm Hg at 37 °C) is so low that mixed-venous blood would equilibrate with alveolar gas within ~1% of capillary length and then carry no further O₂; oxygen delivery would be incompatible with life. Hemoglobin **reversibly binds O₂** (≈96% saturated at normal alveolar $P_{O_2}$), raising blood O₂-carrying capacity ~65-fold over saline; it also chemically transports CO₂ (carbamino + Bohr-shift coupling) and serves as the dominant non-bicarbonate **blood pH buffer**. Other lineages use hemocyanin (Cu, mollusks/arthropods) or hemerythrin (Fe, polychaetes); only the iron-porphyrin hemoglobin reaches mammalian efficiency.
- **Air vs. water.** Air contains ~210 mL O₂ / L (STPD); water in equilibrium with air contains only ~35 mL O₂ / L — a 6-fold disadvantage to gill-breathers. A human extracts 250 mL O₂ from ~4000 mL inspired air per minute (ratio 16 : 1); an oyster must pump ~16 000 mL water per 1 mL O₂ extracted.
- **"Pathophysiology recapitulates phylogeny in reverse."** Failure of any one tier of the respiratory system makes the human behave as a more primitive organism: air-pump failure (barbiturate overdose, ALS, kyphoscoliosis) → unicellular organism with stagnant unstirred layer; surface-area loss (emphysema, lobectomy) → amphibian sack-lungs; intracardiac shunt → fish single-ventricle mixing; severe anemia → invertebrate without efficient pigment.

### Equations

- **Fick proportionality** (Eq. 26-1):

$$\text{Flow} \propto \Delta P$$

- **Surface-area amplification** (Eq. 26-2):

$$\text{Flow} \propto \Delta P \cdot \text{Area}$$

- **Dalton's law / mole-fraction definition** (Box 26-2):

$$P_Z = X_Z \cdot P_{\text{total}}$$

- **Henry's law for dissolved gas concentration** (Box 26-2):

$$[\text{O}_2]_{\text{dis}} = s \cdot P_{O_2}$$

  with $s \approx 0.0013$ mM/mm Hg for O₂ at 37 °C in plasma; $s_{CO_2}$ is ~23-fold higher.

- **Wet-gas partial pressure** (Box 26-1):

$$P_{O_2} = F_{O_2}^{\text{dry}} \cdot (P_B - P_{H_2O})$$

  In room-temperature dry air: $P_{O_2} = 0.21 \cdot 760 = 159$ mm Hg. In wet tracheal air at 37 °C: $P_{O_2} = 0.21 \cdot (760 - 47) = 149$ mm Hg.

- **Volume conventions** (Box 26-3):

$$\Delta V_{\text{BTPS}} = \frac{P_B - P_{H_2O}\!\left(T_{\text{ambient}}\right)}{P_B - P_{H_2O}\!\left(T_{\text{body}}\right)} \cdot \frac{T_{\text{body}}}{T_{\text{ambient}}} \cdot \Delta V_{\text{ATPS}}$$

$$\Delta V_{\text{BTPS}} = \frac{P_B}{P_B - P_{H_2O}\!\left(T_{\text{body}}\right)} \cdot \frac{T_{\text{body}}}{273\,\text{K}} \cdot \Delta V_{\text{STPD}}$$

  Worked anchors at $T_{\text{body}}=37$ °C, $T_{\text{ambient}}=25$ °C: 1000 mL ATPS = **1074 mL BTPS**; 1000 mL STPD = **1210 mL BTPS**.

### Clinical boxes — Boxes 26-1, 26-2, 26-3

- **Box 26-1 — Wet gases.** Partial pressures of O₂ and CO₂ in solutions equilibrated with wet air; canonical derivation of $P_{O_2} = 149$ mm Hg at 37 °C from dry-air $P_{O_2} = 159$ mm Hg minus $P_{H_2O} = 47$ mm Hg.

- **Box 26-3 — Conventions for measurement of volumes of gases.** **BTPS** — body temperature (37 °C, 310 K), $P_B$, saturated with water vapor: the convention for lung volumes. **ATPS** — ambient temperature, $P_B$, saturated: the convention for spirometer readings. **STPD** — 0 °C (273 K), 760 mm Hg, dry: the convention for blood gases. 1 mole of an ideal gas at STPD occupies 22.4 L.

Four-panel figure that walks the diffusion-plus-convection logic from a paramecium to a mammal. **Panel A — Unicellular organism, no convection.** A single cell sits in still bulk pond water. The y-axis of an overlaid grid shows $[O_2]$ and $[CO_2]$; the x-axis is distance. Profiles drop / rise gradually across the **extracellular unstirred layer**, step abruptly at the plasma membrane (some membrane resistance to gas flow), then drop / rise gradually across the **intracellular unstirred layer** to the mitochondria. ## Section 2 — Organization of the respiratory system in humans (pp. 596–600)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Humans optimize each aspect of external respiration — ventilation, circulation, area amplification, gas carriage, local control, and central control** (pp. 596–597)
- **Conducting airways deliver fresh air to the alveolar spaces** (pp. 597–598)
- **Alveolar air spaces are the site of gas exchange** (pp. 599–600)

### Core claims — six functional components

The textbook lists six and uses them as the organizing skeleton for all of Section V:

1. **Air pump (external convection, Ch 27).** Upper airway + large pulmonary airways + thoracic cage + respiratory muscles. Inspiration is **active**: contraction of diaphragm and external intercostals expands the thoracic cavity, lowers intrapleural pressure, expands alveoli passively, and lowers alveolar pressure below atmospheric so that air flows in. Quiet expiration is **passive** (elastic recoil); forced expiration uses abdominal and internal intercostal muscles.
2. **Gas carriage in blood (Chs 28, 29).** RBCs concentrate hemoglobin to ~33 g/dL; arterial blood carries ~20 mL O₂ / dL (~65× more than saline). CO₂ is carried as dissolved gas (~5%), as carbamino-Hb (~5–10%), and as bicarbonate (~80–85%). The bicarbonate route is coupled to the buffering of H⁺ on hemoglobin and is the **link between gas transport and acid-base homeostasis** (Ch 28).
3. **Gas-exchange surface (Ch 30).** Alveoli are 75–300 μm hemispheric pouches lined by a continuous layer of pneumocytes; the diffusion path from alveolar gas to RBC hemoglobin is ~0.6 μm total (pneumocyte ~0.15 μm + basement membranes + endothelium).
4. **Circulation (internal convection, Ch 31).** Four-chambered heart in series; pulmonary and systemic circuits described in detail in Ch 17. The pulmonary bed receives the full cardiac output at low pressure (~25/10 mm Hg; mean ~15 mm Hg).
5. **Local V/Q regulation (Ch 31).** Ventilation and perfusion are not uniform across the lung (gravity-induced gradients; regional disease). Local feedback mechanisms — bronchoconstriction in poorly-perfused areas via low alveolar $P_{CO_2}$; **hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV)** in poorly-ventilated areas via low alveolar $P_{O_2}$ — minimize V/Q mismatch.
6. **Central control (Ch 32).** Unlike the heart, the respiratory rhythm is **not intrinsic to the effector organ**; it is generated by brainstem respiratory centers (pre-Bötzinger complex, dorsal and ventral respiratory groups in medulla; pontine respiratory group). Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies) sense arterial $P_{O_2}$, $P_{CO_2}$, and pH; central chemoreceptors (ventral medulla) sense CSF $P_{CO_2}$ / pH; these feed back to set respiratory rate and depth.

### Core claims — conducting vs. respiratory airways

- **Pleural sacs.** Each lung is invested by a visceral pleura (containing lymphatics that drain the pleural space) and surrounded by a parietal pleura (producing the ~10-mL ultrafiltrate that lubricates the space). Excess pleural-fluid production → **pleural effusion** → restricted lung expansion.
- **Lobes.** Right lung = upper + middle + lower; left lung = upper + lower (the lingula is part of the upper). Right lung ≈ 55% of total lung mass and function (less encumbered by the heart).
- **Generations.** Boron uses **23 generations** of dichotomous airway branching (Weibel scheme):
  - **Generations 0–10:** trachea → bronchi. Walls contain cartilage rings (C-shaped tracheal rings → plates lower down). Ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium with goblet cells and submucosal glands; serous + mucous secretions; subepithelial smooth muscle.
  - **Generations ~11–16:** **bronchioles**. No cartilage; held open only by radial traction from surrounding parenchyma + by being at a more negative outer (intrapleural) pressure than inner (luminal) pressure. The most distal cartilage-free conducting airway is the **terminal bronchiole (~generation 16)**.
  - **Generations 0–16 inclusive = conducting zone.** Total volume = the **anatomical dead space** $V_D \approx 150$ mL in young males (slightly less in females); does **not** participate in gas exchange.
  - **Generations 17–19:** **respiratory bronchioles**, partly alveolated.
  - **Generations 20–22:** **alveolar ducts**, fully alveolated.
  - **Generation 23:** **alveolar sacs**, terminal blind clusters of alveoli.
  - **Generations 17–23 = respiratory zone.**
- **Cross-section / velocity.** Tracheal $A \approx 2.5$ cm². Aggregate $A$ initially *decreases* slightly (the daughters of each parent do not quite double the cross-section), reaching a minimum near generation 3 — at which linear velocity is maximal (relevant for **cough mechanics**, Ch 32). From generation 3 onward $A$ rises, with a sharp inflection at generation 16 to alveolar values of ~600 cm² aggregate. At the terminal bronchiole, $A \approx 180$ cm² → linear velocity has fallen to ~1.4% of tracheal. Beyond generation 17 convection becomes negligible and **diffusion dominates** gas movement.
- **Right-mainstem-bronchus rule.** The right mainstem bronchus is wider and more nearly parallel to the trachea than the left; inhaled foreign bodies preferentially lodge in the right lung.

### Core claims — alveolar epithelium and pulmonary defense

- **Numerical anchors:** ~300 million alveoli; aggregate surface area 50–100 m²; aggregate alveolar volume 5–6 L (vs. ~0.15 L in the conducting airways); barrier thickness 0.15–0.30 μm.
- **Type I pneumocytes.** Squamous, extremely thin. Cover **90–95% of the alveolar surface**. The substrate for gas diffusion. Terminally differentiated; injured cells are replaced by proliferating type II cells.
- **Type II pneumocytes.** Cuboidal, granular. Located in clusters at alveolar corners. Approximately *equal in number* to type I cells but cover only 5–10% of surface area. **Two roles**: (i) synthesize and secrete **pulmonary surfactant** (a phospholipid-protein complex that drops alveolar surface tension; see Ch 27, pp. 613–614); (ii) **serve as progenitors / "repair cells"** for type I cells after alveolar injury.
- **Alveolar macrophages.** Free phagocytes resident on the alveolar surface; engulf inhaled particles that have escaped upper-airway filtration. Together with **interstitial macrophages**, they constitute the cellular innate immunity of the lower respiratory tract.
- **Pores of Kohn.** Small openings (N26-10) in the interalveolar septum that allow some collateral ventilation between abutting alveoli; their exact function is unclear.
- **Mucociliary escalator (defense pillar 1).** Goblet cells and submucosal glands of generations 0–10 secrete a mucus blanket; cilia beat the carpet upward toward the pharynx where it is swallowed. Particles >10 μm impact in the nose (turbinate turbulence + nasal hairs); 2–10 μm sediment in small airways; <0.5 μm are mostly exhaled as aerosols; the 0.5–2 μm window is the most penetrating.
- **Cough and sneeze reflexes (defense pillar 2; Ch 32 Box 32-4).** Airway irritation triggers a glottic-closure / explosive-expiration reflex that accelerates linear velocity in the central airways above the normal value (high $v$ generated by the small cross-section near generation 3 — Fig. 26-6) and clears trapped particles.
- **Phagocytosis and lymphatic drainage (defense pillar 3).** Alveolar and interstitial macrophages internalize particles; lymphatics in the visceral pleura and along the bronchovascular bundles carry phagocytes (and the antigens they have presented) to peribronchial and hilar lymph nodes.
- **Immunoglobulins (defense pillar 4).** Secretory IgA in upper-airway mucus; IgG in lower-airway fluid; surfactant proteins A and D act as innate opsonins.
- **Conditioning of inspired air.** The nose (turbinates, rich blood supply) warms inspired air to body temperature and saturates it with water vapor before it reaches the alveoli; this is energetically expensive but essential to keep alveolar gas at 37 °C and saturated, and to prevent alveolar desiccation.

### Core claims — dual blood supply

- **Pulmonary arteries.** Carry deoxygenated mixed-venous blood from the right ventricle alongside the bronchial tree. Bifurcate with each airway generation. Form alveolar capillaries that ensheathe each alveolus in a hexagonal mesh ("twisted ribbon" of blood flowing between abutting alveoli).
- **Pulmonary capillary anchors:** internal diameter ~8 μm; segment length ~10 μm; mean RBC transit time ~**0.75 s** (Ch 30 alveolar gas-exchange time budget); during one transit an erythrocyte may traverse up to **three alveoli**.
- **Bronchial arteries.** Branches of the **aorta**, carry freshly oxygenated systemic blood; supply the conducting airways down to the level of the respiratory bronchioles, where they anastomose with the pulmonary capillary bed.
- **Venous return.** Most bronchial blood drains *into pulmonary veins* — i.e., into oxygenated systemic arterial blood. Because the bronchial venous blood is partially deoxygenated, this constitutes a small **physiological right-to-left shunt** (~2% of CO; one of the reasons systemic arterial $P_{O_2}$ is slightly below alveolar $P_{O_2}$; Ch 31). A small remainder of bronchial venous blood drains into the azygos / hemiazygos veins back to the right heart.

## Section 3 — Nonrespiratory functions of the lung (pp. 600–601)

### Subsection heading (verbatim)
- **The lungs play important nonrespiratory roles, including filtering the blood, serving as a reservoir for the left ventricle, and performing several biochemical conversions** (pp. 600–601)

### Core claims
- **Olfaction.** Inspired air delivers odorants to the olfactory epithelium (Ch 15, pp. 354–356). Sniffing is the deliberate sampling subroutine.
- **Conditioning of inspired air (warming, humidifying, filtering).** The conducting airways trade metabolic cost (energy to warm and humidify air) for the benefit of delivering body-temperature, water-saturated gas to the alveolus. Nose breathing is more efficient than mouth breathing for all three functions; this is the basis of the deviated-septum / chronic mouth-breather problem clinically.
- **Particle filtering by size.**
  - **>15 μm:** filtered by nasal hairs.
  - **>10 μm:** impacted in the nasopharyngeal turbulence (right-angle turn into the trachea) and embedded in the mucus blanket; many lodge in the lymphoid-tissue-rich posterior pharynx (Waldeyer ring).
  - **2–10 μm:** sediment from the slowly moving air of small airways onto the mucus blanket and are removed by the mucociliary escalator.
  - **0.5–2 μm:** the most penetrating window; reach the alveoli.
  - **<0.5 μm:** ~80% expelled in the exhaled air as aerosols; the remainder reach the alveoli.
- **Alveolar particle handling.** Alveolar macrophages phagocytose; interstitial macrophages phagocytose; lung enzymes degrade; lymphatics drain to hilar nodes; particles in alveolar fluid can flow back up to the terminal bronchioles to re-enter the mucociliary escalator.
- **Cough and sneeze.** Reflexes triggered by airway irritation; covered in Ch 32 (Box 32-4).
- **Left-ventricular reservoir.** Pulmonary vessels of a 70-kg adult hold ~**440 mL** of blood (Table 19-2). This high-compliance, low-pressure pool buffers the filling of the left ventricle. Clamping the pulmonary artery of an experimental animal leaves enough blood in the pulmonary bed for the left heart to sustain ~**two beats** of cardiac output.
- **Embolus filter.** Mixed-venous blood routinely contains microscopic emboli (small clots, fat, air bubbles, even single cancer cells). The pulmonary microcirculation traps them before they reach the systemic circulation, where they could occlude end-arterial beds without collateral circulation (brain, kidney, heart). Trade-off: cancer emboli that lodge in pulmonary capillaries can seed **pulmonary metastases** — the lung is the most common site of distant metastasis for many primary tumors. Alveolar cells receive their own O₂ directly from alveolar gas and do not require capillary perfusion; therefore small pulmonary emboli rarely infarct the parenchyma, and the bronchial circulation provides a backup nutrient supply.
- **Biochemical conversions (Table 26-3).** The entire cardiac output passes once per beat over the ~70 m² of pulmonary endothelium. Selective endothelial activities: (i) hydrolyze **angiotensin I → angiotensin II by ACE** (a dipeptidyl-carboxypeptidase, gene *ACE*; Ch 40, p. 841); (ii) inactivate **bradykinin** (also via ACE — the basis of ACE-inhibitor side effects: dry cough, angioedema); (iii) clear **serotonin, PGE / PGF, leukotrienes**; (iv) **spare** histamine, catecholamines, ADH, oxytocin, angiotensin II, PGA, PGI₂.

- > "Although their main function is to exchange O₂ and CO₂ between the atmosphere and the blood, the lungs also play important roles that are not directly related to external respiration." (p. 600)
- > "Warming, moisturizing, and filtering are all more efficient with nose breathing than with mouth breathing." (p. 600)

### Connecting note — sleep apnea (preview)

Although not treated in detail in Ch 26, the sleep-apnea spectrum is set up by this chapter's anatomy and re-emerges in Ch 32 (control of ventilation). **Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)** results from collapse of the upper airway (oropharynx, hypopharynx) during sleep, when pharyngeal-dilator tone falls; the conducting-airway resistance rises sharply, alveolar ventilation falls, $P_aO_2$ falls and $P_aCO_2$ rises until a chemoreceptor-driven arousal restores tone. **Central sleep apnea** is a brainstem-controller defect (medullary respiratory groups, Ch 32). The chapter's "pathophysiology recapitulates phylogeny in reverse" rubric applies: in OSA the human transiently behaves like a small organism with a stagnant unstirred layer.

## Section 4 — Lung volumes and capacities (pp. 601–605)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The spirometer measures changes in lung volume** (pp. 601–602)
- **The volume of distribution of helium or nitrogen in the lung is an estimate of the RV** (pp. 602–604)
- **The plethysmograph, together with Boyle's law, is a tool for estimation of RV** (pp. 604–605)

### Core claims — spirometric volumes (Fig. 26-8)

A spirometer is a sealed inverted bell whose floor is water and into which the subject exhales; bell elevation tracks expired volume. Modern spirometers are electronic, but the four primary volumes and four primary capacities are unchanged. Because spirometer air is **ATPS** and alveolar air is **BTPS**, all volumes must be temperature-corrected (Box 26-3).

| Quantity | Definition | Typical adult range |
|---|---|---|
| **Tidal volume (V$_T$ or TV)** | Volume of air entering or leaving the lungs with each quiet breath | 0.4–0.5 L |
| **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** | Additional volume that can be inspired with maximal effort from end of a quiet inspiration | 1.9–2.5 L |
| **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** | Additional volume that can be expired with maximal effort from end of a quiet expiration | 1.1–1.5 L |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | Volume of air remaining in the lungs after a maximal expiration | 1.0–1.5 L (1.25–1.9 per text) |
| **Total lung capacity (TLC)** | IRV + TV + ERV + RV | 4.9–6.4 L |
| **Inspiratory capacity (IC)** | TV + IRV | 2.3–3.0 L |
| **Functional residual capacity (FRC)** | ERV + RV | 2.6–3.4 L |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | IRV + TV + ERV | 3.4–4.5 L |
| **Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁)** | Volume forcibly exhaled in the first second after a maximal inspiration; depends on VC *plus airway resistance* | ~80% of VC in healthy young adults |

- **FEV₁ / VC ratio** is the standard pulmonary-function-test index of airway obstruction (developed in Ch 27). Obstructive disease (asthma, COPD) reduces FEV₁ disproportionately to VC; restrictive disease (pulmonary fibrosis, kyphoscoliosis) reduces both proportionally.
- **Determinants of IRV.** Current lung volume; **lung compliance**; respiratory-muscle strength and innervation; comfort/pain; flexibility of the chest wall (kyphoscoliosis, arthritis); posture (lower recumbent).
- **Determinants of ERV.** Same list plus abdominal-muscle strength.
- **Total ventilation** = $V_T \cdot f$ (typically 0.5 L × 12/min ≈ 6 L/min BTPS).
- **Mass-balance asymmetry.** At rest $\dot V_{O_2} \approx 250$ mL/min, $\dot V_{CO_2} \approx 200$ mL/min, so the **respiratory exchange ratio** $R = \dot V_{CO_2} / \dot V_{O_2} \approx 0.8$. Inspired volume slightly exceeds expired volume; by convention, expired volume ($V_E$) is reported.
- **Why an RV exists.** Two reasons: (i) reopening a collapsed airway requires a much higher pressure than keeping it open, so a positive RV minimizes the work of subsequent inspirations; (ii) pulmonary blood flow is continuous but ventilation is episodic, so a positive RV maintains a steady alveolar-blood gas exchange and prevents $P_aO_2$ from oscillating widely between inspiration and expiration.

### Core claims — methods for measuring RV / FRC / TLC

A spirometer cannot measure any volume that includes RV (i.e., RV, FRC, or TLC) because RV never reaches the spirometer. Three indicator-dilution / gas-physics methods are used (Fig. 26-9):

#### 1. Helium-dilution (closed-circuit) method

- Use He because it is **poorly soluble** in body water → does not cross the alveolar–capillary barrier appreciably during the measurement.
- Start: spirometer + tubing contains a known $V_{S,\text{initial}}$ at known $[He]_{\text{initial}}$. Subject's lungs contain unknown volume $V_L$ (no He).
- Open the stopcock between mouth and spirometer at the **end of a quiet expiration** (to measure FRC) or **end of maximal expiration** (to measure RV). Subject breathes the circuit until $[He]$ equilibrates throughout the closed system.
- Conservation of He mass (Eq. 26-3):

$$[He]_{\text{initial}} \cdot V_S = [He]_{\text{final}} \cdot (V_S + V_L)$$

  Solve for $V_L$ (Eq. 26-4):

$$V_L = V_S \cdot \left( \frac{[He]_{\text{initial}}}{[He]_{\text{final}}} - 1 \right)$$

  Worked anchor (Eq. 26-5): $V_S = 2$ L, $[He]_{\text{initial}}=10\%$, $[He]_{\text{final}}=5\%$ → $V_L = 2(10/5 - 1) = 2$ L.

#### 2. Nitrogen-washout (open-circuit) method

- The lung initially holds $V_L$ of gas at the alveolar $N_2$ concentration of $[N_2]_{\text{initial}} \approx 75\%$ (room air at $P_B = 760$, minus $P_{H_2O} = 47$, minus alveolar $P_{O_2} \approx 100$, minus $P_{CO_2} \approx 40$, leaving $\sim 573$ mm Hg of N₂ → 75% of 760).
- Subject inhales **100% O₂** from a tank and exhales into a collection sack of initial volume 0. Each inspiration dilutes alveolar N₂; each expiration deposits the displaced N₂ into the sack. After ~**7 min** of breathing, essentially all N₂ has been washed out of the lungs into the sack.
- Conservation of N₂ mass (Eq. 26-6):

$$[N_2]_{\text{initial}} \cdot V_L = [N_2]_{\text{sack}} \cdot V_{\text{sack}}$$

  Solve for $V_L$ (Eq. 26-7):

$$V_L = V_{\text{sack}} \cdot \frac{[N_2]_{\text{sack}}}{[N_2]_{\text{initial}}}$$

  Worked anchor (Eq. 26-8): $V_{\text{sack}} = 40$ L (≈ 6 L/min × 7 min ventilation), $[N_2]_{\text{sack}}=3.75\%$, $[N_2]_{\text{initial}}=75\%$ → $V_L = 40 \cdot (3.75/75) = 2$ L.

  Begin at end-quiet-expiration → measures FRC. Begin at end-maximal-expiration → measures RV.

#### 3. Body plethysmography (Mead) — Boyle's law

- Subject sits in an airtight box ("plethysmograph") and breathes via a tube to the outside. An electronically controlled shutter can close the tube on command.
- At the end of a quiet expiration, mouth pressure = alveolar pressure = $P_B$, and lung volume = $V_L$ (FRC).
- Close the shutter; ask the subject to make a small inspiratory effort (~50 mL volume change) against the closed tube. Lung volume rises by $\Delta V_L$; alveolar pressure falls by $\Delta P$; the box volume falls by an equal $\Delta V_L$ (and box pressure rises). Lung gas is isothermal during the brief manoeuvre, so Boyle's law (Eq. 26-9 / 26-10):

$$P \cdot V_L = (P - \Delta P) \cdot (V_L + \Delta V_L)$$

  Solve for $V_L$ (Eq. 26-11):

$$V_L = \Delta V_L \cdot \frac{P - \Delta P}{\Delta P}$$

  Worked anchor (Eq. 26-12): $\Delta V_L = 50$ mL = 0.050 L; $\Delta P = 12$ mm Hg; $P = 760$ mm Hg → $V_L = 0.050 \cdot (748/12) \approx 3.1$ L = FRC.

- **Why plethysmography is the clinical gold standard.** Unlike the indicator-dilution methods, plethysmography measures **all gas in the thorax**, including gas trapped behind closed airways (e.g., in emphysematous bullae, in pneumothorax, behind mucus plugs in severe asthma). He-dilution and N₂-washout systematically *underestimate* TLC in obstructive disease because the indicator gas never reaches the trapped gas.

### Equations

- **He-dilution conservation** (Eq. 26-3):

$$[He]_{\text{initial}} \cdot V_S = [He]_{\text{final}} \cdot (V_S + V_L)$$

- **He-dilution solved for $V_L$** (Eq. 26-4):

$$V_L = V_S \cdot \left( \frac{[He]_{\text{initial}}}{[He]_{\text{final}}} - 1 \right)$$

- **N₂-washout conservation** (Eq. 26-6):

$$[N_2]_{\text{initial}} \cdot V_L = [N_2]_{\text{sack}} \cdot V_{\text{sack}}$$

- **N₂-washout solved for $V_L$** (Eq. 26-7):

$$V_L = V_{\text{sack}} \cdot \frac{[N_2]_{\text{sack}}}{[N_2]_{\text{initial}}}$$

- **Boyle's law** (Eq. 26-9):

$$P \cdot V_L = \text{constant} \quad (\text{at constant } T, n)$$

- **Plethysmography conservation** (Eq. 26-10):

$$P \cdot V_L = (P - \Delta P) \cdot (V_L + \Delta V_L)$$

- **Plethysmography solved for $V_L$** (Eq. 26-11):

$$V_L = \Delta V_L \cdot \frac{P - \Delta P}{\Delta P}$$

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **External respiration / internal respiration** — atmosphere↔mitochondria transport vs. mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.
- **Conducting zone / respiratory zone** — generations 0–16 (no gas exchange, anatomical dead space) vs. generations 17–23 (gas exchange).
- **Generation number** — Weibel index of dichotomous branching; 0 = trachea, 23 = alveolar sac.
- **Trachea, mainstem bronchi, lobar bronchi, segmental bronchi, bronchi, bronchiole, terminal bronchiole, respiratory bronchiole, alveolar duct, alveolar sac, alveolus** — anatomical names along the airway tree.
- **Anatomical dead space ($V_D$)** — total volume of the conducting airways; ~150 mL adult ♂.
- **Alveolar dead space** — alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused; Ch 31. Together with $V_D$, contributes to **physiological dead space**.
- **Primary lobule / terminal respiratory unit** — everything distal to one terminal bronchiole; the functional gas-exchange unit.
- **Type I pneumocyte** — squamous; 90–95% of alveolar surface; substrate of gas diffusion; cannot divide.
- **Type II pneumocyte** — cuboidal; produces surfactant; serves as progenitor for type I cells after injury.
- **Alveolar macrophage / interstitial macrophage** — innate phagocytes of the alveolus.
- **Pore of Kohn** — interalveolar septal pore; collateral ventilation; function unclear.
- **Mucociliary escalator** — coordinated upward sweep of mucus carpet by ciliated airway epithelium.
- **Goblet cell / submucosal gland / ciliated columnar cell / basal cell / club (Clara) cell** — cellular composition of airway epithelium.
- **Surfactant** — DPPC + protein mixture made by type II cells; lowers surface tension; Ch 27.
- **Pulmonary circulation** — low-pressure (mean ~15 mm Hg), low-resistance, high-flow circuit; receives the entire cardiac output.
- **Bronchial circulation** — branches of aorta; supplies airway walls; partial drainage into pulmonary veins creates the physiological shunt.
- **Pulmonary capillary** — ~8 μm diameter, ~10 μm long segments forming a hexagonal mesh; RBC transit ~0.75 s.
- **Physiological shunt** — admixture of partially deoxygenated blood (bronchial venous + Thebesian + V/Q mismatch components) into systemic arterial blood; ~2% of CO; Ch 31.
- **Visceral pleura / parietal pleura / pleural fluid / pleural effusion** — investments of the lung surface and disease state.
- **Air pump** — chest wall + respiratory muscles + upper airway; Ch 27.
- **Tidal volume ($V_T$ / TV) / IRV / ERV / RV / TLC / FRC / IC / VC / FEV₁** — spirometric volumes and capacities.
- **Respiratory frequency ($f$) / total ventilation ($\dot V_E = V_T \cdot f$) / alveolar ventilation ($\dot V_A$) / dead-space ventilation ($\dot V_D$)** — ventilation parameters.
- **Respiratory exchange ratio $R = \dot V_{CO_2}/\dot V_{O_2}$** — ~0.8 on a typical Western diet.
- **Partial pressure ($P$) and mole fraction ($X$)** — Dalton's law; $P_Z = X_Z \cdot P_{\text{total}}$.
- **Henry's law** — $[gas]_{\text{dis}} = s \cdot P_{\text{gas}}$.
- **BTPS / ATPS / STPD** — three volume conventions; lung volumes are BTPS; spirometer readings are ATPS; blood-gas volumes are STPD.
- **Helium-dilution method** — closed-circuit indicator-dilution measurement of FRC or RV; underestimates trapped gas.
- **Nitrogen-washout method** — open-circuit; same; same caveat.
- **Body plethysmography (Mead)** — Boyle's law method; measures total thoracic gas including trapped gas; clinical gold standard for TLC.
- **Boyle's law** — $P \cdot V = $ constant at constant $T$, $n$.
- **Pathophysiology recapitulates phylogeny in reverse** — failure of any respiratory tier makes the human behave like a more primitive organism.
- **Symbol conventions (Macklem 1985)** — $\dot V$ = ventilation rate; $\dot Q$ = blood flow; $P$ = pressure; subscripts $a/A/c/E/I/v/\bar v$ = arterial / alveolar / capillary / expired / inspired / venous / mixed venous.
- **Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)** — pulmonary endothelial dipeptidyl carboxypeptidase; converts angiotensin I → II; inactivates bradykinin; substrate of ACE-inhibitor drug class.
- **Sleep apnea (preview)** — obstructive (upper-airway collapse during sleep) vs. central (brainstem controller failure); Ch 32.
