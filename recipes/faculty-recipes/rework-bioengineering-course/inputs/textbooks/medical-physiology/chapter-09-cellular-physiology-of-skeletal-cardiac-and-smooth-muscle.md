## Section 1 — Skeletal muscle architecture (pp. 228–232)

### Subsection headings
- **Skeletal muscle is organized hierarchically from muscle to myofilament** (p. 228)
- **The sarcomere is the basic contractile unit** (pp. 228–230)
- **Thin and thick filaments are highly organized arrays of actin and myosin** (pp. 230–231)
- **Auxiliary proteins (titin, nebulin, α-actinin, desmin, dystrophin) maintain sarcomere geometry** (pp. 231–232)

### Core claims
- **Hierarchy**: whole muscle → fascicles → muscle fibers (single multinucleated cells) → myofibrils → sarcomeres → thick + thin filaments → myosin/actin/regulatory proteins.
- **Sarcomere** between adjacent Z-disks:
  - **A band**: full thick filaments + their overlap with thin filaments. Anisotropic.
  - **I band**: only thin filaments. Isotropic.
  - **H zone**: only thick filaments in the middle of the A band.
  - **M line**: midpoint of the A band, where thick filaments are linked transversely.
  - **Z disk**: anchor for the (+) ends of thin filaments; α-actinin cross-links them.
- **Thin filament**: F-actin double helix decorated with **tropomyosin** (covers myosin-binding sites in the resting state) and the **troponin complex** (TnT binds tropomyosin, TnI inhibits actin-myosin interaction, TnC is the Ca²⁺ sensor — 4 EF-hand sites; 2 high-affinity structural, 2 low-affinity regulatory).
- **Thick filament**: bundle of ~300 myosin II molecules with motor heads projecting laterally; antiparallel assembly at the M line.
- **Auxiliary proteins**:
  - **Titin** — spans Z-disk to M-line; acts as a giant molecular spring providing passive tension and centering thick filaments.
  - **Nebulin** — co-aligned with thin filament; sets thin-filament length.
  - **α-Actinin** — cross-links thin filaments at the Z-disk.
  - **Desmin** — intermediate filament linking adjacent Z-disks across myofibrils.
  - **Dystrophin** — links the sarcolemma to the cytoskeleton via the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex (DGC); mutations cause **Duchenne / Becker muscular dystrophy**.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Skeletal muscle is organized hierarchically from muscle to myofilament." (p. 228)
- > "The sarcomere is the basic contractile unit of striated muscle." (p. 229)
- > "Each thin filament consists of actin, tropomyosin, and the troponin complex." (p. 230)
- > "Titin spans half a sarcomere from the Z disk to the M line and acts as a molecular spring that contributes to passive tension." (p. 231)
- > "Dystrophin links the sarcolemma to the extracellular matrix via the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex; loss of dystrophin causes Duchenne muscular dystrophy." (p. 232)

## Section 2 — EC coupling in skeletal muscle (pp. 232–235)

### Subsection headings
- **The transverse tubule (T-tubule) system carries the action potential into the muscle fiber** (pp. 232–233)
- **The triad and the L-type Ca²⁺ channel (DHPR) / ryanodine receptor (RyR1) couple T-tubule depolarization to SR Ca²⁺ release** (pp. 233–234)
- **EC coupling in skeletal muscle is mechanical and does not require extracellular Ca²⁺** (p. 234)
- **SR Ca²⁺ reuptake by SERCA terminates contraction** (pp. 234–235)

### Core claims
- **T-tubule** = invagination of sarcolemma that runs transversely through the fiber, encountering each sarcomere at the A–I boundary. Brings the AP within tens of nm of the **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)** terminal cisternae.
- **Triad** = one T-tubule flanked by two SR terminal cisternae. The T-tubule membrane houses **Cav1.1 (DHPR)** in arrays of four, each tetrad apposed to one **RyR1** channel on the SR membrane.
- **Skeletal-type EC coupling (Fig. 9-3, viewed):**
  1. Sarcolemmal AP propagates down T-tubule.
  2. T-tubule depolarization moves the **voltage sensor** of Cav1.1.
  3. Cav1.1 acts as a **mechanical voltage sensor** — it transmits a direct protein-protein conformational coupling to RyR1.
  4. RyR1 opens → SR Ca²⁺ floods into cytoplasm → free $[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i$ rises from ~100 nM to ~10 μM.
  5. Cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ binds TnC → tropomyosin shifts → myosin-binding sites on actin exposed → cross-bridge cycling → contraction.
  6. Termination: **SERCA1** on the SR pumps Ca²⁺ back; **calsequestrin** in the SR lumen buffers stored Ca²⁺.
- Crucially, in skeletal muscle the **influx of extracellular Ca²⁺ is not required** for contraction; Cav1.1 acts as a voltage sensor, not as a Ca²⁺-source channel. Contrast with cardiac (next section).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In skeletal muscle, EC coupling is mechanical and does not require extracellular Ca²⁺ entry." (p. 234)
- > "The L-type Ca²⁺ channel of the T-tubule (DHPR) interacts directly with the SR Ca²⁺-release channel RyR1." (p. 234)
- > "SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR to terminate contraction; calsequestrin in the SR lumen buffers the stored Ca²⁺." (p. 234)

### Box 9-1 — Defective EC coupling in muscle (clinical anchor)
- **Hypokalemic periodic paralysis** — *CACNA1S* (Cav1.1) mutation. Loss of skeletal EC coupling when serum K⁺ falls.
- **Malignant hyperthermia** — *RYR1* (most common) and *CACNA1S* mutations. Triggered by volatile anesthetics and depolarizing NMJ blockers (succinylcholine) → uncontrolled SR Ca²⁺ release → hyper-metabolism, rigidity, hyperthermia, acidosis. Treatment: dantrolene (RyR1 inhibitor).
- **Central core disease** — *RYR1*; congenital myopathy; predisposes to malignant hyperthermia.

### Figures

#### Figure 9-3 — EC coupling in skeletal muscle *(viewed)*

Schematic cross-section of a **triad** at the A–I boundary. A T-tubule runs left–right with two SR terminal cisternae above and below. **L-type Ca²⁺ channels (Cav1.1)** in the T-tubule membrane are drawn as four-subunit tetrads facing **RyR1** channels in the SR membrane (RyR1 is a tetramer with a "foot" projection reaching across the junctional gap to engage the four DHPRs). ## Section 3 — The cross-bridge cycle (pp. 235–238)

### Subsection headings
- **The cross-bridge cycle is driven by ATP hydrolysis** (pp. 235–237)
- **Each cross-bridge transitions through structurally distinct states** (pp. 236–237)
- **Rigor mortis is a no-ATP state of the cross-bridge cycle** (p. 237)
- **Ca²⁺ and tropomyosin gate the cycle** (pp. 237–238)

### The four-step Lymn–Taylor cross-bridge cycle (compact reference)

| State | Bound nucleotide | Myosin–actin attachment | Lever arm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Rigor | none | strong | post-stroke |
| 2. ATP-bound | ATP | none (detached) | re-priming |
| 3. ATP-hydrolyzed | ADP·Pi | weak | pre-stroke (cocked) |
| 4. Power stroke | ADP (Pi released) | strong | mid-stroke (force generation) |

Steps:

1. **Rigor → ATP-bound**: ATP binding to the myosin head dissociates it from actin.
2. **ATP-bound → ATP-hydrolyzed**: hydrolysis "cocks" the head (lever-arm swing in the pre-stroke direction).
3. **ATP-hydrolyzed → Power stroke**: head re-binds actin (initially weak attachment); Pi release triggers the power stroke (lever-arm swing) and converts weak attachment to strong.
4. **Power stroke → Rigor**: ADP release leaves the head strongly bound (rigor state) until new ATP arrives.

In **rigor mortis**, postmortem ATP depletion locks cross-bridges in state 1.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The cross-bridge cycle is driven by ATP hydrolysis." (p. 235)
- > "Each ATP hydrolyzed produces one power stroke of about 5 to 10 nm." (p. 237)
- > "Ca²⁺ binding to troponin C displaces tropomyosin from the myosin-binding sites on actin, allowing cross-bridges to form." (p. 237)
- > "In rigor mortis, depletion of ATP after death leaves myosin heads strongly bound to actin." (p. 237)

## Section 4 — Mechanics of contraction (pp. 238–243)

### Subsection headings
- **The length–tension relationship reflects sarcomere geometry** (pp. 238–240)
- **Isometric vs. isotonic contraction: tension or length is held constant** (p. 240)
- **The force–velocity relationship is hyperbolic (Hill equation)** (pp. 240–242)
- **Power output is the product of force and velocity** (pp. 242–243)
- **At higher loads, velocity is lower because fewer cross-bridges act simultaneously** (p. 240)

### Equations

- **Hill force–velocity equation** for a contracting muscle:

  $$(P + a)\,(v + b) = (P_0 + a)\,b$$

  where $P$ is the load, $v$ is the shortening velocity, $P_0$ is isometric force ($v=0$), and $a$, $b$ are empirical constants with thermodynamic interpretations (Hill linked them to heat-of-shortening).

- **Power output**:

  $$\text{Power} = P \cdot v$$

  Maximum power occurs at intermediate load (~0.3 $P_0$); zero at $P=0$ ($v_{\max}$) and at $P=P_0$ ($v=0$).

- **Length–tension relationship** (Gordon, Huxley & Julian, 1966) reflects the geometry of thick/thin filament overlap. Maximum active tension at **optimum length $L_0$** corresponds to full overlap with no opposing thick-filament collisions. Tension falls at shorter lengths (thin-filament interference / thick filament–Z-disk compression) and at longer lengths (decreasing overlap).

### Core claims
- Total tension = active tension (contractile) + passive tension (titin elasticity, ECM).
- **Twitch** (single AP) generates a small brief tension. **Tetanus** (high-frequency stimulation that does not let Ca²⁺ fall between APs) produces sustained maximum tension.
- **Summation** and **unfused vs. fused tetanus** depend on stimulus frequency relative to Ca²⁺ removal kinetics.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Under isometric conditions, the velocity of shortening decreases as the applied load opposing contraction of the muscle fiber increases." (p. 240)
- > "Power is the product of force and velocity." (p. 240)
- > "The total measured tension is thus the sum of the passive and active components." (p. 240)

## Section 5 — Cardiac muscle (pp. 243–248)

### Subsection headings
- **Cardiac muscle is striated like skeletal but the cells are mononuclear and electrically coupled** (pp. 243–244)
- **Cardiac EC coupling uses Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release (CICR)** (pp. 244–246)
- **Cardiac contractility is graded** (pp. 246–247)
- **Length–tension and Frank–Starling at the cellular level** (pp. 247–248)

### Core claims
- **Cardiomyocyte features**: 1–2 nuclei per cell; cells joined at **intercalated disks** combining adherens junctions, desmosomes (mechanical), and gap junctions (electrical, Cx43) — making the ventricle an electrical syncytium.
- **Cardiac EC coupling (CICR)**:
  - Sarcolemmal AP → T-tubule depolarization → **Cav1.2** (L-type) opens → Ca²⁺ trickle enters cytoplasm.
  - This trigger Ca²⁺ binds **RyR2** on the SR → much larger SR Ca²⁺ release.
  - In contrast to skeletal, **extracellular Ca²⁺ entry is obligatory** (no mechanical Cav1.2-RyR2 coupling).
- **Cardiac contractility is graded**: each AP can produce different force depending on Ca²⁺ trigger size, SR load, and β-adrenergic phosphorylation status.
- **β-adrenergic modulation**: PKA phosphorylates **phospholamban** (relieving inhibition of SERCA2a → faster Ca²⁺ reuptake → faster relaxation), **troponin I** (decreasing Ca²⁺ affinity → faster relaxation), L-type Ca²⁺ channels (increasing open probability), and RyR2 (modulating release). Net effect: increased force (inotropy) and faster relaxation (lusitropy).
- **Length–tension in cardiac muscle** is steeper than skeletal: increasing sarcomere length both increases overlap *and* sensitizes the myofilaments to Ca²⁺ (length-dependent activation). This is the cellular basis of the **Frank–Starling law of the heart** (Ch 22).
- **Cardiomyocyte Ca²⁺ extrusion**: NCX (3 Na⁺/1 Ca²⁺) and PMCA in the sarcolemma; SERCA2a back into the SR. Quantitatively, SERCA accounts for ~70–80% of removal in human ventricle; NCX for ~20–25%.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "In cardiac muscle, EC coupling requires extracellular Ca²⁺ entry, which triggers Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release from the SR." (p. 244)
- > "The properties of cardiac cells vary with location." (p. 244)
- > "Cardiac contractility is graded, in contrast to skeletal muscle." (p. 246)
- > "β-adrenergic stimulation increases contractility by raising cAMP and activating PKA." (p. 247)

## Section 6 — Smooth muscle (pp. 248–253)

### Subsection headings
- **Smooth muscle lacks sarcomeric organization but uses the same actin–myosin chemistry** (pp. 248–249)
- **Contractile activation: myosin light-chain phosphorylation by MLCK** (pp. 249–250)
- **Ca²⁺ sources: extracellular entry (L-type, store-operated, ROCs) and SR release (RyR3, IP₃R)** (pp. 250–251)
- **Latch state: persistent force at low ATP cost** (pp. 251–252)
- **Pharmacomechanical coupling: GPCR signaling can drive contraction without membrane depolarization** (pp. 252–253)
- **Smooth muscle is single-unit (visceral, syncytial) or multi-unit (vascular, iris, ductus deferens)** (p. 253)

### Core claims
- **No sarcomeres**: actin and myosin filaments are arranged obliquely, anchored at dense bodies (analogous to Z-disks) and dense bands at the membrane. Filament shortening produces global cell shortening with little visible striation.
- **Activation switch is myosin, not actin**:
  - Cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin → Ca²⁺-CaM activates **MLCK** → MLCK phosphorylates the myosin **regulatory light chain (RLC)** at Ser19 → myosin ATPase becomes activatable → cross-bridge cycling.
  - Termination requires **MLCP** (myosin light-chain phosphatase) dephosphorylation of RLC.
- **Ca²⁺ sensitization**: GPCR pathways (especially Gα12/13 → RhoA → ROCK) phosphorylate the MYPT1 subunit of MLCP, *inhibiting* phosphatase activity → more phosphorylated myosin at the same [Ca²⁺]ᵢ. Basis for vasoactive drug pharmacology (e.g., Rho-kinase inhibitors).
- **Latch state**: dephosphorylated cross-bridges remain attached to actin but cycle very slowly. Allows sustained tonic contraction (sphincters, vascular tone) at a fraction of the ATP cost of actively cycling cross-bridges.
- **Pharmacomechanical vs. electromechanical coupling**: many smooth-muscle GPCR pathways (α1, AT1, ET-A, V1, M3) can elevate [Ca²⁺]ᵢ via IP₃R-mediated SR release without membrane depolarization. Conversely, hyperpolarization (e.g., by KATP openers) can relax smooth muscle without changing transmitter levels.
- **Innervation and coupling patterns**:
  - **Single-unit (visceral)**: extensively gap-junction-coupled; behaves as a syncytium; pacemaker activity. GI, ureter, uterus.
  - **Multi-unit**: each cell receives its own innervation; little coupling. Iris, ductus deferens, large airways, some vascular beds.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Smooth muscle lacks the sarcomeric organization of striated muscle but uses the same actomyosin chemistry." (p. 248)
- > "Contraction is activated by phosphorylation of the myosin regulatory light chain by Ca²⁺-calmodulin-activated myosin light-chain kinase." (p. 249)
- > "The latch state allows smooth muscle to sustain force with low energy expenditure." (p. 251)
- > "Smooth muscle can be activated by pharmacomechanical coupling without a change in membrane potential." (p. 252)

## Comparative summary — three muscle types

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Striation | yes | yes | no |
| Nuclei per cell | many | 1–2 | 1 |
| Electrical coupling | none | gap junctions (Cx43) | yes (single-unit) / no (multi-unit) |
| AP duration | ~1–2 ms | 200–400 ms (plateau) | variable |
| EC coupling | mechanical (DHPR ↔ RyR1) | CICR (Cav1.2 → RyR2) | IP₃R + Cav1.2 + RyR3 |
| Extracellular Ca²⁺ required | no | yes | yes |
| Regulatory switch | thin filament (Tn-C) | thin filament (Tn-C) | thick filament (MLCK phosphorylation of RLC) |
| Grading of force | recruitment + summation | graded per cell | continuous, GPCR-modulated |
| Speed of contraction | fast | intermediate | slow |
| Tetanus | yes | no (long refractory period) | sustained tonic + latch |
| Innervation | somatic NMJ | autonomic (modulatory) + intrinsic AP | autonomic + paracrine |
| Diseases (examples) | DMD/BMD, myasthenia, periodic paralyses, malignant hyperthermia | cardiomyopathies, channelopathies | hypertension, asthma, achalasia, urge incontinence |

## Equations summary (compact)

| Quantity | Equation |
|---|---|
| Cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ balance | $\frac{d[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i}{dt} = J_{\text{influx}} + J_{\text{release}} - J_{\text{SERCA}} - J_{\text{NCX}} - J_{\text{PMCA}} - J_{\text{buffer}}$ |
| Force vs. $[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i$ (Hill) | $F = F_{\max} \dfrac{[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i^n}{K^n + [\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i^n}$ |
| Cross-bridge ATPase | each cycle hydrolyzes 1 ATP; produces 5–10 nm step |
| Force–velocity (Hill) | $(P + a)(v + b) = (P_0 + a)\,b$ |
| Maximum power | at $\sim 0.3\,P_0$ load |
| MLC phosphorylation balance | $\dfrac{d[\text{MLC-P}]}{dt} = k_{\text{MLCK}}[\text{Ca-CaM}]\,[\text{MLC}] - k_{\text{MLCP}}\,[\text{MLC-P}]$ |

Numerical anchors: resting $[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i \approx 100\,\text{nM}$; active $[\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}]_i \approx 1{-}10\,\mu\text{M}$; sarcomere $L_0 \approx 2.2\,\mu\text{m}$; cardiac AP duration 200–400 ms; smooth-muscle tonic latch sustains force for minutes at <1% ATP cost of actively cycling cross-bridges.
