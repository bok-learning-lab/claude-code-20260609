## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **Biology of fetal growth** (pp. 1151–1155) — fetoplacental vs. uteroplacental circulations; placental gas/nutrient transfer; hyperplasia→hypertrophy; growth-controlling hormones (insulin, IGFs, T₄); fetal hematopoiesis and HbF; lipid/protein accretion in the third trimester.

4. **Neonatal physiology** (pp. 1164–1169) — nonshivering thermogenesis in brown fat; glucose/fat mobilization; fluid, acid-base, renal, GI, immune adaptations; risks of prematurity.

## Section 1 — Biology of fetal growth (pp. 1151–1155)

### Subsection headings
- **Two distinct circulations — fetoplacental and uteroplacental — underlie the transfer of gases and nutrients** (pp. 1151–1152)
- **Growth occurs by hyperplasia and hypertrophy** (pp. 1152–1153)
- **Growth depends primarily on genetic factors during the first half of gestation and on epigenetic factors thereafter** (pp. 1152–1153)
- **Increases in placental mass parallel periods of rapid fetal growth** (pp. 1153–1154)
- **Insulin, the insulin-like growth factors, and thyroxine stimulate fetal growth** (pp. 1153–1154)
- **Many fetal tissues produce red blood cells early in gestation** (p. 1154)
- **The fetal gastrointestinal and urinary systems excrete products into the amniotic fluid by midpregnancy** (pp. 1154–1155)
- **A surge in protein synthesis … is a major factor in rapid fetal weight gain during the third trimester** (p. 1155)
- **Fetal lipid stores increase rapidly during the third trimester** (p. 1155)

### Core claims
- **Two parallel circulations.** The *fetoplacental* circulation (umbilical arteries → villus capillaries → umbilical vein) faces the maternal *uteroplacental* circulation across the syncytiotrophoblast. Gas exchange is diffusive, driven by partial-pressure gradients, exactly as in the postnatal lung.
- **Placental O₂ transfer.** Uterine arterial blood enters at PO₂ ≈ 80–100 mm Hg; umbilical arterial blood arrives at PO₂ ≈ 20 mm Hg; effluent umbilical *venous* blood leaves at PO₂ ≈ 35–50 mm Hg. Uterine venous PO₂ stays 4–15 mm Hg higher — analogous to the alveolar–arterial PO₂ gradient.
- **Transfer mechanisms vary by solute:** O₂/CO₂ by diffusion; glucose by facilitated diffusion (GLUT1); amino acids by active transport then diffusion; IgG by receptor-mediated endocytosis; fluid by Starling forces.
- **Growth = hyperplasia then hypertrophy.** Three organ-specific phases: (1) pure hyperplasia, (2) hyperplasia + hypertrophy, (3) hypertrophy alone. The **placenta** grows mostly by hypertrophy and stops adding cells in the third trimester; the **fetus** grows almost entirely by hyperplasia (DNA content rises linearly from the early second trimester).
- **Genetic → epigenetic handoff.** First half of gestation: the fetal genetic program dominates. Second half: four epigenetic factors (placental, hormonal, environmental/maternal nutrition, metabolic/diabetes) become superimposed. The fertilized ovum (<1 ng) undergoes ~42 cell divisions to reach a ~3-kg newborn; only ~5 more divisions are needed to reach adult mass.
- **Growth hormones.** Glucocorticoids drive late-gestation hepatic glycogen storage; **insulin** acts as a fetal growth factor (its receptor knockout → mild growth restriction); **IGF-1 and IGF-2** are the key fetal mitogens (fetal IGF-2 > IGF-1, opposite of the adult pattern; both correlate poorly with GH in utero); **thyroid hormones** are obligatory; GH itself has limited fetal effect (few hepatic GHRs — hence anencephalic fetuses grow nearly normally).
- **Maternal diabetes:** sustained maternal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperglycemia → fetal hyperinsulinemia → **organomegaly and macrosomia**; severe maternal diabetes with placental vascular insufficiency can instead produce growth restriction.
- **Fetal hematopoiesis migrates:** yolk-sac islands (ends ~6 wk) → vessel endothelium/mesenchyme + liver (~4 wk on) → bone marrow/spleen by end of first trimester → bone marrow dominant by third trimester. RBCs start nucleated, become non-nucleated; reticulocyte fraction falls to ~5% at term (adult <1%); term fetal RBC life span ~80 days (≈⅔ adult).
- **Fetal hemoglobin (HbF, α₂γ₂)** predominates at birth; embryonic ζ/ε chains cease by 8 wk; HbA (α₂β₂) and HbA₂ (α₂δ₂) replace HbF over the first 12 months. Fetal [Hb] rises to ~15 g/dL by midgestation and ~17 g/dL at term (higher than the ~12 g/dL maternal level). The postnatal nadir of ~11 g/dL at ~2 months is the **physiological anemia of infancy** (iron-unresponsive).
- **Amniotic fluid.** The fetus swallows amniotic fluid by ~20 wk and excretes meconium continuously; **fetal urine ≈ 75% of amniotic-fluid production**. Final nephron number is reached by 34–36 wk, but renal regulatory function matures months after birth.
- **Third-trimester accretion.** Protein synthesis (mainly muscle and liver) rises 3–4× and consumes 15–20% of fetal metabolic energy (each peptide bond costs 4 ATP; ~0.86 kcal/g). Skeletal muscle accounts for 25–50% of weight gain in the second half of gestation. **Fat** rises from ~1% of body weight (first trimester) to ~15% at term — humans are unusually fat among newborns; brown fat supports later thermogenesis.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The uterine arterial blood enters the placenta with a PO₂ of 80 to 100 mm Hg, whereas umbilical arterial blood enters with a PO₂ of ~20 mm Hg, so that O₂ diffuses from the uterine to the umbilical circulation." (p. 1151)
- > "This difference is analogous to the alveolar-arterial PO₂ gradient." (p. 1151)
- > "In contrast to placental growth and development, growth of the fetus occurs almost entirely by hyperplasia." (p. 1152)
- > "During the first half of pregnancy, the fetus's own genetic program is the primary determinant of growth, and thus constrains patterns of growth." (p. 1152)
- > "In the fetus, both IGF-1 and IGF-2, which are mitogenic peptides, are extremely important for growth." (p. 1154)
- > "Programmed development governs increased synthesis of fetal Hb (HbF, α₂γ₂), which predominates at birth." (p. 1154)
- > "At birth, humans have more fat than other warm-blooded animals … with the exception of hibernating mammals and migratory birds." (p. 1155)

## Section 2 — Development and maturation of the cardiopulmonary system (pp. 1155–1159)

### Subsection headings
- **Fetal lungs develop by repetitive branching of both bronchial and pulmonary arterial trees** (pp. 1155–1156)
- **An increase in cortisol, with other hormones, triggers surfactant production in the third trimester** (p. 1156)
- **Fetal respiratory movements begin near the end of the first trimester but wane just before birth** (p. 1157)
- **The fetal circulation has four unique pathways — placenta, ductus venosus, foramen ovale, and ductus arteriosus — to facilitate gas and nutrient exchange** (pp. 1157–1158)

### Core claims — lung development
Two prerequisites for effective neonatal gas exchange: (1) structural branching that builds an extensive alveolar–capillary interface, and (2) **surfactant** to permit expansion without excessive effort. The lung begins as a foregut outpouching at ~24 days; the bronchial tree branches repetitively from 4–6 wk through four overlapping stages:

| Stage | Weeks | What forms |
|---|---|---|
| **Pseudoglandular** | 5–17 | Airways resemble branching exocrine glands; acinar buds in peripheral lung. Pulmonary arterial branching begins, paralleling bronchial branching. |
| **Canalicular** | 16–26 | Canalization complete at ~17 airway generations incl. respiratory bronchioles; each respiratory bronchiole → up to 6 alveolar ducts → primitive alveoli. At ~24 wk much interstitium still separates capillaries from epithelium. |
| **Saccular** | 24–38 | Respiratory epithelium thins; capillaries push into alveolar sacs; gas-exchange potential improves after ~24 wk as capillaries approach thin type I pneumocytes. Surfactant synthesis/storage **begins** (in type II cells). |
| **Alveolar** | late fetal → ~8 yr | Alveoli present at ~32 wk; 10–15% of adult alveolar number by 34–36 wk; alveolar number keeps rising until ~8 years. |

### Core claims — surfactant and lung fluid
- **Surfactant control is hormonal.** Glucocorticoid receptors appear in lung by midterm; fetal cortisol rises through the third trimester and surges just before birth (~⅔ fetal origin). Multiple hormones (glucocorticoids, thyroid hormones, TRH, prolactin, EGF) stimulate surfactant biosynthesis; **glucocorticoids** are essential — they raise the number of type II pneumocytes and lamellar bodies. The predominant surfactant phospholipid is **dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC)**. Net effect at ~32 wk: vastly increased surfactant → large rise in lung distensibility and stability on inflation.
- **Lecithin/sphingomyelin (L/S) ratio** (Box 57-2): an amniotic-fluid **L/S > 2.0** (with a positive phosphatidylglycerol assay) indicates mature lungs.
- **Fetal lung fluid.** In utero the alveoli/airways are filled with fluid at a volume approximating the neonatal FRC. Labor brings surges in catecholamines and arginine vasopressin that **stop fluid production and switch on active Na⁺ reabsorption via ENaCs**; reabsorbed fluid leaves chiefly via the pulmonary circulation (some via lymphatics); residual fluid is squeezed out through the trachea during vaginal passage.
- **Fetal breathing movements** begin near the end of the first trimester (promoted by hypoxia and tactile stimulation), occupy <½ of any 24-h period, and decrease just before labor; suppressing them experimentally limits lung growth.

### Core claims — the fetal circulation and preferential streaming
- The defining feature is the **placenta**, which performs in utero the jobs done postnatally by lungs (gas exchange), GI tract (nutrition), liver (nutrition/waste), and kidneys (fluid/electrolyte/waste).
- Because the right and left hearts pump **in parallel** (not in series) and their inputs/outputs mix, the chapter defines **combined cardiac output (CCO) = RV output + LV output**. In the fetus RV output (66% CCO) **exceeds** LV output (34% CCO).
- **The three shunts** route blood around organs that are nonfunctional in utero, and — critically — **preferentially stream the best-oxygenated blood to the heart and brain**:
  - **Ductus venosus** (first shunt): well-oxygenated umbilical venous blood (~50% CCO, SO₂ ~85%) bypasses the liver to join the IVC; ~69% of CCO (SO₂ ~70%) enters the right atrium.
  - **Foramen ovale** (second shunt, right→left): the valve-like septum streams ~27% of CCO from IVC straight into the **left atrium** (SO₂ ~70%), so the left heart and thus the coronary/cerebral/upper-body circulation receive the most oxygenated blood. SVC and coronary return is directed *across the tricuspid valve* to the RV instead.
  - **Ductus arteriosus** (third shunt, right→left): diverts most RV output from the pulmonary artery to the descending aorta. Its patency is maintained by **low PO₂** and **prostaglandins (esp. PGE₂)** — note this is *hypoxic vasodilation*, like most systemic vessels; fetal PGE₂ is much higher than adult.
- **High pulmonary vascular resistance** is the linchpin: only **~7% of CCO** perfuses the unventilated fetal lungs because of hypoxic vasoconstriction, acidosis, collapsed airways, and possibly leukotrienes (LTD₄). The remaining ~59% of CCO entering the pulmonary artery crosses the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta; ~50% of CCO ultimately perfuses the placenta, where SO₂ rises from ~60% back to ~85%.
- The RV in utero is therefore thick-walled with a large chamber → the newborn ECG shows large right ventricular vectors and **right-axis deviation**. Despite unequal stroke volumes, both ventricles pump at the **same pressure** because the low-resistance ductus arteriosus accepts ~90% of RV output.

### Equations
- **Flow-weighted mixing of O₂ saturation** at any point where two streams merge (Fig. 57-3B):

  $$ S_{O_2,\text{merged}} = \frac{(\dot Q \cdot S_{O_2})_{a} + (\dot Q \cdot S_{O_2})_{b}}{\dot Q_{a} + \dot Q_{b}} $$

  The resulting PO₂ is then read off the **HbF–O₂ dissociation curve** (left-shifted vs. HbA).
- **O₂-content advantage of fetal blood.** After equilibration across the placenta, fetal blood holds more O₂ than maternal blood because (1) **HbF has higher O₂ affinity than HbA** (left-shifted curve; HbF binds 2,3-BPG poorly) and (2) **fetal [Hb] is higher**. Schematically, content scales as

  $$ C_{O_2} \approx (1.34 \cdot [\text{Hb}] \cdot S_{O_2}) + (0.003 \cdot P_{O_2}) $$

  so both the higher [Hb] term and the higher SO₂ (from the left-shifted HbF curve at any given PO₂) raise fetal O₂ content.

- > "Glucocorticoids, in particular, play an essential role in stimulating fetal lung maturation by increasing the number of both type II pneumocytes and lamellar bodies." (p. 1156)
- > "The key principle governing the unique pattern of fetal blood flow is the presence of three shunts that allow blood to bypass future postnatal routes." (pp. 1157–1158)

- > "After O₂ equilibration across the placental membranes, the fetal blood has a higher O₂ content than maternal blood because (1) HbF has a higher O₂ affinity than does HbA, and (2) fetal blood has a higher Hb content than maternal blood." (p. 1158)

## Section 3 — Cardiopulmonary adjustments at birth (pp. 1159–1164)

### Subsection headings
- **Loss of the placental circulation requires the newborn to breathe on its own** (p. 1159)
- **Mild hypoxia and hypercapnia, as well as tactile stimuli and cold skin, trigger the first breath** (pp. 1159–1160)
- **At birth, removal of the placenta increases systemic vascular resistance, whereas lung expansion decreases pulmonary vascular resistance** (pp. 1160–1162)
- **Closure of the ductus venosus within the first days of life forces portal blood to perfuse the liver** (p. 1162)
- **Closure of the foramen ovale occurs as left atrial pressure begins to exceed right atrial pressure** (pp. 1162–1163)
- **Closure of the ductus arteriosus completes the separation between the pulmonary and systemic circulations** (pp. 1163–1164)

### Core claims
- **The first breath** is normally the hardest inspiration of a lifetime: overcoming surface tension may require a **transpulmonary pressure (P_TP) of ~60 cm H₂O to add only ~40 mL** of volume, versus an adult's ~2.5 cm H₂O for a 500-mL tidal breath. Once surfactant reaches the new air–water interface, the second breath may need only ~40 cm H₂O; adult-relative compliance may not be reached until ~1 h after birth.
- **Triggers of breathing:** transient hypoxia + hypercapnia from cord occlusion, tactile stimulation, and cold skin; sensed by peripheral and central chemoreceptors; sympathetic tone may sharpen the hypoxic signal at the carotid body. Severe asphyxia, however, *obtunds* rather than stimulates breathing → resuscitation needed.
- **Umbilical vasoconstriction** has two origins: mechanical stretch during delivery and the sudden rise in arterial PO₂. Umbilical *veins* do not constrict, allowing an **"autotransfusion"** of ~75–100 mL (substantial against the neonate's ~300 mL ≈ 90 mL/kg total blood volume) if the infant is held below the placenta with the cord unclamped.
- **The resistance crossover.** Removing the placenta (a major low-resistance parallel path, ~50% CCO) roughly **doubles total peripheral resistance**, raising aortic and LV systolic pressure. Simultaneously, lung expansion **drops pulmonary vascular resistance >5-fold** (Fig. 57-4) via (1) relief of mechanical compression, (2) rising alveolar PO₂ → vasodilation, (3) falling alveolar PCO₂, and (4) PGI₂ (prostacyclin) release. Pulmonary blood flow rises ~4-fold; PA and RV pressures fall abruptly, then keep declining over months (medial regression, new vessel growth, falling hematocrit/viscosity). The circulations convert from **parallel → series**.
- **Sequential shunt closure (functional, then anatomic):**
  - **Ductus venosus:** functional closure within days (smooth-muscle constriction, mechanism unknown) → portal pressure rises → portal blood now perfuses the liver.
  - **Foramen ovale:** increased pulmonary venous return raises **left** atrial pressure while falling descending-aortic flow lowers **right** atrial pressure; the reversed gradient pushes the septal flap shut (functional closure), with permanent anatomic closure over months. **~15–25% of adults retain a patent foramen ovale**, a route for right-to-left shunting in pulmonary hypertension or heavy weightlifting and for **paradoxical embolism / stroke**.
  - **Ductus arteriosus:** functional closure within hours from (1) the rise in arterial PO₂ from ~18–22 mm Hg in utero to ~60 mm Hg (O₂ now constricts the ductus), (2) falling circulating PGE₂ (placenta gone + increased pulmonary PGE₂ uptake), and (3) fewer ductal PGE₂ receptors. Flow ceases within ~1 week; the lumen obliterates anatomically within ~1 month (thrombosis, intimal thickening, smooth-muscle loss).
- **Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).** Common in preterm infants (reduced O₂ sensitivity, less smooth muscle). A persistently patent ductus allows a **left-to-right** shunt (aorta → pulmonary artery) → pulmonary overcirculation and systemic steal. Treatment: a **COX inhibitor such as indomethacin** (lowers vasodilator PGE₂) or surgical ligation. Conversely, in **duct-dependent congenital lesions** (Box 57-4), **infused PGE₂ keeps the ductus open** as a life-saving bridge.
- **Net oxygenation at birth:** despite some intrapulmonary shunt/V̇/Q̇ mismatch making neonatal arterial PO₂ lower than the adult's, the **left-shifted HbF curve lets the newborn reach an adult-like O₂ saturation** (Fig. 57-5B). The LV roughly doubles its stroke volume; the neonate's low systemic vascular resistance permits high flow at low perfusion pressure.

### Equations
- **Resistance change on losing the placenta** (qualitative, p. 1162): with descending-aortic flow nearly unchanged, doubling total peripheral resistance forces aortic/LV systolic pressure up:

  $$ R_{\text{TPR,postnatal}} \approx 2 \cdot R_{\text{TPR,fetal}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_{\text{aortic systolic}}\uparrow $$

- **Pulmonary resistance fall vs. flow** (Fig. 57-4 logic): mean PA pressure falls because the fractional **fall in PVR exceeds the rise in pulmonary blood flow**:

  $$ \bar P_{PA} = \dot Q_{\text{pulm}} \cdot \text{PVR}, \qquad \frac{\Delta \text{PVR}}{\text{PVR}} > \frac{\Delta \dot Q_{\text{pulm}}}{\dot Q_{\text{pulm}}} \;\Rightarrow\; \bar P_{PA}\downarrow $$

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The first breath is normally also the most difficult inspiration of a lifetime." (p. 1159)
- > "When the placental circulation disappears at birth, the total peripheral resistance doubles." (p. 1162)
- > "At birth, pulmonary vascular resistance abruptly decreases >5-fold with breathing." (p. 1162)
- > "As the pressure in the left atrium rises above the pressure in the right atrium — due to the large decrease in pulmonary vascular resistance — the flap of the foramen ovale pushes against the septum." (p. 1163, Fig. 57-5 caption)
- > "The relatively rapid functional closure of the ductus arteriosus after birth is primarily the result of an increased PO₂ of the arterial blood … a decrease in circulating PGE₂ … and a decrease in PGE₂ receptors in the ductus wall." (pp. 1163–1164)
- > "A patent ductus arteriosus allows a left-to-right shunt of blood from the aorta to the pulmonary artery because the aorta has a higher pressure." (p. 1164)
- > "In some newborns with congenital cardiac defects that block or severely decrease pulmonary blood flow, the ductus arteriosus may be the only route for getting blood to the lungs for gas exchange." (p. 1164)

## Section 4 — Neonatal physiology (pp. 1164–1169)

### Subsection headings
- **Although the newborn is prone to hypothermia, nonshivering thermogenesis in brown fat helps to keep the neonate warm** (pp. 1164–1166)
- **The neonate mobilizes glucose and FAs soon after delivery** (pp. 1166–1167)
- **Breast milk from a mother with a balanced diet satisfies all of the infant's nutritional requirements during the first several months of life** (pp. 1166–1167)
- **The neonate is at special risk of developing fluid and acid-base imbalances** (p. 1167)
- **Humoral and cellular immune responses begin at early stages of development in the fetus** (pp. 1167–1168)
- **In premature newborns, immaturity of organ systems and fragility of homeostatic mechanisms exacerbate postnatal risks** (p. 1168)

### Core claims — thermoregulation
- The newborn faces three metabolic challenges at birth: **hypoxia, hypoglycemia, hypothermia**. **Evaporation** dominates heat loss at delivery (wet skin from a warm liquid milieu into cool dry air); once dry, other routes (radiation, conduction, convection) take over.
- Predisposing factors to hypothermia: large surface-area-to-mass ratio (esp. the high-flow head), limited shivering capacity, poor adipose insulation, no behavioral adjustment.
- **Nonshivering thermogenesis** (liver, brain, **brown fat**) is the key defense. Cold stress → ↑TSH and ↑epinephrine. Brown fat is dense in mitochondria (cytochromes give its color) and concentrated in the neck and upper-back midline.
- **Mechanism (Fig. 57-6):** epinephrine (β/cAMP/PKA) activates lipase → frees fatty acids from triacylglycerols; locally, the 5′/3′-monodeiodinase converts T₄ → the more active **T₃**, which upregulates **uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1 / thermogenin)**, an H⁺ channel in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Normally purine nucleotides (ATP, GDP/GTP) block UCP1; the liberated **fatty acids relieve the block**, letting protons re-enter the matrix through UCP1 and **short-circuit the proton gradient** that ATP synthase would use — so oxidation is **uncoupled from phosphorylation** and the energy appears as **heat** (~27 kcal/kg/day).

### Core claims — fuel metabolism, fluids, immunity
- **Carbohydrate:** glucocorticoids build glycogen late in fetal life; after birth the neonate uses **glycogenolysis** (phosphorylase + glucose-6-phosphatase, active only after birth) to mobilize hepatic glycogen, which is depleted within ~12 h → risk of **hypoglycemia** if feeding is delayed. Falling glucose lowers insulin and raises glucagon → hepatic glucose release. Infants of diabetic mothers have islet β-cell hyperplasia and hyperinsulinism → severe postnatal hypoglycemia (<40 mg/dL).
- **Fat:** ~500 g (~15% body weight) stored in the last 2 months; glucagon + epinephrine activate **hormone-sensitive lipase** → glycerol (→ gluconeogenesis) + FAs (→ hepatic **ketone bodies**), important fuels as glycogen depletes.
- **Metabolic rate:** neonatal resting metabolic rate ~55 kcal/kg/day, nearly double the adult ~30; caloric requirement 100–120 kcal/kg/day at birth, falling to 90–100 by 1 year.
- **Nutrition/immunity:** colostrum then breast milk meet all needs early; both deliver **secretory IgA** and macrophages, and milk fosters protective lactobacilli. Hepatic iron stores last 6–9 months; **vitamin D** supplementation may be needed (rickets risk).
- **Fluid/acid-base:** newborns lose 6–10% of body weight in week 1 (interstitial/intravascular contraction). Total-body water ~75% of weight (vs. 60% men, 50% women); daily water turnover 100–120 mL/kg (3–4× adult). Renal immaturity: **GFR very low at birth**, rising over the first 2 weeks but not reaching adult levels until ~1 year (renal blood flow climbs from ~5% to 20% of cardiac output); maximal urine osmolality only ~450 mOsm (vs. ~1200 adult) due to short loops of Henle and low medullary urea. The high acid load + immature acid excretion → metabolic-acidosis risk.
- **Immunity:** placenta **actively transports IgG** (fetal IgG can exceed maternal); IgA/IgE/IgM cross poorly, so the newborn starts low in these. The fetus makes IgM to intrauterine infections, plus complement, lysozyme, and interferon-γ. Maternal IgG wanes to a nadir at ~3 months; even at 1 year the infant's Ig levels are ~half adult. Maternal antibody persistence varies (measles antibodies blunt vaccination before ~15 months; pertussis protection fades by 1–2 months — hence DTaP/polio start at 2 months).
- **Prematurity** (WHO: birth <37 wk vs. normal 40): nearly every neonatal challenge is worsened by prematurity or IUGR, reflecting organ immaturity (lungs, gut, liver, kidney) and fragile homeostasis (thermoregulation).

#### Figure 57-6 — Nonshivering thermogenesis in brown fat *(viewed)*
A brown-fat-cell schematic linking the endocrine trigger to mitochondrial uncoupling.
- **Top:** COLD STRESS → ↑TSH (→ thyroid gland → T₄) and ↑**epinephrine**.
- **Plasma membrane:** epinephrine → β-receptor/**G-protein/AC** → cAMP → **PKA (inactive → active)**. PKA activates **triacylglycerol lipase**, splitting triacylglycerols → **fatty acids**.
- **Nucleus arm:** T₄ → 5′-monodeiodinase → **T₃**, which (via THR/RXR on the TRE) drives transcription that **upregulates UCP** ("UCP, H⁺ channels … becomes part of the mitochondrial inner membrane").
- **Mitochondrion (lower panels), two states:**
  - *Left (blocked):* purine nucleotides (ATP, GTP) sit in the UCP channel; it is closed, so the electron-transport-chain H⁺ gradient is preserved and ATP synthase makes ATP.
  - *Right (uncoupled):* **fatty acids relieve the block and open UCP**; H⁺ short-circuits back into the matrix, dissipating the gradient set up by the electron-transport chain and producing **HEAT** in the matrix instead of ATP.

## Glossary
- **Fetoplacental vs. uteroplacental circulation** — the two parallel circulations meeting across the syncytiotrophoblast.
- **Combined cardiac output (CCO)** — RV + LV output (they pump in parallel); RV = 66%, LV = 34% in the fetus.
- **Ductus venosus / foramen ovale / ductus arteriosus** — the three fetal shunts; remnants = ligamentum venosum / fossa ovalis / ligamentum arteriosum.
- **Preferential streaming** — valve-like foramen ovale directs the most-oxygenated IVC blood to the left heart, coronaries, and brain.
- **HbF (α₂γ₂)** — fetal hemoglobin; left-shifted O₂ curve, poor 2,3-BPG (2,3-bisphosphoglycerate) binding → higher O₂ affinity than HbA.
- **High fetal pulmonary vascular resistance** — hypoxic vasoconstriction + collapsed airways + acidosis → only ~7% of CCO to the lungs.
- **Pseudoglandular / canalicular / saccular / alveolar** — the four lung-development stages.
- **Surfactant / DPPC / type II pneumocytes / lamellar bodies** — surface-tension-lowering apparatus; cortisol-driven in the third trimester.
- **L/S (lecithin/sphingomyelin) ratio** — amniotic-fluid lung-maturity index; >2.0 = mature.
- **Fetal lung fluid** — FRC-volume fluid; reabsorbed at birth via ENaC under catecholamine/AVP drive.
- **First breath** — needs ~60 cm H₂O transpulmonary pressure to overcome surface tension.
- **Functional vs. anatomic closure** — smooth-muscle constriction first, lumen obliteration later, for each shunt.
- **Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)** — persistent ductus → left-to-right shunt; treated with **indomethacin** (COX inhibitor); kept open with **PGE₂** in duct-dependent lesions.
- **Patent foramen ovale** — ~15–25% of adults; route for paradoxical embolism/stroke.
- **Nonshivering thermogenesis / brown adipose tissue / UCP1 (thermogenin)** — fatty-acid-disinhibited, T₃-upregulated mitochondrial proton uncoupling → heat.
- **Physiological anemia of infancy** — [Hb] nadir ~11 g/dL at ~2 months; iron-unresponsive.
- **Physiologic jaundice / bilirubin** — neonatal hepatic immaturity (see also Box 57-2 era apparatus; bilirubin handling noted in liver adaptation).
- **Infant respiratory distress syndrome (IRDS)** — surfactant deficiency of prematurity.
