# Chapter 24 — Special Circulations

> Section IV · The Cardiovascular System · pp. 556–571 · Author: Steven S. Segal
> This chapter applies the **four-mechanism local-control vocabulary** built in Ch 20 (neural, myogenic, metabolic, endothelial) and the **pressure-volume / cardiac-cycle** vocabulary of Ch 22 to the regional circulations of the brain, heart, skeletal muscle, splanchnic viscera, and skin. Each organ's circulation is a different *weighting* of the same four mechanisms: the brain is metabolic-dominant (autoregulated; tight CO₂ control); the coronary bed is metabolic-dominant + uniquely **diastolic-perfused**; skeletal muscle is metabolic-dominant with huge dynamic range and a **muscle pump**; the splanchnic bed is sympathetic-reservoir + postprandial hyperemia; the skin is **neural-dominant** in service of thermoregulation. Forward links: pulmonary (Ch 31), renal (Ch 34), placenta + fetal (Chs 56, 57).

## Chapter map (top-level)

1. **General introduction — the four control mechanisms recapped** (p. 556) — neural, myogenic, metabolic, endothelial mechanisms set vasomotor tone; their relative weights differ by organ. Mechanical extravascular compression (heart, skeletal muscle) is a non-regulatory factor that transiently halts flow.
2. **The brain** (pp. 557–559) — Circle of Willis collateralization; ~15% of resting CO supports ~2% of body mass; cerebral blood flow ≈ 50 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹; tight autoregulation between MAP ~70–150 mm Hg; metabolic control dominated by extracellular pH (CO₂/H⁺) with secondary roles for adenosine, K⁺, NO; weak direct neural control but powerful **neurovascular coupling** via astrocyte endfeet and conducted dilation along the endothelium; Cushing reflex.
3. **The heart (coronary circulation)** (pp. 560–563) — ~5% of resting CO; 60–80% O₂ extraction at rest → flow must rise to meet demand; **diastolic-predominant LV coronary perfusion** with brief early-systolic flow reversal; adenosine + Po₂/Pco₂/K⁺ + NO as metabolic dilators; sympathetic α-vasoconstriction is unmasked only with β-block; collateralization with gradual stenosis; **coronary steal** as a hazard of indiscriminate vasodilator therapy (Box 24-1).
4. **The skeletal muscle** (pp. 563–566) — 5–10 → 250 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹ (≥ 50× dynamic range); the **microvascular unit** (terminal arteriole + 15–20 capillaries < 1 mm long); **functional sympatholysis** (metabolic dilation overrides sympathetic constriction in active muscle); **conducted vasodilation** ascending the resistance network from terminal arterioles up to feed arteries; the **muscle pump** providing up to half the energy of circulation during heavy exercise; up to 80% of CO redirected to active muscle during whole-body exercise.
5. **The splanchnic organs** (pp. 566–569) — celiac + SMA + IMA; arcaded collaterals; villus countercurrent O₂ exchange at low flow; **postprandial hyperemia** up to 8× resting (cephalic, metabolic, hyperosmolar, hormonal/CCK/kinins); sympathetic vasoconstriction as the master reservoir-mobilization switch; hepatic dual blood supply (portal vein 75%, hepatic artery 25%); reciprocal arterial-buffer response; **portal hypertension** and ascites (Box 24-2).
6. **The skin** (pp. 570–571) — overperfused relative to nutritional need; primary role is **thermoregulation**; **apical skin** (ears, nose, fingers, toes) has glomus-body AV anastomoses under sympathetic noradrenergic control (only active vasoconstriction; passive vasodilation on withdrawal of tone); **nonapical skin** has dual sympathetic noradrenergic vasoconstrictor + sympathetic cholinergic-active vasodilator innervation (the cholinergic system co-activates sweat glands → kallikrein → bradykinin); local axon-reflex responses (white reaction → red reaction → flare → wheal, "triple response").

## Section 1 — Introduction; the four mechanisms recapped (p. 556)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The blood flow to individual organs must vary to meet the needs of the particular organ, as well as of the whole body** (p. 556)
- **Neural, myogenic, metabolic, and endothelial mechanisms control regional blood flow** (pp. 556–557)

### Core claims
- Each organ has its own **weighting** of the four control mechanisms; the chapter's organizing question is which mechanism dominates in each bed, and why.
- During **whole-body exercise**, the redistribution of cardiac output reads off the organ weights: active skeletal muscle ↑ (metabolic + muscle pump beat sympathetic constriction in active muscle); skin ↑ (thermoregulation); splanchnic + renal + inactive muscle ↓ (sympathetic vasoconstriction); brain and heart **preserved** (autoregulation + metabolic dilation).
- The four mechanisms (built in Ch 20 §5):
  - **Neural** — autonomic fibers, mainly sympathetic, invest the resistance vessels of nearly every organ. Apart from regulating systemic BP and CO, they redistribute local flow.
  - **Myogenic (Bayliss)** — VSMCs constrict in response to stretch and dilate in response to reduced transmural pressure; the structural basis of organ-level **autoregulation** in brain, heart, skeletal muscle, kidney.
  - **Metabolic** — local ↓Po₂, ↑Pco₂, ↓pH, ↑[K⁺]ₒ, ↑adenosine, ↑lactate, ↑ATP/ADP → vasodilation (Ch 20 Table 20-9). Dominant in tissues with high energy demand.
  - **Endothelial** — shear-stress-evoked NO release, plus a portfolio of EDRF/EDHF/PGI₂ and ET/TXA₂/EDCF (Ch 20 Table 20-10); endothelium ↔ VSMC gap junctions for conducted signaling.
- **Mechanical (non-regulatory) factors** — extravascular compression by contracting tissue transiently halts flow (heart systole; skeletal muscle contraction).

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "Several mechanisms govern vascular resistance and thus the distribution of blood circulating throughout the body." (p. 556)
- > "The extent to which a particular vascular bed depends on a particular blood-flow-control mechanism varies from organ to organ." (p. 556)
- > "Tissues with high energy demands—such as the brain, heart, and skeletal muscle during exercise—rely heavily on such local control mechanisms." (p. 556)
- > "Endothelial cells and VSMCs also use gap junctions for electrical and chemical signaling between themselves, thereby coordinating their activity during blood flow control." (p. 556)
- > "In the heart and skeletal muscle, muscle contraction transiently halts blood flow by compressing blood vessels within the tissue." (p. 557)

## Section 2 — The brain (pp. 557–559)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **Anastomoses at the circle of Willis and among the branches of distributing arteries protect the blood supply to the brain, which is ~15% of resting cardiac output** (p. 557)
- **Neural, metabolic, and myogenic mechanisms control blood flow to the brain** (pp. 558–559)
- **The neurovascular unit matches blood flow to local brain activity** (p. 559)
- **Autoregulation maintains a fairly constant cerebral blood flow across a broad range of perfusion pressures** (p. 559)

### Core claims

#### Anatomy and the protective architecture
- **Mass / flow disproportion.** Brain ~2% of body mass receives ~15% of resting CO; entirely **oxidative**, no anaerobic reserve; ~100 g/day glucose oxidized.
- **Source arteries** — paired **internal carotids** + paired **vertebrals**; the vertebrals fuse to the **basilar**; both feed the **Circle of Willis** at the base of the brain, from which the **anterior / middle / posterior cerebral arteries** envelop the hemispheres. The carotid pair supplies the bulk; the Circle provides **collateral cross-flow** when one source narrows.
- **Distributing → pial → penetrating arterioles → capillaries.** Pial-surface anastomoses provide the second-level collateral. Penetrating arterioles enter the cortex at right angles.
- **Veins**: thin-walled, no valves, near-zero VSMC; radial centrifugal drainage to a **superficial pial venous plexus** → **dural sinuses** → **internal jugular vein**. The deep white matter and basal ganglia drain centripetally to a separate deep venous system.
- **Capillaries: the blood-brain barrier (BBB)** (Ch 11). Tight junctions exclude paracellular flux of polar solutes and proteins; only water, O₂, CO₂, and other gases cross freely; glucose enters by facilitated diffusion (GLUT1). Drugs designed for other organs may have **no CNS effect** by virtue of the BBB. **Circumventricular organs** (Ch 11) are the exception: fenestrated, "intestinal-like" capillaries permit sensing of plasma composition.
- **Lymphatics: the brain has none.** Implication: interstitial fluid management depends on glymphatic-like astrocytic clearance and CSF circulation, not on a lymphatic loop.
- **Fixed-volume cranium.** Because the skull is rigid, any **regional vasodilation must be compensated by reciprocal volume changes elsewhere** within the cranium. Cerebral edema, hemorrhage, or tumor → raised intracranial pressure → compression of vasculature → ischemia; the **Cushing reflex** (below) is the systemic-pressure response.

#### Control of cerebral blood flow
- **Cerebral blood flow (CBF) ≈ 50 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹**, held nearly constant by autoregulation.
- **Neural control is weak.** Sympathetic fibers from superior cervical ganglia travel along the carotid + vertebral arteries; release NE; cause modest constriction. Parasympathetic fibers from the facial nerve cause modest dilation. **Sensory C-fibers** in the trigeminal ganglia carry substance P and CGRP — local stimulation triggers an **axon reflex** vasodilation.
- **Metabolic control is dominant.**
  - **CO₂ / extracellular pH is the principal stimulus.** Cerebral VSMCs are exquisitely sensitive to **decreased BECF pH** (brain extracellular fluid pH) but insensitive to Pco₂ per se and only weakly responsive to intracellular pH (which actually causes weak vasoconstriction).
  - **Respiratory acidosis** (↑arterial Pco₂; Ch 28) rapidly crosses the BBB → BECF pH drops → marked dilation. Breathing 7% CO₂ can **double CBF**.
  - **Hyperventilation** drops arterial Pco₂ → BECF pH rises → vasoconstriction → dizziness; **clinically used to lower CBF in acute cerebral edema and glaucoma**.
  - **Metabolic acidosis** (low arterial pH at constant Pco₂) has **little effect on CBF**, because H⁺ cannot cross the BBB.
  - **Hypoxia** (↓Po₂) dilates cerebral vessels but less dramatically than hypercapnia; effects are direct on VSMCs or mediated by **adenosine**, **K⁺**, or **NO** released into the BECF.
  - **Local activity → ATP hydrolysis → adenosine release** (potent vasodilator).
- **Myogenic control** — penetrating arterioles constrict to ↑ transmural pressure and dilate to ↓ transmural pressure; this is the substrate of autoregulation.

#### The neurovascular unit and neurovascular coupling
- **The neurovascular unit = neurons + astrocytes + vasculature** functioning as an integrated module to distribute flow to active regions.
- **Astrocyte endfeet** wrap penetrating arteriolar VSMCs and capillary endothelium. Neuronal release of **glutamate** and **GABA** triggers **[Ca²⁺]ᵢ waves** in astrocytes and dendrites; these waves release **NO** and **arachidonic-acid metabolites** that dilate the local arteriole.
- **Conducted vasodilation.** The dilator signal **propagates through gap junctions** along the endothelium and VSMCs of penetrating arterioles **retrograde to the pial arteries**, where the bulk of cerebral vascular resistance sits. Reducing proximal resistance directs the increased flow to the active region (see Fig. 24-2). This is the mechanism behind the regional flow patterns visualized by ¹³³Xe washout (Ingvar; Fig. 24-2) and by modern fMRI BOLD imaging.

#### Autoregulation and the Cushing reflex
- **Autoregulation range** — CBF is nearly constant over **mean arterial pressures ~70 to 150 mm Hg** (Ch 20). Below ~70 mm Hg, CBF falls and risks ischemia; above ~150 mm Hg, the autoregulation breaks and CBF rises with pressure (hypertensive encephalopathy is the corresponding clinical syndrome).
- In **chronic hypertension** the autoregulation range shifts rightward; rapid normalization of BP can therefore precipitate hypoperfusion.
- **Cushing reflex.** Rising intracranial pressure (head trauma, tumor) compresses the cerebral vasculature → local ischemia → **medullary vasomotor center activation** → systemic sympathetic outflow → ↑ MAP. Over a considerable range, the rise in perfusion pressure offsets the vascular compression and **maintains CBF**.

### Equations

- **Whole-organ Fick principle for cerebral blood flow** (carried from Ch 17 / Ch 20):

$$F_{\text{CBF}} = \frac{\dot Q_{O_2,\text{brain}}}{[O_2]_a - [O_2]_{v,\text{jugular}}}$$

  Because the brain is **obligately oxidative**, $\dot Q_{O_2}$ is essentially constant minute-to-minute → CBF and a–v O₂ difference move reciprocally as the autoregulation operates.

- **Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP)** (clinical):

$$\text{CPP} = \text{MAP} - \text{ICP}$$

  Autoregulation operates on **CPP**, not MAP alone; this is why raised ICP is dangerous even at normal systemic pressure.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The brain accounts for only ~2% of the body's weight, yet it receives ~15% of the resting cardiac output. Of all the organs in the body, the brain is the least tolerant of ischemia." (p. 557)
- > "One of the most characteristic features of the brain vasculature is the blood-brain barrier, which prevents the solutes in the lumen of the capillaries from having direct access to the brain extracellular fluid." (p. 558)
- > "Cerebral VSMCs relax mainly in response to low extracellular pH; these cells are insensitive to increased Pco₂ per se, and decreased intracellular pH actually causes a weak vasoconstriction." (p. 558)
- > "The rise in arterial Pco₂ caused by inhalation of 7% CO₂ can cause cerebral blood flow to double." (p. 558)
- > "Despite this innervation, neural control of the cerebral vasculature is relatively weak. Instead, it is the local metabolic requirements of the brain cells that primarily govern vasomotor activity in the brain." (p. 559)
- > "The local control of cerebral blood flow maintains a nearly constant blood flow through perfusion pressures ranging from ~70 to 150 mm Hg." (p. 559)
- > "The Cushing reflex, an increase in arterial pressure that occurs in response to an increase in intracranial pressure." (p. 559)

## Section 3 — The heart (coronary circulation) (pp. 560–563)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The coronary circulation receives 5% of the resting cardiac output from the left heart and mostly returns it to the right heart** (pp. 560–561)
- **Extravascular compression impairs coronary blood flow during systole** (pp. 561–562)
- **Myocardial blood flow parallels myocardial metabolism** (p. 562)
- **Although sympathetic stimulation directly constricts coronary vessels, accompanying metabolic effects predominate, producing an overall vasodilation** (p. 563)
- **Collateral vessel growth can provide blood flow to ischemic regions** (p. 563)
- **Vasodilator drugs may compromise myocardial flow through "coronary steal"** (p. 563)

### Core claims

#### Anatomy
- **5% of resting CO** to ~0.5% of body mass.
- **Substrates.** Healthy fasting heart oxidizes mostly **fatty acids (> 60% of O₂ consumption)**, plus **carbohydrate (≤ 40%)**, **ketone bodies** (starvation, DKA), and **lactate/pyruvate** when O₂ supply is adequate. Under O₂ deficit, the heart **releases lactate** from its own glycogen, sustaining ATP briefly. Persistent supply-demand mismatch → **angina pectoris** (nociceptive referred pain) → **myocardial infarction** if uncorrected.
- **Coronary arteries** arise from the **aortic root behind the cusps of the aortic valve**. The **right coronary** supplies right atrium + right ventricle; the **left coronary** divides into the **left circumflex** (left atrium + LV) and the **left anterior descending (LAD)** (interventricular septum + apex + parts of both ventricles).
- **Capillary density** in human myocardium **> 3000/mm²** — roughly **8× skeletal-muscle density** (~400/mm²); small fiber diameter (< 20 µm, vs. ~50 µm in skeletal muscle) shortens diffusion distance. Both features serve the heart's high obligate O₂ consumption.
- **Venous drainage.** Most venous blood collects in epicardial veins → **coronary sinus** → right atrium. Some drains via **thebesian veins** directly into the cardiac chambers (a small physiological right-to-left shunt that bypasses the pulmonary circulation).
- **Collaterals.** Numerous interarterial and venous anastomoses provide alternative routes when a primary vessel narrows.

#### Diastolic-predominant coronary perfusion (the central concept; Fig. 24-4)
- The heart **is its own perfusion source** *and* its own perfusion **obstacle**: contraction compresses the very vessels that supply it.
- **Left coronary phasic flow** (Fig. 24-4, middle panel) shows the textbook paradox:
  - **Early systole (isovolumetric contraction)**: LV contraction compresses left coronary vessels; aortic pressure has not yet risen (aortic valve still closed) → **flow briefly reverses**.
  - **Later systole** (ejection): aortic pressure rises and partly overcomes extravascular compression; flow becomes positive but does not reach peak.
  - **Early diastole**: ventricle relaxes → extravascular compression released; **aortic pressure is still high** → coronary flow **rockets to its maximum**.
  - **Late diastole**: aortic pressure falls along its diastolic runoff; coronary flow falls in parallel.
- **~80% of total left coronary flow occurs in diastole.** The corollary is that **shortening diastole (tachycardia)** disproportionately curtails LV perfusion — dangerous only when coronary stenosis is present, otherwise offset by metabolic dilation.
- **Right coronary phasic flow** (Fig. 24-4, lower panel) tracks the aortic-pressure trace: RV wall tension is much lower (RV pressure ~25 mm Hg vs. LV ~130 mm Hg), so extravascular compression does not occlude right coronary vessels and **no flow reversal occurs**; systole contributes a larger fraction of total right coronary flow.
- **Endo/epi gradient.** During systole, intramural pressure is **highest near the endocardium** and lowest near the epicardium. To compensate, **endocardial vessels have lower intrinsic resistance** (more vasodilator tone at rest, more reserve in diastole). When aortic diastolic pressure drops (aortic regurgitation) or coronary resistance rises (atherosclerosis), the **endocardium is preferentially injured** — the textbook substrate for "subendocardial ischemia / infarction".

#### Metabolic coupling — the dominant control
- The heart already extracts **70–80% of arterial O₂** at rest (arterial ~20 mL O₂/dL → venous ~5 mL/dL). It has **almost no extraction reserve** — meeting increased demand requires **increased flow**.
- Resting coronary flow ≈ **60–70 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹**; in exercise this can rise to **> 250 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹** (~4×).
- Because systemic BP varies within narrow limits, this rise comes from **vasodilation**, not perfusion-pressure increase.
- **Principal mediators:**
  - **Adenosine** — released on ATP breakdown when O₂ supply lags demand; binds purinergic (A₂) receptors on coronary VSMCs → lowers [Ca²⁺]ᵢ → vasodilation. Acts as a **negative-feedback Po₂ sensor**.
  - **K⁺** — myocyte AP repolarization releases K⁺ that may contribute to the **initial** rise in coronary perfusion; not the sustained mediator.
  - **CO₂ / H⁺** — rises when O₂ demand outstrips supply; contributes to vasodilation (analogous to BBB cerebral mechanism but without the BBB-imposed restriction).
  - **NO** (endothelial) — shear-stress-evoked; sustains basal dilator tone.
- **Autoregulation range** — coronary flow is constant from MAP ~70 to > 150 mm Hg (similar to cerebral). Substrate: myogenic + adenosine/Po₂ tuning.

#### Sympathetic control — vasodilation by metabolic proxy
- Sympathetic stimulation has **two competing effects** on coronary tone:
  1. **Direct α-vasoconstriction** on VSMCs.
  2. **Indirect metabolic vasodilation** via β1 effects (↑HR + ↑contractility → ↑O₂ demand → ↑adenosine, etc.).
- Net effect under physiological conditions: **vasodilation**. The α-vasoconstriction is **unmasked only when β1 receptors are pharmacologically blocked** (because then ↑HR/↑contractility no longer drives metabolic dilation).
- **Vagal acetylcholine** has only modest effect on coronary tone — not because the vessels are insensitive (direct ACh perfusion dilates) but because vagal ACh release is **anatomically restricted to the vicinity of the SA node**. Hence the dominant vagal effect is chronotropic, not coronary.

#### Collateral growth
- Acute occlusion → infarct. **Gradual stenosis** lets new collateral vessels develop by remodeling of existing branches (endothelial + smooth-muscle proliferation), driven by **angiogenic factors** (Ch 20) released from ischemic tissue and by altered mechanical wall stress.

#### Coronary steal — a pharmacological hazard
- In atherosclerotic coronary disease, downstream microvessels distal to a stenosis are **already maximally dilated** by local ischemic metabolites.
- Giving a systemic vasodilator dilates only the **non-ischemic parallel beds**; this **drops the pressure at the upstream branch point**, reducing the driving pressure across the stenosis → flow to the ischemic region **falls**.
- Implication: in angina, the benefit of nitrates et al. **comes mostly from systemic preload/afterload reduction (↓ wall-tension cost; cf. Ch 22 tension-time integral)**, not from selective coronary dilation.

### Equations

- **Cardiac extraction-reserve identity** (rearranged whole-organ Fick):

$$E_{O_2} = \frac{[O_2]_a - [O_2]_v}{[O_2]_a} \approx 0.7\text{–}0.8 \quad (\text{at rest})$$

  Because $E_{O_2}$ is near its ceiling, $\Delta\dot Q_{O_2}$ at the heart **requires $\Delta F_{\text{coronary}}$**, not $\Delta E$. This is the central physiological reason for the heart's intense metabolic vasoregulation.

- **Cycle-time-weighted left coronary flow** (conceptual; Fig. 24-4):

$$\bar F_{\text{LC}} \approx \frac{t_{\text{diastole}} \cdot \bar F_{\text{diastole}} + t_{\text{systole}} \cdot \bar F_{\text{systole}}}{t_{\text{diastole}} + t_{\text{systole}}}$$

  with $\bar F_{\text{diastole}} \gg \bar F_{\text{systole}}$ → tachycardia (↓$t_{\text{diastole}}$) preferentially curtails left coronary perfusion.

- **Endo/epi balance condition** (qualitative Laplace argument):

$$P_{\text{intramural}}(r) \approx P_{LV}\cdot\frac{r_{\text{endo}}}{r}$$

  Intramural pressure is highest near the endocardium, where vascular resistance is therefore set lowest at baseline to balance perfusion.

### Citation-anchor quotes
- > "The heart receives ~5% of the resting cardiac output, although it represents <0.5% of total body weight." (p. 560)
- > "More than 60% of myocardial O₂ consumption in the fasting state is due to the oxidation of fatty acids." (p. 560)
- > "In the coronary circulation, flow is somewhat paradoxical: Although the heart is the source of its own perfusion pressure, myocardial contraction effectively compresses the heart's own vascular supply." (pp. 561–562)
- > "All told, ~80% of total left coronary blood flow occurs during diastole." (p. 562)
- > "The heart can meet large increases in O₂ demand only by increasing coronary blood flow, which can exceed 250 mL/min per 100 g with exercise." (p. 562)
- > "The heart relies primarily on metabolic mechanisms to increase the caliber of its coronary vessels. Adenosine has received particular emphasis in this regard." (p. 562)
- > "Coronary blood flow is relatively stable between perfusion pressures of ~70 mm Hg and >150 mm Hg." (p. 562)
- > "Blocking of β1 receptors 'unmasks' adrenergic vasoconstriction." (p. 563)
- > "Administration of a vasodilator can then increase the diameter of blood vessels only in nonischemic vascular beds that are parallel to the ischemic ones … The result is coronary steal." (p. 563)

Stacked four-trace plot vs. time over slightly more than one cardiac cycle (color-banded as **Diastole → Systole → Diastole**, with narrow bands at the boundaries flagged as **isovolumetric contraction** and **isovolumetric relaxation**). **Trace 1 — Aortic pressure (mm Hg, 0–120):** the familiar arterial pulse: ~80 mm Hg at end-diastole, rapid rise during ejection to ~120 mm Hg, dicrotic notch at aortic-valve closure, slow diastolic runoff. **Trace 2 — Left coronary blood flow (mL/min, 0–~400):** **near-zero or briefly negative** during isovolumetric contraction (extravascular compression beats the not-yet-risen aortic pressure → **transient flow reversal**), **modest** during ejection (aortic pressure has risen but myocardial compression is still substantial), and then **rockets to its peak** in early diastole (compression released; aortic pressure still high). > Vision note: This is the chapter's signature figure. Reading it teaches three things at once: (i) the heart is **not perfused like other organs** — it is perfused mostly **between contractions**; (ii) **tachycardia is dangerous** in coronary disease because it eats diastole; (iii) **LV ≠ RV** because wall tension drives the compression effect. Anchor for Ch 22 (tension-time integral as energy cost) and Ch 25 (integrated control during exercise).

## Section 4 — The skeletal muscle (pp. 563–566)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **A microvascular unit is the capillary bed supplied by a single terminal arteriole** (pp. 563–564)
- **Metabolites released by active muscle trigger vasodilation and an increase in blood flow** (pp. 564–565)
- **Sympathetic innervation increases the intrinsic tone of resistance vessels** (p. 565)
- **Rhythmic contraction promotes blood flow through the "muscle pump"** (pp. 565–566)

### Core claims

#### Dynamic range and the microvascular unit
- **Dynamic range** — **5–10 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹** at rest → **250 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹** at maximal aerobic exercise (≥ 50×). The linear correspondence among work rate, $\dot Q_{O_2}$, and muscle blood flow defines the **coupling** of fiber activity to O₂ delivery (Ch 60).
- **Architecture (Fig. 24-5).**
  - **Feed arteries** lie **outside the muscle** and account for **30–50% of total muscle vascular resistance** at rest. Because they are physically removed from contracting fibers, their tone is set primarily by **sympathetic activity and shear-stress endothelial signaling**, not by metabolites of the parenchyma. This makes feed-artery tone the upper limit on muscle blood flow during whole-body exercise.
  - Within the muscle the network branches through **primary → secondary → transverse → terminal arterioles**. The **terminal arteriole** is the smallest VSMC-containing vessel — the **functional equivalent of the precapillary sphincter** (Ch 20).
  - One terminal arteriole + the **15–20 capillaries** it supplies, running parallel to muscle fibers for **< 1 mm**, ending in a **collecting venule** = one **microvascular unit**. This is the smallest unit of muscle flow control.
  - Because capillaries are short (< 1 mm) but muscle fibers are long (centimeters), many microvascular units in series span each fiber; contraction recruits flow in all of them simultaneously.

#### Metabolic dilation and capillary recruitment
- At rest, ~80% of muscle capillaries are unperfused (closed precapillary sphincters; Ch 20 Fig. 20-4B).
- **Exercise onset: terminal arterioles dilate first** → recruitment of previously closed capillaries → **↑ density of perfused capillaries → ↓ effective intercapillary distance → ↑ tissue Po₂** (Ch 20 Krogh).
- **Initial O₂ extraction rises sharply** (E_{O₂}) while total flow rises modestly; as demand continues to climb, the only way to deliver more O₂ is **further vasodilation**.
- **Ascending vasodilation.** Dilation **conducts upstream** from terminal arterioles through the arteriolar network to the feed arteries (gap-junction-mediated electrical spread along the endothelium + VSMCs; same cell-to-cell mechanism as in the brain). A coordinated ascending dilation is essential because the series-resistance segments **all** need to relax — selective dilation of the most distal vessels alone cannot raise muscle flow appreciably while upstream segments remain constricted.
- **Vasodilator candidates** — **adenosine** (most general), **CO₂**, **K⁺** (early, transient; may also hyperpolarize VSMCs and endothelial cells, conducting the upstream signal), **lactate/H⁺**, **NO** (endothelial; sustained); **no single molecule fully explains** the integrated response. Hyperemia begins within **1–2 seconds** of exercise onset — too fast for diffusion of newly produced metabolites alone, implying the **conducted electrical signal initiated by [K⁺]ₒ + the muscle pump** drives the initial rise.

#### Sympathetic control and functional sympatholysis
- Sympathetic fibers invest the entire resistance network from feed arteries to terminal arterioles. Basal firing rate **1–2 Hz** → modest tonic constriction. With exercise / baroreflex stress, firing rises to **8–16 Hz** — enough to **close** arteriolar lumens in resting muscle.
- In active muscle, however, the same level of sympathetic firing does **not** close the arterioles, because:
  - Metabolic dilators (adenosine, NO, low Po₂) directly oppose NE-induced VSMC contraction at the resistance vessels.
  - NO and adenosine may **presynaptically inhibit NE release** from sympathetic terminals near arterioles.
- This is **functional sympatholysis** — the central paradox of exercise physiology: **the same sympathetic activity that constricts inactive muscle is overridden in active muscle**, allowing redistribution of CO toward where it is needed.
- Importantly, **feed arteries**, sitting outside the muscle and beyond reach of parenchymal metabolites, remain **subject to sympathetic constriction** — they are the **upper-limit valve** on muscle blood flow during heavy whole-body exercise. This explains why each active muscle group during whole-body work gets less flow than it would if it were the only group working.
- During exercise, **up to 80% of CO** flows to active skeletal muscle. The redistribution comes from: (i) ↑ sympathetic constriction of splanchnic, renal, and inactive-muscle beds; (ii) metabolic override in active muscle; (iii) sparing of brain and coronary beds.

#### The muscle pump (Fig. 24-6)
- Each rhythmic contraction compresses intramuscular veins → **valves prevent backflow** → blood is forced toward the heart → during relaxation, venous pressure drops sharply → **arteriovenous pressure gradient (driving force for capillary flow) increases**.
- **Flow is maximal between contractions**, not during them — analogous to the LV coronary timing relation.
- The muscle pump **imparts substantial kinetic energy** to the blood, **reducing the work of the heart**; in vigorous exercise, it may **generate up to half** of the total energy required to circulate blood. This is why pharmacological vasodilation of an inactive limb cannot match the flow achieved by rhythmic contraction.
- Postural-tolerance corollary (Ch 22 Fig. 22-7C): the muscle pump is the only thing that prevents venous pooling and orthostatic syncope during prolonged standing.

### Equations

- **Capillary-recruitment-mediated PS-product change** (carried from Ch 20):

$$P_X \cdot S\big|_{\text{exercise}} \approx 10 \times P_X \cdot S\big|_{\text{rest}}$$

  driven by ~10× increase in $S$ (perfused capillary number) with only modest change in $P_X$ — the textbook "$P_X \cdot S$ is dynamic" claim of Ch 20 turned into a quantitative anchor in exercising muscle.

- **Feed-artery resistance as the bottleneck:**

$$R_{\text{total}} = R_{\text{feed}} + R_{\text{arteriolar}} + R_{\text{capillary}}, \qquad R_{\text{feed}}/R_{\text{total}} \approx 0.3\text{–}0.5$$

  → maximal muscle flow is set by feed-artery dilation, which is sympathetically gated rather than metabolically gated.

## Section 5 — The splanchnic organs (pp. 566–569)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The vascular supply to the gut is highly interconnected** (pp. 566–567)
- **Blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract increases up to eight-fold after a meal (postprandial hyperemia)** (p. 567)
- **Sympathetic activity directly constricts splanchnic blood vessels, whereas parasympathetic activity indirectly dilates them** (pp. 567–568)
- **Changes in the splanchnic circulation regulate total peripheral resistance and the distribution of blood volume** (p. 568)
- **Exercise and hemorrhage can substantially reduce splanchnic blood flow** (p. 568)
- **The liver receives its blood flow from both the systemic and the portal circulation** (pp. 568–569)

### Core claims

#### Anatomy and the arcaded collateral
- **Splanchnic circulation** = blood flow through stomach + small intestine + large intestine + pancreas + spleen + liver.
- Three arterial trunks: **celiac** (stomach, pancreas, spleen), **superior mesenteric (SMA)** (most of small intestine + parts of stomach/pancreas; carries > 10% of CO — the largest single splanchnic branch), and **inferior mesenteric (IMA)** (distal colon).
- **Arcades** — extensive interconnections between the branching arterial fans (Fig. 24-7B) provide **multiple collateral pathways**; segmental ischemia is rare unless multiple arcades are compromised.
- **Wall anatomy.** Penetrating arteries → muscularis arterioles → **submucosal vascular plexus** → arterioles projecting toward the lumen (mucosa and **villi**). Venules emerging from villi + muscularis converge into veins paralleling the arterial supply.

#### Villus countercurrent O₂ exchange
- Each villus contains an **incoming arteriole running up its center** branching into capillaries that descend along the villus walls into venules at the villus base (Fig. 24-7D — "fountain" arrangement).
- At **low flow**, the geometry produces **countercurrent O₂ shunting**: O₂ diffuses from the ascending arteriole directly into the descending venule before reaching the **tip** — the tip is the **first to become ischemic** under hypoperfusion (post-hemorrhage, post-exercise ischemia). At high postprandial flow, transit times are short and countercurrent exchange is negligible.
- **Capillaries in villi are fenestrated** (large pores) + high surface area → ideal for absorption.
- **Lipophilic nutrients** enter the **central lacteal** of each villus (→ intestinal lymphatics → thoracic duct) rather than the venous return.

#### Postprandial hyperemia (up to 8×)
- Resting fasting intestinal flow ~30 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹ → peak postprandial ~250 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹ (~8×). Multiple converging mechanisms:
  1. **Anticipatory CNS / "cephalic phase"** — mere thought of food raises splanchnic flow (parallels gastric + pancreatic cephalic-phase secretion).
  2. **Mucosal metabolic activity** — active transport of nutrients across the epithelium consumes O₂ and produces **adenosine** + **CO₂**.
  3. **Hyperosmolality** in blood and lymphatics of villi (driven by nutrient absorption) directly increases blood flow.
  4. **Hormonal**:
     - GI hormones with local vasoactive effect — **CCK**, **neurotensin** (Ch 41).
     - **Kinins** (bradykinin, kallidin) released by the intestinal epithelium during digestion — powerful vasodilators.
     - **Bile acids and partially digested fats** act on **chemoreceptors in the intestinal mucosa** to potentiate hyperemia.
- Flow rises **sequentially** along the GI tract: stomach first → small intestine → distal segments, as luminal contents move through. Postprandial hyperemia lasts **2–4 hours**.
- **Peristalsis transiently reduces flow** in the wall (extravascular compression, analogous to heart and skeletal muscle).

#### Sympathetic and parasympathetic control
- Splanchnic vessels (all except capillaries) receive postganglionic sympathetic fibers from celiac, SMA, and IMA ganglia, traveling along the matching arteries.
- **Dominant sympathetic effect = α-vasoconstriction**, mediated by NE on VSMCs. Constriction is uniform across muscularis and mucosal layers (no redistribution between layers). Maximal sympathetic activity can reduce flow to **< 10 mL·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹** (~⅓ of resting flow).
- Parasympathetic input is **indirect**: vagal/pelvic preganglionics → postganglionics within the gut wall → **stimulate motility and secretion** → ↑ intestinal metabolism → metabolic vasodilation. The enteric nervous system (ENS; Ch 14) integrates these signals and contributes additional vasoactive neurotransmitters (myenteric plexus).

#### Splanchnic bed as resistance modulator + venous reservoir
- **Splanchnic resistance is one of the body's largest adjustable peripheral resistances.** Sympathetic constriction during exercise (or hemorrhage) raises TPR substantially, supporting MAP.
- **Splanchnic veins hold ~15% of total blood volume**, mostly in the **liver**. Sympathetic arteriolar constriction → fall in capillary pressure → **passive collapse of splanchnic veins** → blood shifted into the **inferior vena cava** → increased effective circulating volume. With stronger sympathetic activation, **active venoconstriction** mobilizes additional reservoir volume.
- During maximal exercise or severe hemorrhage, splanchnic flow may fall to **< 25%** of resting. The intestinal mucosa tolerates this **temporarily** (only ~20% O₂ extraction at rest, so extraction reserve buffers brief reductions). **Prolonged ischemia** → mucosal sloughing (especially villus tips, where countercurrent exchange compounds hypoxia) → pancreatic enzyme leakage → toxic activators → **multiple organ failure** + **endotoxic shock** (loss of mucosal barrier; bacterial translocation).

#### Hepatic dual blood supply
- **Liver receives ~25% of resting CO** through two parallel inflows:
  - **Portal vein — 75% of hepatic blood flow.** Has already given up much O₂ to the gut. Pressure ~10–12 mm Hg.
  - **Hepatic artery — 25% of hepatic blood flow.** Carries ~75% of the O₂ used by the liver. Pressure ~90 mm Hg.
- Both feed the **sinusoids** (8–9 mm Hg) — the liver's "capillaries". Sinusoids drain to terminal hepatic venules (central veins) → hepatic veins (~5 mm Hg) → IVC.
- **Three pressure-drop conclusions:**
  1. **Hepatic-arterial precapillary resistance is very high** (90 → 8–9 mm Hg drop), preventing arterial backflow into the portal vein.
  2. **Portal precapillary resistance is very low** (12 → 8–9 mm Hg drop), enabling 75% of liver flow at low driving pressure.
  3. **Sinusoidal resistance is very low** (8–9 → 5 mm Hg), so **hepatic-vein backpressure** (e.g., in right-sided heart failure) **directly transmits to the sinusoid** and produces transudation into the peritoneum → **ascites**.
- **Sinusoidal endothelium has large fenestrations and gaps** (sinusoidal / discontinuous capillary type; Ch 20 Fig. 20-3C) — sinusoids are **more permeable to protein** than any capillary elsewhere. **Kupffer cells** (Ch 46) lining the sinusoids remove bacteria and particulate matter from portal blood before it reaches the systemic circulation.
- **Hepatic-arterial-buffer response.** Drops in portal inflow trigger compensatory rises in hepatic-arterial flow (mediated by adenosine accumulation when portal washout falls). The reverse — arterial → portal compensation — is purely passive (sinusoidal pressure falls when arterial inflow drops). **Hepatic-arterial supply autoregulates; the portal supply does not.** The two together do **not fully** stabilize total hepatic flow; the liver compensates further by varying its O₂ **extraction ratio**.

### Equations

- **Hepatic blood-flow partition**:

$$F_{\text{hepatic}} = F_{\text{HA}} + F_{\text{PV}}, \qquad F_{\text{HA}} \approx 0.25 \, F_{\text{hepatic}}, \quad F_{\text{PV}} \approx 0.75 \, F_{\text{hepatic}}$$

  but $\dot Q_{O_2,\text{HA}} \approx 0.75 \, \dot Q_{O_2,\text{liver}}$ — the **arterial supply carries ¼ of the flow but ¾ of the O₂**.

- **Splanchnic venoconstriction as effective-volume gain** (qualitative):

$$\Delta V_{\text{effective circulating}} \approx \Delta V_{\text{splanchnic venous}} \approx -C_{\text{splanchnic}} \cdot \Delta P_{\text{splanchnic venous}}$$

  reservoir mobilization scales as the (large) splanchnic venous compliance × the pressure-fall induced by upstream arteriolar constriction.

## Section 6 — The skin (pp. 570–571)

### Subsection headings (verbatim)
- **The skin is the largest organ of the body** (p. 570)
- **Specialized arteriovenous anastomoses in apical skin help control heat loss** (pp. 570–571)
- **Mechanical stimuli elicit local vascular responses in the skin** (p. 571)

### Core claims

#### Skin blood flow serves thermoregulation, not nutrition
- Skin is **overperfused** relative to nutritional requirements → local metabolic control is **of little functional importance**.
- **Thermoregulation is the dominant role.** Forward link: Ch 59 (regulation of body temperature).
- **Anatomic note** — capillaries reach only as deep as the dermis (the epidermis has no blood supply); subepidermal venular plexus holds an appreciable volume → in light-skinned individuals, dermal blood volume sets visible skin color (pallor when vasoconstricted, flushing when dilated).

#### Apical vs. nonapical skin: two architectures for the same job
- **Apical skin** (ears, nose, lips, hands, feet, fingertips, toes, nail beds) has **high surface-to-volume ratio** → favors heat exchange. Its distinctive feature is **arteriovenous anastomoses called glomus bodies** — small, thick-walled vessels containing multiple layers of myoepithelioid cells — densely innervated by sympathetic NE fibers. **Density ~500/cm² in nail beds.** Glomus bodies sit **in parallel** with the nutritional capillaries; opening them shunts blood from arteriole directly to venule, dramatically increasing convective heat exchange but bypassing capillary exchange.
- **Nonapical skin** (most body surface) **lacks glomus bodies**. Instead, it has **two types of sympathetic fibers**:
  - **Adrenergic (NE)** → α-vasoconstriction.
  - **Cholinergic (ACh)** → **active vasodilation**, partly indirect (cholinergic activation of eccrine sweat glands → sweat-gland kallikrein → conversion of kininogens to **bradykinin** → VSMC relaxation in nearby vessels) and partly direct (co-released **CGRP** and **VIP** act on VSMCs; atropine-resistant component).

#### Thermoregulatory control rules (Table-style summary)

| Site | Vasoconstriction | Vasodilation |
|---|---|---|
| **Apical skin** | Sympathetic NE → glomus-body closure (active) | **Passive** — withdrawal of sympathetic tone; **no active dilation** |
| **Nonapical skin** | Sympathetic NE → α-vasoconstriction (modest at rest) | Sympathetic ACh → active vasodilation (sweat-gland-mediated kinin pathway + direct VIP/CGRP) |

- Implication 1 — **at rest in a cool environment**, apical skin has high tonic sympathetic NE drive; blocking it can **quadruple** apical skin flow (~equivalent to heat-stress dilation). Nonapical skin at rest in a thermoneutral environment has little baseline NE tone; blocking does little.
- Implication 2 — **with rising core temperature**, nonapical skin actively dilates (cholinergic, with sweating); apical skin **passively dilates** (NE-tone withdrawal). The combination redistributes a large fraction of CO to the skin (the chapter's running comment that, during exercise heat stress, only brain and heart are spared from vasoconstrictor redistribution).
- Implication 3 — **with falling core temperature**, sympathetic NE outflow rises; glomus bodies close completely (apical flow → minimum); nonapical NE constriction reduces flow modestly. Conserves heat at the cost of distal-extremity perfusion (frostbite topology).

#### Local mechanical responses — the cutaneous "triple response" (axon reflex)
A graded sequence of cutaneous responses to mechanical injury:
1. **White reaction** — gentle stroke with a blunt instrument. **Immediate blanching** = passive expulsion of blood. Over 15–60 s, the trailing line becomes more sharply white as **microvascular VSMCs and pericytes contract in response to mechanical stimulation** (active emptying of capillary loops, collecting venules, and subpapillary venous plexus).
2. **Red reaction (red component of triple response)** — more forceful stroke. **Local redness** appears within seconds along the line of injury, reflecting capillary + venular dilation. **Independent of innervation**; mediated by local **histamine** release from disturbed cells; persists ~1–several min.
3. **Flare** — stronger stimulus. Reddening **spreads beyond** the line stroked. This is the **axon reflex** (Ch 15): a single sensory C-fiber branches at a peripheral junction; the AP travels centrally to the branch point and then **antidromically along the collateral branch**, where it releases **substance P + CGRP** to dilate arterioles in the surrounding skin. Survives anesthetic block of the proximal nerve until the fiber degenerates — this is the diagnostic test.
4. **Wheal** — most intense (whip lash). **Localized swelling** within minutes; reflects increased capillary permeability (histamine) → filtration > absorption (Ch 20 Starling) → interstitial edema. Preceded by + replaces the red reaction; often surrounded by the flare.

## Boxes in Chapter 24

### Box 24-1 — Treating Coronary Artery Disease (p. 564)
- **Angina pectoris** is the first symptom in many patients; the **chest pain emerges when O₂ demand exceeds the compromised supply**, accompanied by **ST/T changes on ECG**.
- Diagnostic confirmation: **exercise stress testing** with ECG monitoring or **thallium scanning**; "silent ischemia" is common.
- **Medical therapy**:
  - **Nitrates** → release NO; dilate peripheral veins (↓preload, ↓ wall tension by Laplace), dilate arteries (↓afterload), may promote coronary collateral flow. The textbook caveat (coronary steal, above) means the dominant benefit is the systemic preload/afterload reduction.
  - **β-blockers** → block β1 on cardiac myocytes; ↓ HR, ↓ contractility → ↓ O₂ demand (↓ tension-time integral; Ch 22).
  - **Calcium-channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem, nifedipine)** → reduce contractility and dilate coronary + systemic vessels.
- **Revascularization**:
  - **CABG (coronary artery bypass grafting)** — older standard.
  - **PTCA (percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty)** with **stent placement** + aggressive anticoagulation — current dominant approach; restenosis (intimal VSMC proliferation) is the main failure mode.
  - **Investigational**: angiogenesis promoters (cf. Ch 20 VEGF/FGF/angiopoietins).

### Box 24-2 — Portal Hypertension (p. 569)
- **Cirrhosis** — fibrotic, shrunken liver from chronic ETOH (US) or HBV/HCV (worldwide); hemochromatosis, Wilson disease, biliary cirrhosis, sclerosing cholangitis. 5-year survival < 10% in advanced cases.
- **Three complication clusters:**
  1. **Metabolic** — ↓ albumin (↓ plasma colloid osmotic pressure → edema; Ch 20), ↓ cholesterol synthesis, ↑ prothrombin time (↓ coagulation-factor synthesis), → **hepatorenal syndrome** (renal hypoperfusion).
  2. **Portal hypertension** — scarring ↑ intrahepatic resistance → ↑ portal venous pressure → ↑ splanchnic-capillary pressure → Starling-driven **filtration > absorption** → **abdominal edema → ascites**. Rising portal pressure → diversion of portal blood into pre-existing **portacaval anastomoses** (lower esophagus, periumbilical, rectal, retroperitoneal). **Esophageal varices** can rupture and produce life-threatening hemorrhage; managed with **sclerotherapy**, **transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS)**, or surgical **portacaval / distal splenorenal shunts**.
  3. **Hepatic encephalopathy** — portal blood bypasses the liver via the dilated anastomoses or via TIPS → toxic metabolites (e.g., **NH₃**; Ch 39) accumulate in the systemic circulation → cross the BBB → acute delirium and altered mental status.

## Glossary (downstream-chunkable)

- **Special circulation** — a regional vascular bed whose control combines the four general mechanisms (neural / myogenic / metabolic / endothelial) in a tissue-specific weighting.
- **Circle of Willis / source arteries (internal carotids + vertebrals → basilar) / distributing arteries (anterior, middle, posterior cerebral) / pial arteries / penetrating arterioles**
- **Blood-brain barrier (BBB)** — tight-junction cerebral capillary endothelium (CLDN5 + occludin); excludes paracellular flux of polar solutes and proteins; gases and lipid-soluble drugs cross freely; glucose via GLUT1 facilitated diffusion.
- **Circumventricular organs** — fenestrated cerebral capillaries (exception to BBB).
- **Brain extracellular fluid (BECF) pH as the dominant cerebral vasodilator stimulus** / Pco₂ (respiratory acidosis) vs metabolic acidosis differential effect on CBF.
- **Cerebral autoregulation** — CBF constant over MAP 70–150 mm Hg.
- **Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) = MAP − ICP**
- **Neurovascular unit / neurovascular coupling** — neurons + astrocytes + vasculature; glutamate/GABA → astrocyte [Ca²⁺]ᵢ waves → NO + arachidonate metabolite release → local arteriolar dilation; conducted vasodilation along endothelium and VSMCs retrograde to pial arteries.
- **Cushing reflex** — sympathetic-mediated systemic hypertension in response to raised intracranial pressure → maintenance of cerebral perfusion against vascular compression.
- **Coronary diastolic-predominant perfusion** — ~80% of left coronary flow in diastole due to systolic extravascular compression; early-systolic flow reversal.
- **Endo/epi perfusion gradient** — subendocardium most vulnerable to ischemia under low aortic diastolic pressure or high coronary resistance.
- **Adenosine** — dominant coronary metabolic vasodilator; ATP-breakdown product; binds purinergic receptors → ↓[Ca²⁺]ᵢ → VSMC relaxation.
- **Coronary autoregulation** — MAP ~70 to > 150 mm Hg.
- **Functional unmasking of α-vasoconstriction by β-block** in the coronary bed.
- **Coronary collateralization** — angiogenesis-driven, occurs with gradual stenosis.
- **Coronary steal** — vasodilator therapy in atherosclerotic disease can divert flow away from maximally-dilated ischemic regions.
- **Microvascular unit (skeletal muscle)** — terminal arteriole + 15–20 parallel capillaries + collecting venule, < 1 mm long; functional equivalent of the precapillary sphincter of Ch 20.
- **Feed artery (skeletal muscle)** — extramuscular muscular artery carrying 30–50% of total muscle vascular resistance; sympathetically gated; not directly influenced by intramuscular metabolites.
- **Conducted vasodilation** — gap-junction-mediated propagation of vasodilator signal upstream along endothelium and VSMCs, from terminal arterioles to feed arteries; substrate for "ascending dilation" during exercise hyperemia.
- **Functional sympatholysis** — metabolic override of sympathetic α-vasoconstriction in active muscle; also possibly via presynaptic inhibition of NE release.
- **Muscle pump** — rhythmic contraction + venous valves convert muscle work into venous return; up to ~50% of circulatory energy during heavy exercise.
- **Postprandial hyperemia** — up to 8× rise in intestinal flow after a meal; cephalic + metabolic + osmolar + hormonal (CCK, neurotensin, kinins, bile acids, fats).
- **Villus countercurrent O₂ exchange** — arteriovenous shortcut at low flow; villus tip first to become ischemic.
- **Splanchnic reservoir** — ~15% of total blood volume; mobilizable by sympathetic arteriolar constriction (passive venous collapse) + active venoconstriction.
- **Endotoxic shock / multiple organ failure from intestinal mucosal sloughing** — sequel of prolonged splanchnic ischemia.
- **Dual hepatic supply** — portal vein (75% flow, 25% O₂; ~12 mm Hg) + hepatic artery (25% flow, 75% O₂; ~90 mm Hg) → sinusoid (8–9 mm Hg) → central vein → hepatic vein.
- **Sinusoidal capillary** — large fenestrations and gaps (Ch 20 discontinuous); high permeability to protein; Kupffer cells lining sinusoids remove bacteria + particulates.
- **Hepatic arterial buffer response** — compensatory rise in hepatic-arterial flow when portal flow drops; adenosine-mediated.
- **Ascites** — transudation of fluid from sinusoids into peritoneal cavity, driven by elevated hepatic venous pressure (e.g., right-sided heart failure or cirrhotic portal hypertension).
- **Portal hypertension / portacaval anastomoses / esophageal varices / hepatic encephalopathy / TIPS / portacaval and distal splenorenal shunts**
- **Apical vs. nonapical skin** — vascular architecture difference defined by the presence or absence of AV anastomoses (glomus bodies).
- **Glomus body** — densely innervated AV anastomosis in apical skin; multiple layers of myoepithelioid VSMCs; ~500/cm² in the nail bed; under sympathetic NE control.
- **Active sympathetic cholinergic vasodilation (nonapical skin)** — co-released ACh activates eccrine sweat-gland kallikrein → bradykinin → VSMC relaxation; CGRP/VIP co-release adds an atropine-resistant component.
- **Apical skin: vasodilation is passive** — by withdrawal of NE tone.
- **Triple response (Lewis)** — red reaction (local histamine), flare (axon reflex via substance P / CGRP), wheal (histamine-driven Starling-imbalance edema).
- **Axon reflex** — antidromic propagation of sensory C-fiber AP along a collateral branch, releasing substance P / CGRP that dilate downstream arterioles.
