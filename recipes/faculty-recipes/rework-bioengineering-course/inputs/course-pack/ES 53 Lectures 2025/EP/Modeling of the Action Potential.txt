                      Action Potentials and the Hodgkin-Huxley Model


What is an action potential?
        An action potential is an all or nothing response of electrically excitable cells that consists of a
rapid depolarization followed by slow repolarization.

         The shape of the action potential in a given cell reflects the specialized functions of that cell. For
example, the brief action potentials of a nerve axon permit rapid signaling, whereas the prolonged,
repetitive action potentials of cardiac and certain types of smooth-muscle cells mediate the slower,
rhythmic contractions of these tissues.

         The membrane potential of a cell can be measured (as a function of time) experimentally with a
very small microelectrode. A small stimulus (current) is required to bring the membrane potential up to
the threshold, called the threshold voltage or threshold potential. Once this threshold is reached an action
potential occurs in that cell. It should be noted that every cell has a membrane potential, but action
potentials do not occur in every type of cell in the body. They only occur in electrically excitable cells
such as nerve and muscle cells. It should also be noted that the stimulus current is used experimentally
when studying excitable cells in vitro. In the body cells are stimulated in a slightly more complex way
from neurons via neurotransmitters. A typical resting membrane potential of a neuron is about -70 mV
and it will depolarize to about 35 mV during the peak of an action potential. The specific resting
membrane potentials and peak depolarizations vary across cell type and location in the body.
         The threshold, amplitude, shape, and duration of the action potential are primarily dependent on
the following factors:

        The gating (opening and closing) and permeability properties of ion channels—these properties
         are dependent on both Vm (membrane potential) and time.
        The intracellular and extracellular concentrations of the ions that pass through these channels,
         such as Na+, K+, Ca2+, and Cl−.
To a lesser degree they are also dependent on membrane properties such as capacitance, resistance, and
the geometry of the cell, but for simplicity we will focus on the above two factors.

Why do we see this non-linear response in excitable cells? Hodgkin and Huxley wondered the same thing.
As it turns out, ion channels, or “gates” in the cell membrane affect the permeability of those ions to flow
through the membrane. These voltage gated channels change the “conductance” to ions. The changes in
conductance to sodium and potassium (more than any other ions) underlie the action potential.



Hodgkin and Huxley conducted experiments in the giant squid axon to derive the model of action
potential kinetics.
Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley’s goal was to account for the action potential by molecular
mechanisms. they tried to develop an empirical kinetic description which would allow them to calculate
the electrical responses and which would correctly predict the shape and time course of the action
potential. They used voltage- and time-dependent parameters to to explain the ionic fluxes and
conductance changes observed during an action potential. Today this model is referred to as the Hodgkin-
Huxley or HH model.
We now understand that the nerve action potential is a phenomenon involving voltage-dependent currents
of Na+ and K+ that flow through distinct molecular pathways called Na+ channels and K+ channels. In
1963, Alan L. Hodgkin and Andrew F. Huxley shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their

Now as you recall, gx is dependent on voltage and time. H&H defined the Na+ and K+ currents in terms
of their maximum possible conductances (gNa-max or gK-max) multiplied by coefficients that varied between
0 and 1. The overall conductance of each ion then varies from 0 to 1. All of the kinetic properties of the
conductances are embedded in the characteristics of these coefficients. [Hodgkin and Huxley determined
these through a detailed series of experiments which you can read about in more detail in their original
manuscript or most Neurobiology textbooks.]
As previously mentioned, the conductances of the sodium and potassium ions (gNa or gK) change due to
changes in voltage-gated channels in the cell membrane. The kinetic properties of these changes are
embedded in the coefficients and modeled mathematically as a set of four “gates” for each ion. This
approach allowed them to sufficiently model the kinetics of the channels. In reality there aren’t actually
four distinct gates, but rather many many channels in each cell membrane. These channels are modeled
mathematically as the combined probability of the four “gates” – which in turn gives us the coefficient for
a single ion conductance.

In total there are three types of gates, m, n, and h. For both sodium and potassium the kinetics are
modeled by four “gates”. These m, n, and h gates each have different time constants (τm, τn, τh). They also
have initial (a.k.a. resting) values (m0, n0, h0) between 0 and 1 related to their probability of being open,
and activation values (m∞, n∞, h∞) associated with their probability of being open at steady state (∞). To
measure these values Hodgkin and Huxley performed voltage clamp experiments that involved changing
the membrane potential essentially instantaneously from the resting potential (~-65 mV) to some other set
potential and clamping it there until it reaches steady state. When doing so the initial values (m0, n0, h0) of
m, n, and h relax to new values m∞, n∞, h∞ at this clamped voltage. These values were all determined
experimentally and are summarized in Table 1.

  Bioengineering Application: Optogenetics may lead to control of neurons.
  An exciting new(ish) area of Bioengineering aims to control excitable cells via optogenetics. This
  optical excitation technique involves the use of membrane proteins called channelrhodopsins (ChRs).
  ChR is homologous to the rhodopsin protein of mammalian photoreceptor cells except that it
  functions as a light-activated ion channel (as opposed to voltage activated). Expression of engineered
  ChR in mammalian neurons allows the Vm (membrane potential) of the transfected neurons to be
  stimulated by illumination with light of an appropriate wavelength. Thus, ChR and related light-gated
  channels can be used as optical switches to turn on or off the electrical activity of specific cells in
  various tissues such as the brain. This method has initiated the field of optogenetics which enables the
  function of individual cells in the nervous system to be studied by observing the physiological
  response to light activation in living transgenic animals. Someday it may be possible to control
  arrhythmia in a diseased heart with light or to restore visual perception to a defective retina with the
  use of such approaches.
