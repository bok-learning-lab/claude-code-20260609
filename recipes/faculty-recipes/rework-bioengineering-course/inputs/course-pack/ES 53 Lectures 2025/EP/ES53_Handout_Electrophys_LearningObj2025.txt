Part II: Nernst potential, membrane potential, driving voltages


The Nernst potential (equilibrium potential) is the intracellular electrical voltage that would produce a
current that would be equal in magnitude but opposite in direction to that produced by the process of
diffusion. For example: the K+ equilibrium potential (EK) is the electrical potential that counters the
diffusion of K+.
If substance x doesn’t undergo chemical transformation, and it is at the same temperature and pressure
on two sides of a membrane (primarily true in most biological cases), then the difference in its electro–
chemical potential across the membrane (“inside” of membrane, versus “outside” as reference) is a
function of the concentrations on either side. For a single ion across a membrane this is:

                                    Ex = Vi-Vo = RT/(ziF)*ln([x]out/[x]in)

Where F is Faraday’s constant, R is the gas constant, T is temperature in Kelvin, and zi is the valence
of the ion. The concentrations of an individual ion (x) inside and outside the cell are [x]in and [x]out
(also written as ci and co) respectively.

Part III: Action potentials
An action potential is an all or nothing response of electrically excitable cells that consists of a rapid
depolarization followed by a slower repolarization. It arises from dramatic changes in ion conductance.
This electrical signal propagates along axons and other excitable cells and allows for long-range
transmission of information.

Voltage-gated ion channel – transmembrane channel whose conductance (ability to pass ions) is
dependent on membrane voltage. Both voltage-gated K+ channels and Na+ channels need to be
depolarized from the resting potential to open.

Inactivation - property of some depolarization-gated ion channels whose conductance goes to zero
even though the membrane depolarized. Voltage-gated sodium channels inactivate shortly after
opening. The membrane must hyperpolarize for the channels to de-inactivate and return to their closed
conformation before they can open again.

The ion channels for Na+ and K+ are specific for each ion.
The channels for Na+ are all gated and are closed at resting potential.

Membrane Summary
   1. Changes in Membrane Potential
          a. Neurons and muscle cells can change their membrane potentials. This property is called
             excitability and is caused by changes in the permeability to certain ions.
          b. At rest, a neuron is considered polarized when the inside is more negative than the
             outside.
          c. When the membrane potential inside the cell increases (becomes more positive), this is
             called depolarization.
          d. A return to resting potential is called repolarization.
          e. When the membrane potential inside the cell decreases (becomes more negative), this
             is called hyperpolarization.
          f. Depolarization occurs when positive ions enter the cell (usually Na+).
          g. Hyperpolarization occurs when positive ions leave the cell (usually K+) or negative ions
             (Cl−) enter the cell.
          h. Depolarization of the cell is excitatory.
          i. Hyperpolarization is inhibitory.
Ion Gating Summary:
   1. Changes in membrane potential are controlled by changes in the flow of ions through channels.
   2. Voltage-Gated Na+ Channels
          a. These channels open if the membrane potential depolarizes to −55mV.
          b. This is called the threshold.
          c. Sodium rushes in due to the electrochemical gradient.
          d. Membrane potential climbs toward sodium equilibrium potential.
          e. These channels are deactivated at +30mV.
   3. Voltage-Gated K+ Channels
          a. At around +30mV, voltage-gated K+ channels open, and K+ rushes out of the cell
             following the electrochemical gradient.
          b. This makes the cell repolarize back toward the potassium equilibrium potential.
