Part I: Cardiac anatomy, electrical properties of the heart, and ECG

The heart is a biochemically powered, electrically-activated, pressure and volume regulated, tandem,

mechanical pump with a mean time to failure of 2 billion cycles

Figure 1: Anatomy of the human heart. Figure 2: Conduction pathways of the heart. 

[From Fox,  Physiology, 2018][From Fox,  Physiology, 2018]

Electrocardiogram - records the electrical activity of the heart by picking up the movement of ions in body tissues in response to this activity.

Does not record action potentials, but results from waves of depolarization

Does not record contraction or relaxation, but the electrical events leading to those events

Waves and intervals you should know

P wave - atrial depolarization

P-Q interval – atrial systole

QRS wave - ventricular depolarization

S-T segment - plateau phase, ventricular systole

T wave - ventricular repolarization

Figure 3: Components of the electrocardiogram (ECG). [From Boron and Boulpaep, Physiology, 2018]

Arrhythmias are abnormal patterns of electrical activity that result in abnormalities of the heartbeat. 

Abnormal heart rhythms

Bradycardia: slow heart rate, below 60 bpm

Tachycardia: fast heart rate, above 100 bpm

These heart rhythms are normal if the person is active, but not normal at rest.

Abnormal tachycardia can occur due to drugs or fast ectopic pacemakers.

Ventricular tachycardia occurs when pacemakers in the ventricles make them contract out of synch with the atria. This condition is very dangerous and can lead to ventricular fibrillation and sudden death.

Flutter and Fibrillation

Flutter: extremely fast (200 to 300 bpm) but coordinated contractions

Fibrillation: uncoordinated pumping of the atria and/or ventricles 

Atrial fibrillation:  

Atrial muscles cannot effectively contract. Only reduces cardiac output by 15%

AV node can’t keep pace with speed of atrial contractions, but some stimulation is passed on. Associated with increased risk of thrombi, stroke, and heart failure

Ventricular fibrillation

Ventricles can’t pump blood, and victim dies without CPR and/or electrical defibrillation to reset the heart rhythm.

Sudden death progresses from ventricular tachycardia, through ventricular fibrillation, ending in astole (straight-line ECG)

Part II: Cardiac mechanics, cardiac cycle

Cardiac Cycle - repeating pattern of contraction and relaxation of heart

Systole refers to contraction phase Diastole refers to relaxation phase

Ventricles begin contraction, pressure rises, and AV valves close (lub); isovolumetric contraction

Pressure builds, semilunar valves open, and blood is ejected into arteries.

Pressure in ventricles falls; semilunar valves close (dub); isovolumetric relaxation

Pressure in ventricles falls below that of atria, and AV valve opens. Ventricles fill passively.

Atria contract, sending last of blood to ventricles

Important pressures of the cardiac cycle

Systolic pressure = arterial pressure during ventricular contraction 

Diastolic pressure = arterial pressure during ventricular relaxation

Pulse pressure = (systolic pressure) – (diastolic pressure)

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) represents average arterial pressure during the entire cardiac cycle

MAP ≈ 2/3 diastolic pressure + 1/3 systolic pressure

MAP ≈ diastolic pressure + 1/3 pulse pressure

Volume changes during the cardiac cycle

End-diastolic volume is volume of blood in ventricles at end of diastole (EDV)

End-systolic volume is amount of blood left in ventricles at end of systole (ESV)

Stroke volume is amount of blood ejected from ventricles during systole (SV = EDV-ESV)

Ejection fraction is the portion of blood ejected from the LV compared to the total volume of blood at the end of diastole. (EF = SV/EDV)

Cardiac Output - the volume of blood pumped/min by each ventricle

CO = SV x HR  =  (stroke volume X heart rate)

(ml/minute)     =     (ml/beat)    X   (beats/min)

Stroke volume (SV) = blood pumped/beat by each ventricle

SV Is determined by 3 variables:

End diastolic volume (EDV) = volume of blood in ventricles at end of diastole = Preload

Contractility = strength of ventricular contraction

Total peripheral resistance (TPR) = impedance to blood flow in arteries = Afterload

Total blood volume is about 5.0L to 5.5L

Part III: Pressure-Volume Dynamics

Cardiac output – the volume of blood pumped each minute by each ventricle:

Average heart rate = 70 bpm

Average stroke volume = 70 to 80 ml/beat

Average cardiac output = 5,000-5,500 ml/minute

Regulation of stroke volume by three variables:

Normally, about 60% of the EDV is ejected – This is known as the ejection fraction

End diastolic volume (EDV): volume of blood in the ventricles at the end of diastole

Preload

Stroke volume increases with increased EDV

Total peripheral resistance: Frictional resistance in the arteries

Afterload

Inversely related to stroke volume

Contractility: strength of ventricular contraction

Stroke volume increases with increased contractility.

Norepinephrine and epinephrine can increase contractility by increasing Ca+ to sarcomeres

Frank-Starling Law of the Heart - ↑ venous return  ↑filling of the left ventricle ↑preload  ↑initial sarcomere length  ↑force of contraction (due to ↑ cross-bridge cycling) 

Stroke work refers to the work done by the ventricle to eject a volume of blood (i.e., stroke volume) into the aorta. The force that is applied to the blood is the intraventricular pressure.

SW ~= Stroke Volume X Mean Aortic (arterial) Pressure
