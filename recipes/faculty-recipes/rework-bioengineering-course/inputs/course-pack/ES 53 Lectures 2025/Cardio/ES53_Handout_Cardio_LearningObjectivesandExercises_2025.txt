Part I: Cardiac anatomy, electrical properties of the heart, and ECG

The heart is a biochemically powered, electrically-activated, pressure and volume regulated, tandem,
mechanical pump with a mean time to failure of 2 billion cycles




Figure 1: Anatomy of the human heart.                       Figure 2: Conduction pathways of the heart.

Electrocardiogram - records the electrical activity of the heart by picking up the movement of ions in
body tissues in response to this activity.
   • Does not record action potentials, but results from waves of depolarization
   • Does not record contraction or relaxation, but the electrical events leading to those events
   • Waves and intervals you should know
        a. P wave - atrial depolarization
        b. P-Q interval – atrial systole
        c. QRS wave - ventricular depolarization
        d. S-T segment - plateau phase, ventricular systole
        e. T wave - ventricular repolarization

Arrhythmias are abnormal patterns of electrical activity that result in abnormalities of the heartbeat.
   1. Abnormal heart rhythms
      a) Bradycardia: slow heart rate, below 60 bpm
      b) Tachycardia: fast heart rate, above 100 bpm
              • These heart rhythms are normal if the person is active, but not normal at rest.
              • Abnormal tachycardia can occur due to drugs or fast ectopic pacemakers.
              • Ventricular tachycardia occurs when pacemakers in the ventricles make them
                  contract out of synch with the atria. This condition is very dangerous and can lead to
                  ventricular fibrillation and sudden death.
   2. Flutter and Fibrillation
      a) Flutter: extremely fast (200 to 300 bpm) but coordinated contractions
      b) Fibrillation: uncoordinated pumping of the atria and/or ventricles
      c) Atrial fibrillation:
          • Atrial muscles cannot effectively contract. Only reduces cardiac output by 15%
          • AV node can’t keep pace with speed of atrial contractions, but some stimulation is
              passed on. Associated with increased risk of thrombi, stroke, and heart failure
      d) Ventricular fibrillation
          • Ventricles can’t pump blood, and victim dies without CPR and/or electrical defibrillation
              to reset the heart rhythm.
          • Sudden death progresses from ventricular tachycardia, through ventricular fibrillation,
              ending in astole (straight-line ECG)

Part II: Cardiac mechanics, cardiac cycle
Cardiac Cycle - repeating pattern of contraction and relaxation of heart
  Systole refers to contraction phase        Diastole refers to relaxation phase
   1.   Ventricles begin contraction, pressure rises, and AV valves close (lub); isovolumetric contraction
   2.   Pressure builds, semilunar valves open, and blood is ejected into arteries.
   3.   Pressure in ventricles falls; semilunar valves close (dub); isovolumetric relaxation
   4.   Pressure in ventricles falls below that of atria, and AV valve opens. Ventricles fill passively.
   5.   Atria contract, sending last of blood to ventricles


Important pressures of the cardiac cycle
   •    Systolic pressure = arterial pressure during ventricular contraction
   •    Diastolic pressure = arterial pressure during ventricular relaxation
   •    Pulse pressure = (systolic pressure) – (diastolic pressure)
   •    Mean arterial pressure (MAP) represents average arterial pressure during the entire cardiac
        cycle
            • MAP ≈ 2/3 diastolic pressure + 1/3 systolic pressure
            • MAP ≈ diastolic pressure + 1/3 pulse pressure

Volume changes during the cardiac cycle
   •    End-diastolic volume is volume of blood in ventricles at end of diastole (EDV)
   •    End-systolic volume is amount of blood left in ventricles at end of systole (ESV)
   •    Stroke volume is amount of blood ejected from ventricles during systole (SV = EDV-ESV)
   •    Ejection fraction is the portion of blood ejected from the LV compared to the total volume of
        blood at the end of diastole. (EF = SV/EDV)


Cardiac Output - the volume of blood pumped/min by each ventricle
   • CO = SV x HR = (stroke volume X heart rate)
   • (ml/minute) = (ml/beat) X (beats/min)
   • Stroke volume (SV) = blood pumped/beat by each ventricle
   • SV Is determined by 3 variables:
          • End diastolic volume (EDV) = volume of blood in ventricles at end of diastole = Preload
          • Contractility = strength of ventricular contraction
          • Total peripheral resistance (TPR) = impedance to blood flow in arteries = Afterload
   • Total blood volume is about 5.0L to 5.5L

Part III: Pressure-Volume Dynamics
   1. Cardiac output – the volume of blood pumped each minute by each ventricle:
      a) Average heart rate = 70 bpm
      b) Average stroke volume = 70 to 80 ml/beat
      c) Average cardiac output = 5,000-5,500 ml/minute

   2. Regulation of stroke volume by three variables:
         • Normally, about 60% of the EDV is ejected – This is known as the ejection fraction
      a) End diastolic volume (EDV): volume of blood in the ventricles at the end of diastole
         • Preload
         • Stroke volume increases with increased EDV
      b) Total peripheral resistance: Frictional resistance in the arteries
         • Afterload
         • Inversely related to stroke volume
      c) Contractility: strength of ventricular contraction
         • Stroke volume increases with increased contractility.
         • Norepinephrine and epinephrine can increase contractility by increasing Ca+ to
            sarcomeres

   3. Frank-Starling Law of the Heart - ↑ venous return  ↑filling of the left ventricle ↑preload 
      ↑initial sarcomere length  ↑force of contraction (due to ↑ cross-bridge cycling)

   4. Stroke work refers to the work done by the ventricle to eject a volume of blood (i.e., stroke
      volume) into the aorta. The force that is applied to the blood is the intraventricular pressure.
      SW ~= Stroke Volume X Mean Aortic (arterial) Pressure
