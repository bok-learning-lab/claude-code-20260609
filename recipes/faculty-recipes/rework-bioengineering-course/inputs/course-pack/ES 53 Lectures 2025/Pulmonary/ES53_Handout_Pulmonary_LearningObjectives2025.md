Pulmonary overview:

Respiration is comprised of 3 related functions: ventilation, gas exchange, and O2 utilization (cellular respiration)

Ventilation moves air in and out of lungs for gas exchange with blood (external respiration)

Gas exchange between blood and tissues occurs via passive diffusion which rely on spatial concentration gradients.

O2 is consumed (and CO2 created) by aerobic metabolism in tissues throughout the body - this is called internal respiration

Partial Pressure, Dissolved Gas, and Gas Exchange Principles

Partial Pressure (PX) is the pressure that specific gas in a mixture exerts (independently of other gases) 

Dalton’s Law: The total pressure of a gas mixture is equal to the sum of the pressures of each gas in it.

Partial pressure: the pressure of an individual gas; can be measured by multiplying the % of that gas by the total pressure

Nitrogen makes up 78% of the atmosphere, O2 21%, and CO2 1%.

O2 makes up 21% of the atmosphere, so partial pressure of O2 = 760 x 21% = 160 mmHg.

Total pressure of dry air (e.g. in our classroom): Pdry = PN2 + PO2 + PCO2 = 760 mmHg

When air gets to our lungs, it is humid, so the calculation changes to: Pwet = PN2 + PO2 + PCO2 + PH2O= 760 mmHg

Pdry air = PN2 + PO2 + PCO2    so, PO2 (in air) = 160 mm Hg  (sea level)

Pwet air = PN2 + PO2 + PCO2 + PH2O   so, PO2 (alveoli in lungs) = 105 mm Hg

Part II: Gas exchange, gas transport, and O2 consumption

Henry’s Law and Partial Pressures in Blood: the solubility of a gas in a liquid at a particular temperature is proportional to the pressure of that gas above the liquid

PX= khC     (P is the partial pressure of the solute in the gas above the solution, c is concentration of the solute and kH is Henry’s Constant

kH is a function the solute (gas), the solvent and the temperature

The concentration of a gas dissolved in a fluid (such as plasma) depends directly on its partial pressure in the gas mixture

The amount of gas that can dissolve in liquid depends on:

Solubility of the gas in the liquid (constant)

Temperature of the fluid (more gas can dissolve in cold liquid); doesn’t change for blood

Partial pressure of the gases  this is the determining factor for blood

When water or plasma is brought into equilibrium with air at a PO2 of 100mmHg, for example, the fluid will contain 0.3 mL of O2 per 100mL of fluid at 37C

If the fluid has come into equilibrium and is fully saturated then the fluid has the same PO2 as the gas. Alveoli and blood capillaries quickly reach equilibrium for O2 and CO2. This helps maximize the amount of gas dissolved in fluid. Henry’s Law predicts this.

Note blood PO2 measurements (with an instrument) does not account for O2 attached to Hb inside cells. 

When lungs are functioning properly, PO2 of systemic arterial blood is only 1-5mm Hg less than PO2 of alveolar air

At normal PO2 of about 100mm Hg, hemoglobin is almost completely filled with O2

Oxyhemoglobin saturation of 97-100%

Fick’s Principle and Oxygen Consumption (internal respiration): (Fick’s principle tells us about oxygen balance. The rate of consumption of O2 by tissues is equal to the rate of delivery of oxygen.

Rate of O2 consumption = Ȯ = Q * (CAO2 - CVO2)       Where: Q = rate of blood flow = cardiac output

CAO2 = O2 concentration in arterial blood

CVO2 = O2 concentration in venous blood

Normal resting oxygen consumption 250 ml O2/min

CAO2 = (SaO2 * 1.34 mL O2/gHb * [Hb])+ 0.003 mL O2/dL/mmHg * PAO2

CVO2 = (SaO2 * 1.34 mL O2/gHb * [Hb])+ 0.003 mL O2/dL/mmHg * PVO2

Where PAO2 is the partial pressure of O2 in arterial blood and is usually equal to the PO2 in alveoli

PVO2 is the partial pressure of O2 in venous blood and is usually equal to tissue PO2.

Transport of gases across tissues

In humans, the lung surface is so large and so thin that O2 and CO2 transport across the alveolar wall is ~3-fold faster than necessary—at least when the person is resting at sea level. Nevertheless, this redundancy is extremely important during exercise (when cardiac output can increase markedly), for life at high altitude (where the PO2 is low), and in old age (when lung function diminishes). A substantial decrease in surface area, or thickening of the barrier, can be deleterious. Examples are the surgical removal of a lung (which reduces the total surface for gas exchange by about half) and pulmonary edema (which increases the effective thickness of the barrier). Thus, if an individual with a thickened barrier loses a lung, the remaining surface area may not be large enough to sustain adequate rates of gas exchange.

Part III: Mechanics of breathing, lung volumes, expiration and inspiration, lung pressures

Mechanisms of Breathing

Inspiration: Volume of thoracic cavity (and lungs) increases vertically when diaphragm contracts (flattens) and laterally when parasternal and external intercostals raise the ribs.

Thoracic & lung volume increase  intrapulmonary pressure decreases  air in

Expiration: Volume of thoracic cavity (and lungs) decreases vertically when diaphragm relaxes (dome) and laterally when external and parasternal intercostals relax for quiet expiration or internal intercostals contract in forced expiration to lower the ribs.

Thoracic & lung volume decrease  intrapulmonary pressure increases  air out

Spirometry: Subject breathes into and out of a device that records volume and frequency of air movement on a spirogram.

Measures lung volumes and capacities

Can diagnose restrictive and disruptive lung disorders

Lung Volume Measurements

Tidal volume: amount of air expired or inspired in quiet breathing

Expiratory reserve volume: amount of air that can be forced out after tidal volume

Inspiratory reserve volume: amount of air that can be forced in after tidal volume

Residual volume: amount of air left in lungs after maximum expiration

Lung Capacity Measurements

Vital capacity: maximum amount of air that can be forcefully exhaled after a maximum inhalation

Total lung capacity: amount of gas in the lungs after a maximum inspiration

Inspiratory capacity: amount of gas that can be inspired after a normal expiration

Functional residual capacity: amount of gas left in lungs after a normal expiration

Relationship between lung volume and capacity

Vital capacity = inspiratory reserve volume + expiratory reserve volume + tidal volume

Functional residual capacity = residual volume + expiratory reserve volume

Total minute volume = tidal volume x breaths per minute (~ 6L/min)

Part V: Pulmonary Disorders

Pulmonary disorders classifications

Obstructive Diseases

Increased resistance to flow

Causes:

Lumen: excessive secretions (COPD); obstruction due to aspiration

Airway:contraction of smooth muscle (asthma); hypertrophy of bronchial wall

Outside of airway: destruction of lung ECM/parenchyma (Emphysema)  lack of airway support

Consequences – reduced expiratory flow rate but near normal vital capacity  Diagnosed by tests, like FEV1, that measure rate of expiration

Examples: COPD, emphysema, asthma

Restrictive (Constrictive) Diseases

Decreased expansion of the lungs

Are characterized by reduced vital capacity but with normal forced expiratory flow

Decreased expansion of the lungs  reduced VC, FRC, normal resistance

Examples: Pneumothorax, pulmonary fibrosis

Gas Exchange

Impaired alveolar-RBC gas diffusion

Low or abnormal Hb

Anemia

CO or CN poisoning

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) involves chronic inflammation, narrowing of the airways, and alveolar destruction

Includes emphysema and chronic obstructive bronchiolitis

Fifth leading cause of death

Most people with COPD are (or were) smokers

Decline in FEV1

Inflammation involves macrophages, neutrophils, and cytotoxic T cells

Excessive mucus production and inflammation triggered by smoking

Emphysema is a chronic, progressive condition that destroys alveolar tissue, resulting in fewer, larger alveoli

Reduces surface area for gas exchange and ability of bronchioles to remain open during expiration  decreased diffusing capacity

Collapse of bronchiole during expiration causes air trapping, decreasing gas exchange

Commonly occurs in long-term smokers - Cigarette smoking stimulates release of inflammatory cytokines which attract macrophages and leukocytes that secrete enzymes that destroy tissue

Consequences: high airway resistance, pulmonary hypertension

Asthma - results from episodes of obstruction of air flow thru bronchioles

Caused by inflammation, mucus secretion, and bronchoconstriction

Inflammation contributes to increased airway responsiveness (i.e. muscle spasm) to agents that promote bronchial constriction

Provoked by allergic reactions that release IgE,  by exercise, by breathing cold, dry air, or by aspirin

Symptoms: dyspnea (shortness of breath) and wheezing

Pneumothorax = collapsed lung caused by air trapped outside of lung – usually on one side

Air enters the pleural space, raising intrapleural pressure so that the pressure difference keeping the lung against the chest wall is eliminated. 

The lung collapses due to its elastic recoil. 

A spontaneous pneumothorax may occur without disease or trauma, by air leaking from the lung due to puncture from a broken rib or to lung disorders such as COPD, cystic fibrosis, or rupture of a lung blister. 

Each lung is in a separate pleural compartment, so a pneumothorax usually occurs in only one lung.

Treatment: chest tube (if necessary), supportive O2

Symptoms

Decreased or absent breath sounds on the affected side

Unequal chest rise

Dyspnea (difficulty breathing)

Tachypnea (rapid breathing)

Tachycardia (rapid heart rate)

Hypotension (low blood pressure)

Hypoxia (deficiency in the amount of oxygen reaching the tissues)

Pale, cool, clammy skin

Pulmonary Fibrosis

Some people accumulate fibrous tissues in the lungs when alveoli are damaged.

May be due to inhalation of small particles

Example: black lung (anthracosis) in miners

Example: Imagine a healthy person with a functional residual capacity (FRC) of 3 L and an intrapleural pressure (PIP) of −5 cm H2O (note that 1 mmH2O is ~ 0.074 mmHg). If that person now develops pulmonary fibrosis, which increases the elastic recoil of the lungs, FRC would decrease because a PIP of −5 cm H2O would no longer be adequate to keep the resting lung volume at 3 L. 

Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS)

Production of surfactant begins late in fetal life, so premature babies may be born with a high risk for alveolar collapse called respiratory distress syndrome (RDS); treated with surfactant

A similar problem may occur in adults caused by septic shock, reduced lung compliance and reduced surfactant – acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS); is not treatable with surfactant

Disorders Caused by High Partial Pressure of Gases

Problems for deep-sea divers

Oxygen toxicity: 100% oxygen is dangerous at 2.5 atmospheres; due to oxidation of enzymes

Nitrogen narcosis: occurs if nitrogen is inhaled under pressure; results in dizziness and drowsiness

Decompression sickness: When a diver comes to the surface too fast, nitrogen bubbles can form in the blood and block small vessels.

Can also happen if an airplane suddenly loses pressure (in this case the PO2 is too low, but has a similar effect on Nitrogen as seen in divers)

Hypocapnia

Hypocapnia (low plasma PCO2) due to hyperventilation causes cerebral vasoconstriction, which results in inadequate brain perfusion and hypoxia that can produce dizziness. 

The hypocapnia of hyperventilation also raises the blood pH (produces a respiratory alkalosis), which lowers plasma Ca2+. 

This can result in neuromuscular irritability and muscle spasms (tetany) in the legs, feet, and hands. 

People can raise their plasma PCO2 and alleviate these symptoms by breathing into a paper bag, but this could be dangerous for people hyperventilating because of asthma or because they have angina and myocardial infarction.

Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

Carbon monoxide poisoning may be suspected when the blood carboxyhemoglobin is greater than 10% in smokers and 3% in nonsmokers.

Carbon monoxide poisoning is usually caused by the burning of fossil fuels by faulty appliances or vehicles within an enclosed space. 

Symptoms may include headache, weakness, dizziness, nausea, confusion, memory loss, psychiatric conditions, and cardiac injury. 

People may be treated for carbon monoxide poisoning by breathing 100% oxygen at 1 atmosphere pressure, or by breathing 100% oxygen at greater than 1.4 atmospheres pressure within a hyperbaric chamber.

Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBOT)

Patients breathe 100% oxygen at 2 to 3 atmospheres pressure; does not increase the amount of oxygen carried by hemoglobin when breathing sea level air. 

It does significantly increase the oxygen carried by plasma from 0.3 ml O2/100 ml blood at sea level to up to 6 ml O2/100 ml blood for 100% oxygen at 3 atmospheres pressure. 

This is useful in the treatment of:

decompression sickness 

carbon monoxide poisoning, 

severe traumatic injuries (such as crush injuries affecting the lungs) 

certain inflammations and diabetic sores
