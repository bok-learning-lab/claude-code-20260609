[2] If you’re on a plane at normal cruising altitude (40,000 ft) and the cabin suddenly depressurizes, you could lose consciousness in just 15-20sec (FYI, airline pilots refer to this time as the “time of useful consciousness”). Why so soon? [brief qualitative answer requested]

When you hold your breath, you prevent your lungs from getting fresh air (i.e. more O2), but your body can still use the O2 in your bloodstream and the O2 in your alveoli. If you simply held your breath the alveolar PO2 would remain high for a couple minutes. When the cabin depressurizes at 40,000 ft, PO2 falls precipitously (PO2

21%*140mmHg = 29.4 mmHg), and if you continue breathing your alveolar PO2 (PaO2 ~ 21%*(140-47)mmHg – 40mmHg/0.8 = ~0) will drop accordingly (based on the Alveolar Gas Equation). Remember that PO2 readily equilibrates across the alveolar wall so that it’s essentially the same for air in the alveoli and for blood in the pulmonary capillaries. Because of this, low PaO2 levels can readily pull O2 out of the bloodstream, so after a massive cabin depressurization your body doesn’t get to use the O2 accumulated in the lungs OR bloodstream, for the most part.
