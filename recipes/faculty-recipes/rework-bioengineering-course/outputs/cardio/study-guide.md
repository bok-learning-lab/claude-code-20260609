## Roadmap

The Cardio module is four lectures across three threads: **(I) anatomy + electrical conduction + ECG**, **(II) cardiac cycle + mechanics + Wigger's diagram**, **(III) pressure–volume dynamics + Frank-Starling + cardiac output**. The heart is the framework instructor's favorite recurring sentence: _"a biochemically powered, electrically activated, pressure- and volume-regulated, tandem mechanical pump with a mean time to failure of 2 billion cycles."_ Everything in this module is one of those four adjectives.

## Conceptual learning objectives

### I. Anatomy, electrical properties, and the ECG

1. **Describe the purpose of all four chambers, the valves, the pulmonary vein, and the aorta — and how defects in each affect flow.** The right side pumps deoxygenated blood through the pulmonary circuit; the left side pumps oxygenated blood through the systemic circuit. AV valves (tricuspid, mitral) prevent atrial backflow during ventricular systole ("lub"); semilunar valves (pulmonic, aortic) prevent arterial backflow during diastole ("dub"). Valvular insufficiency → regurgitation; stenosis → pressure overload upstream.
2. **Map the flow of blood from the LV through systemic and pulmonary circuits and describe how pressure varies along the path.** LV → aorta → arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins → RA → RV → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → LA → LV. Pressure is highest in the aorta during systole (~120 mmHg), drops steeply across arterioles (the major resistance vessels), is lowest in the venae cavae and pulmonary vein, and never gets very high on the pulmonary side (~25/8 mmHg).
3. **How do electrical signals move through the heart and why is the order important?** SA node (pacemaker, RA) → atrial myocardium → AV node (delay, ~100 ms) → bundle of His → left & right bundle branches → Purkinje fibers → ventricular myocardium from apex up. The AV delay lets the atria empty into the ventricles before ventricular contraction; the apex-up activation pattern lets ejection squeeze toward the outflow tracts. Order matters because *coordinated* contraction is what produces stroke volume.
4. **Draw an ECG and label the waves; indicate the electrical and mechanical events that produce them.**
   - **P wave** — atrial depolarization
   - **P–Q interval** — atrial systole / AV delay
   - **QRS complex** — ventricular depolarization (atrial repolarization is buried in it)
   - **S–T segment** — plateau phase / ventricular systole
   - **T wave** — ventricular repolarization
   The ECG records body-surface voltage from the depolarization wave, **not** action potentials directly and **not** mechanical contraction.
5. **Differentiate atrial vs. ventricular abnormalities on an ECG.** Abnormal P waves (missing, multiple, inverted, dissociated from QRS) → atrial origin. Abnormal QRS (wide, irregular, missing, chaotic) → ventricular origin. Rate-related (PR-interval changes with heart rate; ST changes with breathing rate via vagal tone).
6. **The SA node is the primary pacemaker; located in the wall of the right atrium near the SVC.** When it fails, AV node (~40–60 bpm) or Purkinje fibers (~20–40 bpm) can pace, but slower and unstable.
7. **What is a refractory period and why is it essential to cardiac contraction?** The long plateau of the cardiac action potential keeps the muscle in absolute refractory through most of the contraction — so cardiac muscle **cannot tetanize**. This is what makes the heart a pump and not a clamp.
8. **Arrhythmias to recognize.** Bradycardia (<60 bpm), tachycardia (>100 bpm), atrial fibrillation (chaotic baseline, irregular QRS, ~15% drop in CO, stroke risk), ventricular tachycardia (wide QRS at rapid rate, can degenerate to VF), ventricular fibrillation (no organized QRS, no output — defibrillate).

### II. Cardiac cycle and mechanics

9. **Re-create a Wigger's diagram with LA pressure, LV pressure, aortic pressure, LV volume, and the ECG aligned in time.** Phases:
   - **Atrial systole** (P wave; final 20% of ventricular filling)
   - **Isovolumetric contraction** (QRS; all four valves closed; pressure rising, volume constant)
   - **Ejection** (aortic valve opens at ~80 mmHg; volume falls from EDV to ESV)
   - **Isovolumetric relaxation** (T wave end; aortic valve closes at ~80 mmHg; mitral valve still closed; pressure falls, volume constant)
   - **Passive filling** (mitral valve opens; LV fills from LA to ~80% of EDV)
10. **Approximate values to memorize.** EDV ≈ 120–130 mL, ESV ≈ 50 mL, SV ≈ 70–80 mL, EF ≈ 60%, HR ≈ 70 bpm, CO ≈ 5 L/min, MAP ≈ 90–95 mmHg, peak LV pressure ≈ 120 mmHg, aortic diastolic ≈ 80 mmHg.

### III. Pressure–volume dynamics

11. **Recreate a PV loop from a Wigger's diagram and estimate stroke work.** The loop traces LV pressure vs. LV volume counter-clockwise: filling (bottom, mitral valve open) → isovolumetric contraction (right side, vertical up) → ejection (top, aortic valve open, going left) → isovolumetric relaxation (left side, vertical down). **Stroke work = area enclosed by the loop**, and is approximately SV × mean aortic pressure.
12. **Pre-load vs. after-load.**
   - **Pre-load** = EDV. Increased venous return → more filling → longer initial sarcomere length → more force (Frank–Starling). PV loop shifts right (wider).
   - **After-load** = arterial pressure / TPR that the LV ejects against. Increased afterload → aortic valve opens at higher pressure → less ejection → ESV rises → SV falls. PV loop shifts up and narrows.
13. **Causes of increased after-load.** Hypertension, aortic stenosis, peripheral vasoconstriction (sympathetic, vasopressors).
14. **Causes of increased pre-load.** Volume overload (IV fluids, renal failure), venous return increases (exercise, lying down), tricuspid/mitral regurgitation.
15. **Frank–Starling Mechanism.** ↑venous return → ↑filling → ↑pre-load → ↑initial sarcomere length → ↑cross-bridge cycling → ↑force of contraction → ↑SV. The intrinsic mechanism that matches CO to venous return on a beat-by-beat basis, without neural input.
16. **Biomedical interventions worth knowing.** Pacemaker (treats bradyarrhythmias / heart block — paces the ventricle when SA/AV nodes fail); ICD (terminates VF/VT); LVAD (mechanical pump in series with LV, augments CO in end-stage heart failure); valve replacement (corrects stenosis / regurgitation); ablation (kills ectopic foci in atrial fibrillation).

## Equations to know cold

1. **Cardiac output** — `CO = SV × HR` — `[mL/min] = [mL/beat] × [beats/min]`. Resting CO ≈ 5 L/min.
2. **Stroke volume** — `SV = EDV − ESV` — all in mL. ~70 mL at rest.
3. **Ejection fraction** — `EF = SV / EDV` — dimensionless (or %). Normal ≈ 60%. Below ~40% defines systolic heart failure.
4. **Pulse pressure** — `PP = SP − DP` — mmHg. ~40 mmHg normally; widens with stiff arteries or high SV.
5. **Mean arterial pressure** — `MAP ≈ DP + (1/3)·PP` ≈ `(2/3)·DP + (1/3)·SP` — mmHg. ~90–95 mmHg normally. The 1/3-vs-2/3 weighting reflects the fact that diastole is roughly twice as long as systole at resting heart rates.
6. **Total peripheral resistance** — `TPR = MAP / CO` — mmHg·min/mL. The arterial-tree analog of Ohm's law (V = IR).
7. **Stroke work** — `SW ≈ SV × mean aortic pressure` — work done by the LV per beat, equal to the area enclosed by the PV loop.

## Quantitative skills

You must be able to **calculate** all of: MAP, EF, SV, SW, CO, HR, PP, TPR — given any consistent set of inputs (pressure trace, volume trace, ECG, or some combination). You must be able to **read** EDV, ESV, EDP, ESP off a Wigger's diagram. You must be able to **draw** the corresponding PV loop and identify how each phase maps to a segment of the loop.

## Common pitfalls

- **The ECG is not the action potential.** It's the body-surface voltage produced by depolarization moving through tissue. The plateau phase of the ventricular AP shows up as the S–T *segment*, not as an explicit wave.
- **The QRS hides atrial repolarization.** Don't go looking for an "atrial T wave."
- **Aortic valve opens at LV pressure ≈ aortic diastolic pressure (~80 mmHg), not at peak LV pressure.** Drawing the PV loop incorrectly with the aortic valve opening at the top is the most common student error.
- **EF is a ratio, not a volume.** It's the *fraction* of EDV ejected — quoting it in mL is the second most common mistake.
- **Cardiac muscle cannot tetanize.** If a question seems to invite you to "increase contraction by stimulating faster," that's a skeletal-muscle answer, not a cardiac one. The long refractory period prevents it.
- **Pre-load and after-load both depend on pressure, but they're not the same axis.** Pre-load is at the *end* of diastole (volume the heart starts ejecting from); after-load is during *ejection* (pressure the heart works against).
- **Increased after-load decreases SV at constant contractility**, but the body compensates via Frank–Starling on the next beat (ESV rises → next EDV is higher → SV partially recovers). Single-beat answers and steady-state answers can differ.
