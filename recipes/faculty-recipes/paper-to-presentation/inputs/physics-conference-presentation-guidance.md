# Physics Conference Presentation Guidance

> Reference context for generating conference presentation materials from a research paper.
> Synthesized from the American Physical Society (APS) Global Physics Summit / March & April
> Meeting presenter instructions (the field's flagship venue), plus widely-cited departmental
> guides (Georgetown, Carleton, UC Merced). Apply these as the default standard for a physics
> talk unless the target venue specifies otherwise.

## How to use this document

When converting a paper into presentation materials, treat the rules below as constraints and
defaults. Hard technical specs (timing, aspect ratio, font) are non-negotiable for APS-style
venues and should be respected unless the user overrides them. The structure and content
guidance shapes *what goes on each slide* and *in what order*.

---

## 1. Format & timing (hard constraints)

- **Standard contributed oral slot: 12 minutes total = 10-minute talk + 2 minutes Q&A.**
- Session times are strictly enforced; the talk must be built to fit 10 minutes when spoken aloud.
- A reasonable slide budget for a 10-minute talk is **~10–12 content slides** (roughly one minute
  per slide), plus title and any backup slides. Do not over-pack.
- Invited talks are longer (often ~30 min), but default to the contributed-talk budget unless told otherwise.

## 2. Slide design specs (hard constraints)

- **Aspect ratio: 16:9.** Never 4:3 — it reduces impact on modern conference screens.
- **Font: sans serif, 20pt or larger.** No exceptions for body text.
- **One idea per slide.** If a slide has two ideas, split it.
- **Readability first.** Assume some of the audience views on a laptop, tablet, or phone. Data and
  charts must be legible at small size.
- **No music, film clips, or multimedia** unless directly relevant to the science (and licensed/open-source if so).

## 3. Overall structure: tell a story

The single most consistent piece of advice across every source. A talk is a narrative with a
beginning, middle, and end — not a compressed paper.

**Recommended slide arc:**

1. **Title slide** — title, authors, affiliation. (Optionally repeat the title on the next slide
   as a reminder in case it was missed during the introduction.)
2. **Motivation / the big question** — why this problem matters, scientific significance, and where
   it sits in the broader field. Hook the audience.
3. **Background** — *just enough* for the audience to understand the significance. 3–4 intro slides
   is usually sufficient. Do NOT give an exhaustive history or every equation.
4. **Approach / methods** — a high-level overview of what you did. Summarize the messy process; omit
   the dead ends, false starts, and five-month setup struggles.
5. **Results (the core of the talk)** — your findings and scientific contribution in a few key
   figures or plots. This is where most of the talk's weight should sit.
6. **Conclusions / takeaways** — what it means, and what's next.
7. **(Optional) Backup slides** — extra detail, derivations, robustness checks for Q&A.

**Key narrative principles:**
- Don't make the audience retrace your tortuous research path. Give them the clean, simple version.
- Don't launch straight into results — establish field context and method overview first.
- After a short intro to the general field, give a method overview and hint at the conclusions early.
- Check logical flow between slides; avoid abrupt jumps.

## 4. Content rules

### Results slides
- Results are the **main focus**. Most weight goes here.
- **Each results slide must have one clear point**, stated in the slide title or summarized at the
  bottom of the slide.
- Usually **one main plot or figure per slide.** Two plots only if both are needed for a single
  interpretation.
- **Label every axis with units.** Annotate graphs: what the axes are, units, key parameters.
- For comparisons, add brief point-form similarities/differences on the slide — this reinforces the
  spoken remarks and helps non-native English speakers follow.

### Equations
- **Minimize equations.** Audiences absorb only the simplest ones in a live talk.
- A complicated mess of equations belongs in an appendix / backup slide, not the main flow.
- Equations are useful mainly for theory-focused audiences.

### Background & jargon
- Provide only enough background to convey scientific significance — no extensive history.
- **Avoid specialized jargon** of your subfield or research group. This is one of the most common
  mistakes. Define terms or use plainer language.
- Know the audience: for a general physics audience, assume undergraduate-level physics knowledge
  and offer brief refreshers rather than risk losing people.

### Visuals
- Prefer pictures and clearly-labeled graphs over text.
- Watch slide density — no walls of tiny text. Push overflow detail to a backup slide or handout.
- Don't expect the audience to remember earlier slides; keep relevant info visible where needed
  (without overcrowding).

## 5. Delivery guidance (informs speaker notes)

If generating speaker notes or a script, encode these:

- **Rehearse to time.** Practice out loud; the talk must land inside 10 minutes.
- **Don't read slides verbatim.** Notes should be cues, not a full script to read.
- **Face the audience, not the screen.**
- Speak clearly and loudly enough for the back of the room; vary tone (no monotone); don't rush.
- Cut filler ("um", "uh", "ok") and distracting mannerisms.
- Show enthusiasm and confidence; you're telling a story the audience invested time to hear.
- Anticipate likely questions and prepare for them (backup slides help).
- Use humor sparingly, if at all — it can distract from a clear physics narrative.

## 6. Logistics (for a real APS-style submission)

Not part of slide content, but useful context:
- Slides must be uploaded ahead of time or at the Speaker Ready Room ≥3 hours before the session.
- Accepted formats: PowerPoint or PDF.
- Arrive ~10 minutes early; introduce yourself to the session chair.
- Bring adapters if your laptop output isn't HDMI; rooms supply an HDMI cable.

---

## Quick checklist for generated materials

- [ ] 16:9 aspect ratio
- [ ] Sans serif, ≥20pt
- [ ] One idea per slide
- [ ] ~10–12 content slides for a 10-min talk
- [ ] Clear narrative arc: motivation → background → methods → results → conclusion
- [ ] Each results slide has one explicit takeaway in the title or footer
- [ ] One main figure per results slide; all axes labeled with units
- [ ] Minimal equations (complex math → backup slides)
- [ ] No unexplained jargon
- [ ] Backup slides prepared for anticipated Q&A
- [ ] Speaker notes are cues, not a read-aloud script
