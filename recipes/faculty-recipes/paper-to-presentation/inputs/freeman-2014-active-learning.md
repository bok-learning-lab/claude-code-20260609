# Active learning increases student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics

**Authors.** Scott Freeman, Sarah L. Eddy, Miles McDonough, Michelle K. Smith, Nnadozie Okoroafor, Hannah Jordt, Mary Pat Wenderoth.

**Citation.** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 111(23): 8410–8415, 10 June 2014. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1319030111. License: CC-BY-NC-ND (the article is open access; this digest paraphrases for workshop use).

**One-line stake.** A meta-analysis of 225 studies finds that active learning raises exam scores by half a letter grade and cuts STEM failure rates by 1.5x compared with traditional lecturing.

---

## Structured abstract

**Background.** US STEM enrollment is declining and attrition is high, particularly among underrepresented students. Calls to replace lecture with "active learning" have circulated for decades, but no comprehensive meta-analysis had quantified the effect across STEM disciplines.

**Question.** Does active learning improve student performance compared with traditional lecturing in undergraduate science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) courses?

**Methods.** Meta-analysis of 225 published and unpublished comparison studies (1998–2010) from biology, chemistry, computer science, engineering, geology, mathematics, physics, psychology, and statistics. Inclusion criteria: (1) at least one section taught via active learning, (2) at least one section taught via traditional lecturing, (3) comparable student populations, (4) shared assessment of identical or formally equivalent learning objectives. Two outcomes were extracted: (a) **exam/concept-inventory scores** (158 studies), and (b) **failure rates** — defined as withdrawals plus D's and F's (67 studies).

**Findings.**

1. Average effect size on assessment scores: **+0.47 standard deviations** (95% CI: 0.37–0.57). Translated to course grade: about a **half letter grade** higher under active learning.
2. Failure rate under traditional lecturing: **33.8%**. Under active learning: **21.8%**. Odds ratio: **1.95** (i.e., students in lecture sections are 1.5x more likely to fail).
3. Effects are robust across STEM disciplines, course levels (introductory vs. upper-division), and class sizes (small ≤ 50, medium 51–110, large > 110). Effect was somewhat larger in smaller classes, but still significant in large.
4. Concept-inventory effects (0.88 SD) were larger than instructor-written-exam effects (0.41 SD) — suggesting active learning particularly improves conceptual understanding, not just rote performance.

**Interpretation.** The authors argue that the burden of proof has shifted: the consistency and magnitude of the effect mean a controlled trial comparing active learning to lecture in STEM is no longer ethically defensible as a "control" arm. The analogy they draw: this is the medical-trial equivalent of insisting on a placebo when the treatment is already established as superior.

**Limitations.** Publication bias is non-trivial but does not erase the effect; failure-rate data are sparser than exam-score data; the heterogeneity of "active learning" interventions (clicker questions, group problem-solving, flipped-classroom, peer instruction, etc.) means the meta-analysis estimates an average across a wide intervention space, not the effect of any single technique.

## Key figures (digest)

**Figure 1.** Forest plot of standardized effect sizes by discipline. All nine disciplines show positive effects. Engineering and physics show the largest effects (≈ 0.55 SD); psychology smallest but still positive (≈ 0.31 SD).

**Figure 2.** Failure rates under lecture (33.8%) vs. active learning (21.8%), stacked bar. Difference is statistically significant at p < 10⁻⁶.

**Figure 3.** Effect size by class size. Small classes: 0.61 SD. Medium: 0.48 SD. Large: 0.35 SD. All positive; the small-vs-large difference is significant but the worst-case large-class effect is still substantial.

**Figure 4.** Effect size by assessment type. Instructor-written exams: 0.41 SD. Concept inventories: 0.88 SD. Standardized tests: 0.39 SD. Strongest evidence on conceptual measures.

## Section excerpts (paraphrased)

**Methods (§Selection of studies).** "We required that the active-learning treatment and the control treatment share the same instructor or have instructors of comparable experience, taught in the same term or in carefully matched terms, and assessed students on identical or formally equivalent material."

**Discussion (§Why does active learning work).** The authors point to two converging hypotheses: (1) **constructivist** — students who actively retrieve and apply concepts during class consolidate them more deeply; (2) **regulatory** — formative feedback during class lets students detect their own misconceptions in time to correct them, before they accumulate on a high-stakes exam.

**Discussion (§Equity).** The benefits of active learning appear to be **disproportionately larger** for students from underrepresented backgrounds, suggesting active learning is a candidate intervention for closing equity gaps in STEM — though the authors flag that this point needs further dedicated study and is a major direction for follow-up work.
