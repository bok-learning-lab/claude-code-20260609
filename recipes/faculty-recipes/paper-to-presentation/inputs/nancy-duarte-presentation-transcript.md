## Walking Through the Shape

There is a beginning, a middle, and an end. The greatest communicators of all time — I went through their speeches, everything — I can overlay this shape. Even the Gettysburg Address follows it.

**The beginning.** You need to establish *what is* — here's the status quo, here's what's going on. And then you need to compare that to *what could be*. Make that gap as big as possible. There's the commonplace of the status quo, and you contrast that with the loftiness of your idea. Here's the past, here's the present — but look at our future. Here's a problem — but look at that problem removed. Here's a roadblock — let's annihilate the roadblock. Really amplify that gap.

This is like the inciting incident in a movie. Suddenly the audience has to contend with what you just put out there, and they have to ask: "Do I want to agree with this and align with it, or not?"

**The middle.** The rest of your presentation supports that gap. The middle goes back and forth — it traverses between *what is* and *what could be*, what is and what could be. You're trying to make the status quo unappealing, and draw them toward what could be with your idea adopted.

On your way to change the world, people are going to resist. They may love the world the way it is. That's why you move back and forth. It's similar to sailing. When you're sailing against the wind, you have to move your boat back and forth to capture the wind. And interestingly, if you set your sail just right, your ship will actually sail faster than the wind itself — it's a physics phenomenon. So by anticipating the way they'll resist, moving between what is and what could be, you actually draw them toward your idea quicker than if you didn't.

**The end.** After moving back and forth, the last turning point is a call to action — every presentation should have one. And at the very end, you describe the world as a new bliss. This is utopia with my idea adopted. This is the way the world is going to look when we join together and solve this big problem. Use that as your ending — in a very poetic, dramatic way.

When I was done, I realized I could use this as an analysis tool. I transcribed speeches and mapped out how much they fit the model. So I want to show you some of that today, starting with the very two people I used first.

## Martin Luther King, Jr. — "I Have a Dream"

Now let's look at Mr. King. He was an amazing visionary, a clergyman who spent his life working hard for equality. This is the shape of the "I Have a Dream" speech — starts with *what is*, moves back and forth, and ends with a very poetic new bliss, the famous part we all know.

Let me stretch it out. I've laid out the actual transcript along the shape. At the end of every line break, he took a breath and paused. He was a Southern Baptist preacher — most people there hadn't heard that cadence and rhythm before; it was really new for them.

Let me cover the lines of text with a bar and use it as an information device.

- The **blue bars** are when he used the rhetorical device of repetition — repeating the same words and phrases so people could remember and recall them.
- He used a lot of **metaphors and visual words** — a way to take complicated ideas and make them memorable. He created scenes with his words so people could envision what he was saying.
- There were a lot of **familiar songs and scriptures**.
- And he made many **political references** to the promises that had been made to the people.

At the very end of *what is* was the very first time people clapped and roared. He said: "America has given the Negro people a bad check — a check which has come back marked 'insufficient funds.'" Everyone knows what it's like to not have money in your account, so he used a familiar metaphor. But the first time they really, really screamed was when he said: "So we have come to cash this check — a check that will give us upon demand the riches of freedom and the security of justice." That's when they really clapped. It was when he compared *what is* to *what could be*.

Moving farther along, it goes back and forth at a more frenzied pace. The audience was in a frenzy, all excited. He says: "I have a dream that one day this nation will rise up and live out the meaning of its creed: 'We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal.'" He uses the promise that the country had made. Then he moves back and forth: "I have a dream that one day... I have a dream that one day... I have a dream that one day..."

At the end, it gets really interesting. There are four shades of green, plus a lot of blue — a heightened sense of repetition, and the green was a heightened sense of songs and scriptures:

1. The first batch of green: the actual scripture from the book of Isaiah.
2. The second: "My Country 'Tis of Thee" — a familiar song that was especially significant for Black people at the time, because they had changed its words as an outcry, saying the promises had not been kept.
3. The third: another stanza from "My Country 'Tis of Thee."
4. The fourth: the Negro spiritual — "Free at last, free at last, thank God Almighty, I'm free at last."

What he did was reach inside the hearts of the audience. He pulled from scripture, which was important to them. He pulled from songs they'd sung together as an outcry against this outrage. He used those as devices to connect and resonate, ending by painting a picture of this new bliss — using the very things inside of them that they already held as sacred.

## Closing

He was a great man. He had a big, big dream — and there are a lot of people here with big dreams. You have really big ideas inside of you that you need to get out.

But we encounter hardships, and it's not easy to change the world. It's a big job. His house was bombed. He was stabbed with a letter opener. Ultimately, he lost his life for what he cared about. Most of us won't be required to pay that kind of sacrifice.

It's a little like that basic story structure — life can be like that. You're all likeable people. You have a desire, you encounter roadblocks — and we stop there. We say, "I had this idea, but I'm not going to put it out there. It's been rejected." We self-sabotage our own ideas. We butt up against the roadblocks again and again, instead of choosing to let the struggle transform us — instead of choosing to have a dream and make it real.

And if I can do this, anybody can do this.

I was raised in an economically and emotionally starved environment. The first time I got to go to camp with my sister, I was abused. It wasn't the first time I was abused — just the most aggressive. My mom and dad married each other three times. That was tumultuous. And when they weren't fighting, they were helping sober up some alcoholic living with us, because they were both sober alcoholics themselves. My mom abandoned us when I was 16, and I took on the role of caretaker of my home and my siblings.

And I met a man. Fell in love. I went to a year of college. I did what every bright young girl should do — I got married when I was 18. And you know what? I knew that I was born for more than this.

Right at that point in the story of my life, I had a choice. I could let all these things push me down and let all my ideas die inside of me. I could say, "Life is too hard to change the world. It's just too tough." But I chose a different story for my life.

So I feel like there are people in this room — you've got those little baking spices and you're thinking, "It's really not that big of a deal. I can't change the whole world." But you can change *your* world. You can change your life. You can change the world that you have control of. You can change your sphere.

And I want to encourage you to do that — because the future isn't a place that we're going to go. **It's a place that you get to create.**
