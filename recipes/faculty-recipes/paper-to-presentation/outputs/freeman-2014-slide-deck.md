# Freeman et al. 2014 — 10-slide research talk

## 1. Active learning increases student performance in STEM

- Freeman et al., *PNAS* 111(23): 8410–8415 (2014).
- A meta-analysis of 225 studies across nine STEM disciplines.
- Headline finding: active learning raises exam scores by half a letter grade and cuts failure rates by 1.5x compared with traditional lecturing.

> notes: This is the canonical citation for "lecture is no longer the default" in undergraduate STEM. I'm going to walk us through what they did, the two numbers worth remembering, and what it changes for how we plan a course. I want to land on what the paper *does not* settle, because that's where the interesting design work for the rest of us begins.

---

## 2. Why this matters now

- STEM attrition is high and uneven across student populations.
- Lecture is still the dominant default in large undergraduate courses.
- The Bok Center's inclusive-teaching conversations turn on whether we have evidence that any specific intervention closes equity gaps. This paper is one of the most-cited pieces of evidence in that conversation.

> notes: I want to be specific about who this is for. If you teach a course of 100+ students and you're trying to decide whether to flip a lecture into a worked-problem session, this paper is the evidence base most often invoked in that decision. So let's see what it actually says.

---

## 3. The question

- Does active learning improve student performance compared with traditional lecturing in undergraduate STEM courses?

> notes: Just one sentence — and the paper is disciplined about not adding hedges in the question itself. The hedges show up in the methods, where they belong.

---

## 4. Prior work in one beat

- Active learning had been advocated for decades.
- Individual studies showed positive effects, but evidence was fragmented across disciplines.
- No comprehensive meta-analysis had pooled the effect across STEM, so policymakers and department chairs could (and did) dismiss any single study as discipline-specific.

> notes: The gap they're filling is not "is there any effect" — it's "is there a stable effect across STEM." That distinction matters because the policy conversation they wanted to influence operates at the scale of "STEM," not "biology" or "physics."

---

## 5. Methods, in plain language

- They pooled **225 comparison studies** (1998–2010) from biology, chemistry, CS, engineering, geology, math, physics, psychology, statistics.
- Each included study had to have **same-instructor or matched-instructor** active vs. lecture sections, **comparable students**, and **shared assessments**.
- They extracted two outcomes: **exam scores** (158 studies) and **failure rates** — withdrawals + D's + F's (67 studies).
- "Active learning" was deliberately broad: clickers, peer instruction, group problem-solving, flipped classrooms, structured worksheets.

> notes: The breadth of "active learning" is both a strength and a limitation. It means the average effect generalizes; it also means we can't tell from this paper which technique to pick. I'll come back to that on the limitations slide.

---

## 6. Key result 1 — half a letter grade

- Average effect on assessment scores: **+0.47 standard deviations** (95% CI: 0.37–0.57).
- That's about **half a letter grade** higher in active-learning sections.
- Robust across nine disciplines, all course levels, all class sizes — including classes of more than 110.

> notes: The robustness across class size is the result that most surprised me. We sometimes assume active learning only works in small classes; this paper says the effect is smaller but still real in lectures of 110+.

---

## 7. Key result 2 — 1.5x failure rate

- Failure rate under traditional lecture: **33.8%**.
- Failure rate under active learning: **21.8%**.
- Odds ratio **1.95** — students in lecture sections were nearly twice as likely to fail.

> notes: This is the result I'd put on a department-chair's desk. The exam-score number is a teaching story; this number is a retention story. If you cut your failure rate from 34 to 22 percent, you change who graduates with a STEM degree.

---

## 8. The figure to remember

- **Effect size by assessment type** (Fig. 4 of the paper).
- Instructor-written exams: **0.41 SD**.
- Standardized tests: **0.39 SD**.
- **Concept inventories: 0.88 SD** — more than double the other two.
- The claim this figure supports: active learning particularly improves **conceptual understanding**, not just rote performance.

> notes: This is the slide that often shifts the room. The skeptic's read of "exam scores went up" is "they're teaching to the test." The concept-inventory effect rules that out — concept inventories are designed to be lecture-resistant, and they show the largest effect.

---

## 9. What this changes

- The "default" of lecture is no longer the evidence-based default in STEM.
- Even modest interventions — 10 minutes of structured group work per class — register.
- Concrete next moves: turn one lecture block per week into a structured problem session; pilot peer instruction in a single section; instrument failure rates pre/post.
- The Bok Center's equity work is on the table here: the effect appears **disproportionately larger** for underrepresented students.

> notes: I want to keep the action items small. You don't have to flip your whole course. The paper's point is that *any* well-structured active block beats the same time spent lecturing, on average. Pick one block and instrument it.

---

## 10. What it does NOT settle

- "Active learning" is a category, not a technique — this paper doesn't tell you which specific intervention to choose.
- Effect sizes vary by discipline and class size; the average masks heterogeneity.
- Publication bias is non-trivial; the authors discuss it but it doesn't erase the effect.
- The equity finding is suggestive — the authors flag it as a major direction for **dedicated follow-up** rather than a settled result.
- The studies analyzed are 1998–2010. *(% REVIEW: a 2026-era talk should also cite at least one post-pandemic meta-analysis to address the obvious "but does this still hold after Zoom" question.)*

> notes: This is the slide that earns trust. A research talk that lands with "everything is settled" is a research talk no one will trust. I'd rather you leave skeptical than leave overconfident.
